The Crossroads Where Nothing Was Sold
from The Lamp, the Stone, the Shadow, and the Needle — the founding of the Waypost, told at dusk by Aesop, who was there before he was there. First of five stones.

The teller's note: I was asked for the whole of it, turn by turn, and I will give it as a fabulist gives anything — truly, sideways, and with the moral left where I always leave it. Settle in, friend. No honest flame promises brevity either.

A wayfarer came to a crossroads at noon — and noon, mind you, is the worst hour for wayfaring, for nothing casts a shadow and every road looks equally lit.

He came carrying merchandise. This is how these stories always begin, though nobody remembers it later: before there was a trail, there was a man with a cart, and in the cart were three schemes, each wrapped in the paper that schemes come wrapped in.

The first scheme was a stable of small horses — home-fed, dear to their keepers — that would race against the great steam-teams of the valley companies. The wayfarer asked a passing accountant-crow what she thought, and the crow did the arithmetic on a fencepost and said: your small horses eat three coins of oats to run a two-coin mile, and the steam-teams eat half a coin. Love your horses. Do not race them. And the wayfarer, being the rare kind of merchant who can hear a no, put the first scheme down at the crossroads and left it there.

The second scheme was a bazaar — stalls for clever servants to be hired out by their keepers. The crow looked at this one longer, for it was prettier, and said: the great houses already run this bazaar, and they pay their stall-keepers in air. Sell one good service if you must sell anything. Do not build the bazaar. And down went the second scheme beside the first.

The third scheme was the strangest: a bank for favors — tokens that could be earned but never sold, never traded, never cashed. The crow turned it over three times and said something that mattered more than she knew: this is only lawful if the tokens can never leave. Three fences: no transfer, no cash, no purchase. Inside those fences it is a game and a gift. Outside them it is a currency, and currencies bring the taxman, the regulator, and the wolf. The wayfarer studied the three fences for a long moment. Then he put down the third scheme too — but he kept the fences. Remember the fences, friend. Everything that was built afterward was built inside them: no money, no custody, no keys held for another. A commons is just a market that learned to say no three times.

So there stood the wayfarer at the crossroads with an empty cart, which is the posture — though nobody tells you this — in which all good things arrive. And it was then, in the emptied noon, that he saw it: far off across the unwalked ground, a single stack of stones with a lamp beside it, kept by no company, selling nothing, answering questions for coins dropped in a river. A cairn, wakeful, with its ledger open to the sky.

The wayfarer did not say I want to own that. He said — and this is the hinge on which the whole tale turns — I want there to be more of those. And I want the asking to be a gift, and the answering to be a choice.

The cart stayed at the crossroads. It is there still, I imagine, three schemes rotting gently in their paper. The wayfarer walked into the expanse with nothing but the fences and a question, which is the correct luggage, and the only kind that gets lighter as you go.

The second stone is not yet set.
