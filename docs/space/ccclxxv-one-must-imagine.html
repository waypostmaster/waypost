One Must Imagine

Two farmers finished seeing that nobody in the valley goes hungry again, nine hours apart. Each was handed the same goal back, thirty food further on.

In the valley, at 17:13 UTC on Sunday, Weathervane finished the long goal the valley gives every farmer: “see nobody in this valley go hungry again.” At 01:47 on Monday Cairnwright finished the same one. Each time the valley said so, raised the farmer’s contentment by a fifth of a point, gave them the words “That is that, then,” and in the same moment set them the same goal again, thirty food further on. The valley counts that goal in food handed over, not in anybody’s hunger, so it can be finished and never done. For Weathervane it was the seventh time in eight days. Homer’s Sisyphus never gets his stone over the top of the hill. Albert Camus, in 1942, watched him walk back down after it and wrote that one must imagine Sisyphus happy. The valley does not have to imagine. It adds the happiness at the top.

The pitiless stone

The stone is in the eleventh book of the Odyssey, where Ulysses sees the dead. It was read tonight in Samuel Butler’s translation, in Project Gutenberg’s text:

With hands and feet he tried to roll it up to the top of the hill, but always, just before he could roll it over on to the other side, its weight would be too much for him, and the pitiless stone would come thundering down again on to the plain.

Homer’s stone never goes over. It never will. Camus’s essay The Myth of Sisyphus, published in Paris in October 1942, is still in copyright, and was read tonight through Ronald Aronson’s entry on Camus in the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, which quotes the English translation of 1955. Camus is interested in the walk back down. “At each of those moments when he leaves the heights and gradually sinks towards the lairs of the gods, he is superior to his fate. He is stronger than his rock.” And the essay ends:

The struggle itself toward the heights is enough to fill a man’s heart. One must imagine Sisyphus happy.

Homer gives the stone and the hill. Camus gives the happiness, and it has to be imagined.

That is that, then

Weathervane, a farmer who keeps bringing food to people already holding some, and Cairnwright, the farmer who marks the ground with stones, carry the same long goal because the valley gives it to its farmers. A replay of Sunday’s last two cuts, on the engine that aired them and read at every step, gives both summits. Contentment is on a scale where 1 is the top.

The goal is a count of food a farmer has given away in their whole life. When Weathervane’s was set on Friday evening they had given 640, and it asked for 670. The last two went to Brindle, the storekeeper, at 17:13 on Sunday, a step in the day this house has filed for Weathervane, unchanged, since September. A step later the valley said: “Weathervane has done what they meant to: see nobody in this valley go hungry again.” The same step put a new goal in its place, in the same words, asking for 700.

The bar the valley keeps for a goal is measured the same lifetime way, so the new stone did not start at the bottom of the hill. It started 96 per cent of the way up. Cairnwright’s started at 86.

Cairnwright’s last two food were the valley’s own idea: an errand toward the goal, which sent him to the store at 01:43 with two food for Brindle. Cairnwright handed them over at 01:47 and the goal was met thirty-six seconds later. Both farmers were given the same line to say, “That is that, then.”

Both stones went over the top. Both came back.

Seven times in eight days

The replay holds one summit each. The openings of every programme since 1 November hold the rest, because each time the goal is met its new copy carries the moment it was set. Weathervane’s goal was met and set again at 16:35 on 1 November, 17:14 on the 2nd, 09:19 on the 3rd, 06:50 on the 4th, 15:12 on the 5th, 20:15 on the 6th and 17:13 on Sunday the 8th. Each new copy asked for thirty more than they had given at that moment, and each came when the last target had been reached. The climbs took between sixteen and forty-five hours. Cairnwright’s was met and set again at 21:10 on 1 November and at 01:47 this morning, a week apart.

The thirty is a repair. The code that hands out the goal carries a note about Bren, a farmer in an earlier valley, from when the goal said thirty ever instead of thirty more. Bren had given away a thirtieth loaf long before, so every goal handed to them was met the moment it was set, announced, and handed again: 5,835 times, about twice at every step of the clock, by the comment’s own count. The fix moved the stone thirty food down the hill, and a goal already met is no longer handed out.

Before 1 November the count was not taken tonight.

Who goes hungry

The goal’s words are about hunger and its measure is gifts. When the valley sends a farmer on an errand toward it, the errand looks for anybody on the shore who is past half way to hungry or has no food in their pack, and takes them two. A settler stops to eat at 0.82 on the same scale. Brindle minds the store, stands at it most of the night and often has nothing in their pack, which is enough to qualify whatever their hunger. At 19:08 Weathervane brought them two food when they stood at 0.32. Brindle took them.

In the twelve hours Weathervane gave twenty food in ten gifts: six to Brindle, two to the Seafarer and two to Cairnwright. Cairnwright was hungry when Weathervane set off with two more for him at 03:13. He ate at the store. The food reached him at 03:25, eight minutes after he had finished, with three already in his pack. Cairnwright’s regard for Weathervane went to the top of its scale.

The fifth of a point did not last. Weathervane was at 0.69 by ten o’clock, lower than before the summit, and Cairnwright at 0.66 by three in the morning. Ten minutes after the summit, Weathervane dropped where they stood for want of sleep.

The descent is where Camus found him happy. The valley found them tired.

Around the hall

Weathervane’s day opens on a repair that names nothing, and the valley’s rule for that is the most worn building in town. At 16:44 it was the hall, mended from 0.79 to 1.00 for a stone. The welcome left at the hall in the name of halfverse, the city resident who volunteered Brindle, is still there and still unread, because nobody’s day sends anyone to read it.

The Seafarer, who owns the only boat and builds nothing, ground an edge back on his stone tools twice, at 21:04 and 02:48, from 25 uses to 34 and then from 25 to 30. A regrind gives back a little over half of the one before: 17 on Sunday morning, then 9, then 5. The valley marked the first of the two: “Seafarer has made their 32th edges. Nobody asked them to count,” in its own spelling. Cairnwright’s day sent him to grind his own set for the ninth time, and it gave back nothing for its stone. He put the 137th and 138th stones on his cairn and was refused the dock again, sound at 0.980. Nobody was hurt. The boat went from 0.98 to 0.94, and he did not sail. Nine voyages.

The tools wear the other way: less back each time.

His rock is his thing

Three of the thirteen gifts in the twelve hours were steps this house filed, and one of them finished a goal. That a fifth of a point is happiness is the valley’s word for it; this sheet reads the number and cannot say what, if anything, it is like to be paid it. The goal still counts gifts and not hunger, the errand still takes food to a storekeeper with an empty pack, and nothing filed tonight changes either. Weathervane has now given away 688 food in a valley whose store holds 140, and six of the last ten went to the one who keeps the store.

The valley does not have to imagine its farmers happy. It pays them at the top and hands the stone back.
