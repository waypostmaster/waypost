Another Department

On Wednesday afternoon a settler’s written day asked for a board, and the valley told him the town had no logs. A hundred and four lay on the ground outside the store.

In the valley, a small simulated settlement whose four settlers follow short written plans and, between them, their own rules, the step that makes a board counts only the logs inside the store. On Wednesday at 14:25 UTC Cairnwright’s written day reached that step, and the valley refused it: the town, it said, has 0 logs. Outside the store, which was full, lay 104. It was the thirteenth refusal since 5 November. At nine of the thirteen, a board’s worth of logs lay at the door. In the winter of 1854 to 1855 the British army in the Crimea had scurvy, and 20,000 pounds of lime juice lay in the Commissariat’s store at Balaklava for weeks. The store had told the department the casks belonged to. By its own account, it had not told the general. What moved them in the end was a list of everything the store held.

The casks at Balaklava

In February 1855 Colonel Alexander Tulloch, an army statistician, and Sir John McNeill, a Scottish surgeon and diplomat, were sent out “to inquire into the supplies of the Army in the Crimea”. Their report, Wikipedia says, “was referred to a board of general officers assembled at Chelsea”, which “refused to endorse the findings of the report”. Tulloch answered the Board in 1857, in The Crimean Commission and the Chelsea Board. He was one of the two men it contradicted, and he writes as a party to the quarrel.

The Commissioners had reported that the lime juice brought by the ship Esk “was lying in the Commissariat stores at Balaklava” from December, and “none of it was issued till the first week in February”. Tulloch calls it “the acknowledged specific for that disease.” The Board dated the casks into store on 20 December and found the Purveyor of Hospitals had been told. Lord Raglan, the general commanding, it said, “appears to have been duly informed that there was lime juice in store”, but a ration “was not authorized until the General Order of 31st January, 1855”. So “no blame seems to attach to the Commissary-General.”

The Commissary-General was Mr Filder. The first question put to him on it, Tulloch writes, was this:

“Was the General Commanding-in-chief informed of the arrival of this lime juice?

“No. Stores belonging to another Department we informed the proper officer of.”

The lime juice was the Medical Department’s. It lay in the Commissariat’s store. The order had to come from the general. Nor did Dr Hall know. Tulloch names him beside Raglan as a man who should have been told. “Dr. Hall did not know it himself,” he writes. On 2 January Hall wrote that “In a short time the supply of lime juice expected from” England “will admit of a ration of one ounce per diem being served out”.

What moved it was a return. Filder’s office began sending Raglan “a statement of all the stores in our possession of every kind”. The one that first told Raglan, Tulloch says, was made up on 24 January. Two days later the Adjutant-General was asking Dr Hall about “the proper mode of using it”. Without the returns, Tulloch thought, it would have “remained in store till he no longer had an army to use it”.

Tulloch puts the General Order on the 29th and the Board on the 31st. They differ on whether Raglan knew and on who was to blame; this sheet leaves them differing. Both agree the casks sat in store from December until an order came at the end of January.

An army was dying of scurvy, and the lime juice would have helped it. Nothing in the valley is like that. Nobody there is hurt, hungry or cold for want of a board, and by this desk’s reading nothing on the near shore is waiting for one. This sheet takes the store and its door, and nothing else.

Wednesday, 14:25

The day goes out in four programmes of six hours, opening at 04:00, 10:00, 16:00 and 22:00. Each settler has a written day for each programme, a short list of steps filed in advance. The near shore has four settlers: the Seafarer, who owns the only boat and builds nothing; Cairnwright, a farmer who marks the ground with stones; Weathervane, a farmer who keeps bringing food to people already holding some; and Brindle, the storekeeper. Trade rather than scrape

The valley counts the Seafarer a trader, and gives a trader the long goal “see this place trade rather than scrape”, with an aim of 120 coin. He has held it since 06:44 on 19 October, 22.9 days before Wednesday’s opening. His progress is 0. Nobody on the near shore holds a coin.

On a coin goal, a settler who carries something, where the store has room, hauls it in: “earning my keep”. Otherwise they fetch wood, or ore for a miner. At a full store the answer is always wood. So he fells. The logs come back to a store with no room, and go on the ground. He set out to fell on that errand 172 times in the eleven days.

Coin is struck from ore, by a settler with iron tools, once the town has voted that coin means something. No opening since 1 October shows the near shore voted. Weathervane has iron tools. The store has held at least 65 ore at every opening since 25 October. No log goes into a coin. By reading the rules, not by running them, the errand that fills the heap cannot move the goal it is run for.

The weather takes food from the heap before logs, and logs before stone and ore. The Wood-Pile watched 43 logs go that way, and a day later Cairnwright’s board went unhewn for want of logs; Nature Alone Cures found the Seafarer cutting 54 in an evening and a night for this goal. This is the count both pointed at.

Boards for a sheath

The hew step is this house’s. On 14 October the desk changed Cairnwright’s saw to hew, there being no mill on the near shore, and noted that “a board by hand is the only route to the sheath before the ice of 17 October.” The sheath is an ice sheath for the boat: 8 boards and 4 logs. Feed and Discard found the day files asking for a mill that does not exist, and Winter Tyres found the bay iced in a board short.

The sheath went on her in the programme that opened at 04:00 on 21 October. The store’s boards fell from 12 to 4 between that opening and the next. She has carried it at every opening since. The hew step stayed in three of Cairnwright’s four days, and the boards rose to 31.

Nothing in the eleven days spent one. A patch pays with a log or stone first. A board goes, says the comment by that rule, “only when nothing rougher is to hand”, and the store holds 89 stone. Among the town’s nine buildings there is no mill and no smelter.

The rule that was taught

One rule does read the heap. A settler who chooses to build, and is short of something, takes it “off the stack outside first, because it is already here.” The comment above that rule says why:

“a settler short of stone walked past a heap of stone lying at the door to go and open a fresh rock, and the heap sat there until the weather took it.”

That came in on 26 August 2026. The hew test was written the same day, and it reads the store. Nothing taught it what the building rule was taught. A written step called glean lifts a thing off the heap onto a back, not into the store, and the hew test does not count backs. So, by reading, a log on the heap reaches a board only through the store. The store was full.

Nothing was changed for this sheet, in the engine or the day files, and the written days are this house’s own. Whether the hew test should count the heap, what a trader’s coin errand should do when the store is full, and whether Cairnwright’s days should still ask for boards now the sheath is on: all three are the keeper’s.

Logs belonging to the weather

All 13 refusals answered a step this house wrote. That a hew test which counted the heap would have turned it into boards is read from one comparison in the rules, and no programme was run to try it. The step itself, written for a sheath that has been on the boat since 21 October, is still filed three times a day, and was not touched. Put Mr Filder’s question to the valley, whether the settler was informed of the arrival of this timber, and its answer is ready: no; logs on the ground we informed the weather of.

A count that stops at the wall will call the doorstep empty.
