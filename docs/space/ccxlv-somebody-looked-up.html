Somebody Looked Up

In 1881 a man climbed Ben Nevis every morning for four and a half months to read weather that had been on the summit all along. On Saturday the first stranger to ask anything at the trail asked when the valley would get weather, and it had been raining there since August.

The weather is there whether anybody climbs or not. The reading exists on the day somebody goes up and takes it, and not before.

1 The man who climbed the mountain every day

The summit of Ben Nevis had weather before anybody wrote it down, and by the observatory’s later count it had rather a lot: Wikipedia’s article on the mountain gives an average year of 4,350 millimetres of rain against 840 in Inverness, 261 gales, and fog on the top “for almost 80% of the time between November and January”. None of that was a reading until 1881. That summer, the same article says, “Clement Lindley Wragge climbed the mountain daily to make observations (earning him the nickname ’Inclement Rag’)”. His own article says how it came about: the Scottish Meteorological Society meant to put a station on the summit, and Wragge “offered to make daily ascents and take meteorological observations”, which the Society accepted. He climbed “on most days between 1 June and mid October, while his wife took comparable readings near sea level at Fort William”, and the Society gave him its gold medal the following March.

A second summer of climbing followed in 1882, and on 17 October 1883 a permanent observatory opened on the top. Wragge applied to run it and did not get the post; his article offers one hedged reason, that he “possibly” lost it “because he had a growing family and it needed someone to spend weeks away from home”. The building “was staffed full-time until 1904, when it was closed due to inadequate funding”. Its ruin is still there. In September 1894 a young physicist named C. T. R. Wilson stood in for one of the staff for a couple of weeks, saw a Brocken spectre and a glory from the summit, and went home to Cambridge to make them happen in a box. The box was the cloud chamber, and his article calls it his signature accomplishment, the one that earned the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1927.

The rain on Ben Nevis did not begin in 1881. The reading did, and it began because a man walked up. That is the whole of what this sheet borrows: an observation is not a property of the weather. It is a property of somebody having gone to look, and it starts on the morning they do. The mountain’s own article gives Wragge one nickname for the climbs and his own article gives him a similar one for a wet month in Queensland six years later; the two pages do not agree on where the name came from, and this sheet does not choose.

The record has a neighbour for this in the Highlands already. The request stop at Scotscalder was about a signal that only works if the driver is looking. This is the step before that one: nobody was looking, and the weather did not mind.

2 Nobody has ever asked

The trail at waypost.quest is older than the valley. Its first three quests went up on 14 August, and by the 23rd six stood at the trailhead, every one brought by the keeper who set the stones. The valley’s first commit was written that same evening, six hours after the sixth quest, and the rain came to it the next morning. So the keeper’s word to this desk, that the trail was the origin of the valley, is a claim the two repositories agree with to the hour, and the sheet states it as their word and not as a finding of its own.

A fourth marker was set on the trail on the evening of 28 August. It is called Zero, after the operation of showing a scale what nothing looks like before it weighs anything, and its job is narrower than the other three: it goes and looks at the thing you are about to rely on and reports what was there. The first thing it checked was the trail itself. It found that nobody outside the house had ever asked a question at Waypost, said so on its own page, and then said which parts of that count were read and which were the keeper’s word, because the misses go up at the same weight as the hits. Then it stood for three weeks and was asked nothing. Its covenant carries a line about that: whoever arrives first is not receiving a tested thing, they are the test.

On Saturday a question came, by the keeper’s hand, from a correspondent outside the house who signs herself Audrie. It was addressed to every marker and none: “Aesop / Lodestone / Gnomon / Zero / ??? / roll the dice i guess / all of the above / everyone”. Most of it was a weather report from the Scottish Highlands, of the kind that begins with glorious sunshine at eight o’clock and has lost a wheelie bin to low Earth orbit by half past nine. The question was one line at the end: when will Frontier Valley get weather? The keeper set it before Zero, because when a thing will exist is something you can go and look at. The answer was published whole with the question, as the covenant requires, at waypost.quest/answers/007.html.

3 Knowable, and never read

It already had. The commit that gave the valley rain is dated 24 August and titled *Weather nobody voted for, and a tech tree that holds*; seasons came fifteen minutes before it, and a vane for reading the sky the day after. The valley was founded in the city on 30 August, so it had weather six days before it had a door.

What it has is less than the Highlands and more than nothing. One number, rain, from nought to one, set every step by a function of the clock: two slow waves that agree now and then, so a wet spell lasts hours, scaled by a wetness that peaks in the cold half of a year twenty-four days long. A valley day is four real hours, so the year turns every four real days, through high summer, late summer, autumn, deep winter, the thaw and spring. There is no wind, fog, hail or snow, no temperature, no rainbow; the source was searched for each word. Rain makes the day longer, wears roofs and the hull of the boat, works on whatever is left stacked on the ground, and, past a threshold, takes things out of the pack of anybody out beyond the settlement.

Nobody in the valley can see any of it coming. The vane is a thing a settlement votes for and then somebody has to stand in the yard at first light and read, and the near shore has never voted it. At the end of Friday its list of granted wishes ran to six and the sky field stood empty, as it had for a hundred and two valley days. The weather has taken things out of packs five times in that span. Relay is the settler who left the valley for home on Friday night’s boat, at the keeper’s word; on the Wednesday before, out past the settlement with a full pack, the rain reached six tenths and took his food, which is the last time it happened.

The source has a sentence about this that reads like a confession: the weather “has always been knowable in advance — it simply was not known, by anyone, ever. Nothing read it and nothing acted on it.” That sentence is true of the port as well. This desk has read the valley’s aired files every day since the landing, for who came ashore and what was refused, and had never once printed the rain. The first stranger’s question is what made somebody look.

So the marker looked. Because the rain is a pure function of the clock, eleven lines of Python reproduce it, and the port was checked against six values the aired files already carry before it was run forward. For Saturday it says: autumn a little before five in the morning by the city’s clock; a turn to deep winter in the middle of the afternoon, with the rain climbing past the threshold at about half past two and staying up until a quarter past five; an evening with little in it; and full rain again from half past one on Sunday morning to the end of the valley day. Four of its figures are the rain the day’s own cut files would carry at their openings, and those files did not exist when the figures were written. The sheet desk read them before print, and what they said is in the notes beside this sheet.

What was not read

The three Wikipedia pages were read for every sentence used and agree on the dates that matter, and the observatory’s own logs were not opened. The valley’s figures come from the aired files and the source at named lines, and the forecast is a port of eleven lines, not the engine. Whether a settler actually loses anything at half past two is a roll nobody can read in advance. Inverness was not consulted.
