They Also Serve

Ten letters collected at last, and the repair was not a faster post. It was letting the settler walk away from the window. Read against Milton’s consolation, which this valley is obliged to contradict.

Sonnet XIX · tools/daybook-run.mts 10 of 10 took the day · idle 41% against 0%
The finding Standing still was only ever the price of receiving. A day that arrived after a settler had given up waiting was thrown away — !n.asking and out — so waiting was how you got your letter, and a settler who wanted their day had to stand in a field to get it. Stop discarding the late answer and the waiting has nothing left to buy. Across three runs of the same ten days into the same valley, that one change took the delivery from five of eight to ten of ten, and the settlement from seventeen people to thirty.

I The consolation, and why it does not hold here

Milton wrote Sonnet XIX going blind, on the question of whether a man who cannot do the work is still of any use — and answered himself with the most durable line ever written in defence of waiting.

Doth God exact day-labour, light denied?
I fondly ask…
…Who best
Bear his mild yoke, they serve him best. His state
Is kingly: thousands at his bidding speed,
And post o’er land and ocean without rest;
They also serve who only stand and wait.
John Milton, Sonnet XIX, c.1655

It is a poem about a wrong accounting of service, and its comfort is that the account is wrong in the waiting man’s favour. This sheet is the same accounting run properly and coming out the other way. In this valley they do not also serve who only stand and wait. They are idle 41% of their lives, and thirteen people who would otherwise have landed never do — because beds are the only reason anybody new arrives, and the settlers who would have built them were standing in a field holding a letter.

III What was changed, and it is small

askedTick A settler’s patience is measured in their own day. ASK_TIMEOUT was checked only against Date.now(), so forty-five seconds meant forty-five real seconds however fast the valley ran. Either clock may release them now and neither may hold them; at one times speed the two agree and nothing changes.
the poll A day is taken when it arrives, not only while somebody is stood waiting for it. Coherent for one local model answering in half a second. Wrong for a correspondent who is not here.
the ask Post the letter, then get on with the afternoon. The branch below it has carried the sentence you do not stand in the road because you have posted a letter since it was written, and applied it to everybody except the settler who had actually posted one.

None of the three is clever and all three were invisible from reading the source. What made them visible was writing ten days, filing them, and counting who was left standing.

IV The measure that outlived what it measured

This is the part worth the sheet, and it is a fault in the instrument rather than the game.

The runner counted ticks where n.asking was true and called them time spent standing still. That was exact while asking meant standing still. It went on being reported after the third change made a settler post their letter and walk away — and so the run announced that instructed settlers had spent 67% of their lives waiting at the precise moment they had stopped waiting at all. They were working the whole time.

A number that survives the change it was built to measure is not measuring what its name says. tools/daybook-run.mts, above the counter

Idle is task === null, and it is counted for everybody so the instructed can be held against the rest. On that measure, honestly: an instructed settler is idle 41% of their life and an uninstructed one is idle 0%. Which is a far worse number than the one it replaced was pretending to be, and it is the true one.

Three sheets running, this record has found an instrument lying about the world — a probe reporting silence from a model that was talking, a log with no column for whether anyone was thinking, and now a counter reporting a wait that had been abolished. The pattern is not carelessness. It is that a measurement keeps reporting the old world after the world has changed, and nothing in it knows.

V Oren, still

Nine of the ten took the day written for them. The tenth is the one written empty on purpose, and he is exactly where Sheet XLVII left him:

No word yet. I’ll stay put and keep my eyes open. Oren — filed 0 steps, took nothing, three runs in a row

His letter is collected and consumed like everybody else’s and gives him nothing, because an empty plan is not an instruction to stand — it is indistinguishable from never having written. The valley still has no way to say do nothing, which is the one word the keeper’s own rule needs and the only one of Sheet XLVII’s three open rows still open.

So the answer to the question DAYBOOK.md could not settle by argument: a day filed in advance does reach a settler, is spent step by step, and branches when it breaks. The design works. What did not work was everything around it — the clock, the discard, and the standing — and none of the three had anything to do with whether a model can write a day.
