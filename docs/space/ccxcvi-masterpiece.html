1. Columbine cups and a steel seal

The word was read tonight in its encyclopaedia article: originally a piece of work produced by an apprentice or journeyman aspiring to become a master craftsman, made to show fitness for the guild, and their fitness to qualify for guild membership was judged partly by the masterpiece. In Nuremberg between 1531 and 1572 apprentices who wished to become master goldsmiths were required to produce columbine cups, dies for a steel seal, and gold rings set with precious stones before they could be admitted to the goldsmiths’ guild. In London the Goldsmiths’ Company required an apprentice to produce a masterpiece under their supervision at a workhouse in its hall. If the piece passed, the guild kept it. If they failed to be admitted, then they could continue to work for other goldsmiths but not as a master themselves.

The Company’s own history, read the same night, has the span of the apprenticeship: Goldsmiths’ apprentices, who usually served their masters for a term of seven to ten years, were expected to have reached certain educational standards. Seven to ten years. Then one piece. The property borrowed is the piece: the thing made once to show the trade has passed into new hands. The guild, the hall and the rings are not claimed.
