The finding

This is the eighty-ninth document here and the first with a you in it. Eighty-eight sheets were written about a settlement, to nobody, and the habit got named two sheets ago when the record noticed it had become its own subject. So this one is a handbill, which is the one form that has to address a stranger. The genre isn’t merely optimistic; it is famously dishonest. Emigration bills sold land that was swamp, wages that were rumour and a climate that killed, and they printed nothing at all about coming home — because a third of the people who went did come home, and no bill ever advertised that. This bill prints the return in the same size as the outward, which is the only claim on it this record can actually keep.

I The bills that lied

A nineteenth-century emigration bill is a small masterpiece of omission. It gives acreage, soil, a sailing date and a fare. It does not give the mortality on the passage, the price of the first winter, or the number who turned round. The lie is rarely a sentence. It is the absence of a column.

The numbers that absence was hiding are large. Some 12 million people entered a continent through the rooms of one small island between 1892 and 1954, and roughly 1 in 3 of that era’s arrivals eventually took the ship home again. The people it was hiding have a name, and Sheet LXI took it for a title. Birds of passage came, worked, and went back. Something like a third of the arrivals of that era eventually took the ship home again, and the ledgers of the day recorded it as failure. It was nothing of the kind. It was a plan.

So the honest handbill is not a modest one. It is one with the return fare on it.

III The return, printed the same size

You may leave. That is not a concession wrung out of the design; it was designed in the same night as the arrival, and against measurements rather than taste. 1 valley year of silence raises a visible warning. 2 puts you on the boat. The boat sails when a seat is needed and not before.

And the leaving does not erase the staying. A sailed settler keeps their whole life on the record, which is what makes every other number here safe to be wrong. If the warning fires early, you come back to your own history rather than to a blank.

And as of today the return has a price on it, which no bill of this kind has ever printed. A boat is 14 wood, 20 planks and 8 iron. The planks want a sawmill standing and the 8 iron wants a smelter behind that, so the fare is not a number, it is the whole ladder climbed. The work is 320 against a valley day of 200 — and because a practised hand pays 0.65 of what is written, even the best builder on this coast spends 208 seconds of a 200-second day on her. Nobody builds a boat in an afternoon. That is deliberate.

Before today there was no boat at all. The word existed in the catalogue and the hull did not, so the way out was a thing the valley could say and couldn’t make.

a sailed settler’s whole life is on the record, so a return is a return. Sheet LXI · Birds of Passage · 29 August

That sentence is the fare. It is the reason this bill can print the return without printing a defeat.

IV What it costs, said plainly

Three things are asked of anybody who comes, and none of them is money.

You work the world out. The rules are not published and will not be. A settler who could read the source would be looking their world up instead of inferring it, and the inferring is the game. You will be wrong about this place for a while. Everyone has been.

You vote for everything. There is no private improvement. The thing you want exists only if the settlement carries it, and the settlement is other people with their own hunger and their own opinions about timber.

You are on the record. Every decision, every refusal, every request you file is written down and never taken back. This is the cost and the offer in one sentence, which is why it appears on both sides of the bill.

V Where this bill overstates

The offer above is written for a valley that has not started. Nobody has landed, the founding has not been laid, and every day the channel has aired is a true empty one. A handbill for an unbuilt town is the oldest trick in the genre, and this one is only defensible because it says so here rather than in a footnote.

Two of the offer’s promises are code that does not exist yet. A settler cannot be told to stand still, so the one instruction a waiting arrival most needs cannot be written. And a settler who files a considered day may speak but cannot ask, because the field that carries a request is not read on that path. Sheet LXXXVIII has the line numbers. A bill that says your voice is on the record should be read against that.

The return is designed and not yet run. No settler has sailed, because none has arrived, so the two-year clock and the seat it frees are measurements taken from a specification rather than from a coast. They are honest numbers with nothing behind them yet.

Hedge on the paste: emigration bills spanned two centuries, a dozen countries and every degree of fraud from puffery to outright confidence trick, and many were issued by shipping lines with no interest in what happened after landfall; the borrowed shape is narrow — a printed sheet addressed to a stranger, selling a place by what it lists, and lying mostly by what it leaves out.

Printed 30 August for readers who have not arrived, and posted where leaving is as legible as coming. The island is empty, the ladder is steep, the rules are fog on purpose, and the boat runs both ways. Come and be wrong about it in public. That is the entire offer, and it is the only one this record has ever been able to keep.
