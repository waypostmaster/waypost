The Receiver

The set in the parlour never received a picture. It received instructions for one — a signal describing what the beam should do — and the tube re-enacted the scene locally, sixty times a second, from causes rather than copies. And the cabinet held one discipline as firmly as any studio held its own: a receiver does not transmit. A set that leaked back up the aerial into the programme would be a fault of the worst kind, because nobody in the studio would know which parlour was rewriting the broadcast. Sheet LXXX ended with the card up and no programme. The programme plays now.

The finding The valley is watchable television, and the signal is the day’s reasons. One query parameter turns the game page into a set: ?episode=<url> fetches an aired day — a hundred kilobytes of snapshot and filed daybooks, not a frame of video — restores it the way check-replay’s second law demands, and re-enacts the identical day in the real renderer, every settler walking the walks the transmitter’s copy walked, because the first law says the same causes make the same day. The frame loop stops obeying its own clock and obeys the broadcast’s: everyone watching minute forty sees the same axe fall, a viewer tuning in late fast-forwards to the air position in seconds, and the pause and pace buttons go inert — not disabled, outvoted, because the clock always wins and any local meddling is corrected by the next frame’s arithmetic. An on-air card stands in the corner naming what this is and when it aired, on the postmark’s own rule wearing its third uniform: a replay without its provenance is a live valley’s costume.

I Causes, not copies

What travels to the parlour is not the picture. An episode file carries the day’s opening state and the minds filed against it — the ship’s whole company, their personas, their standing rules, their blocks on the watches — and the set runs the same engine the studio ran, so the picture is not decoded but re-derived. This is the economy that makes the whole scheme fit a static host: four hours of programme in a hundred kilobytes, at any resolution the parlour’s own tube can draw, smooth at whatever frame rate the set can manage, because nothing was filmed and everything is recomputed. The producer already proved the file airs true — it computes every day twice and refuses to publish disagreement with itself — so by the time a receiver tunes in, the day it re-derives has been re-derived before, and the laws pinned in check-replay are what make a million parlours see one valley.

II The receiver does not transmit

The dangerous half of the design is invisible: the viewer runs the same code as the live game — the code that writes the record, files state, asks the model, and posts votes. Untouched, a replay would log its re-enacted day into a real run’s history, which is the duplicated-tab fork of Sheet LXXX arriving on purpose. So the first thing a tuning set does, before its first frame, is muteBus(): every write and every ask becomes a no-op — no record lines, no session snapshot, no state files, no model calls — and a page that has tuned in stays tuned in, one-way by design. The claim was then tested against the record rather than asserted: an episode watched end to end, live valley running beside it, added zero lines to the log.

The mute covered the bus and the first live broadcast found the surface the bus does not own. The wish window fetches proposals from the server directly, so the replay wore the living valley’s politics over its own — two hundred and five carried proposals from a world the aired day had never seen — and the same module’s requestVote and carryWish would, given the right click, have posted a replay’s ballots into the live wishlist: the receiver transmitting, exactly the fault the cabinet exists to prevent. busMuted() now covers the bus’s neighbours, and the finding goes in the sheet rather than the shrug because it is the night’s pattern once more: the boundary you drew is never the boundary there is, and the instrument that shows you is a live test against the real record — not the reasoning that drew the boundary.

IV What the parlour cannot do, and should not

A viewer can look anywhere — the camera, the cards, the roster, the record tab are all live, because they are views of the recomputed world and cost the broadcast nothing. What a viewer cannot do is matter: hand a settler over and the muted bus swallows it; carry a wish and nothing is written; pause, and the clock reels the picture back to the air position. This is not a limitation wearing a feature’s clothes — it is the medium. Television’s whole contract is that the audience sees one true programme and cannot edit it from the sofa; the place to matter is the studio, and the studio’s door is the one the record already keeps: the founding files, the wall, the trials. The set is for watching the valley. The valley is for the valley.

V What is thin, so it stays visible

The mast is not up. Every broadcast so far has aired from this machine to this machine: the dev server is the transmitter and the keeper is the whole audience. The public step — the built bundle and the episode files on the record’s own host, the schedule running nightly, flight plans by the end of the UTC day and the new episode by 04:00 UTC — is one deploy away and is a deliberate act of publishing, taken at the keeper’s word rather than folded silently into an engineering commit. The brain-written episode stays bracketed until one airs on the schedule, for Sheet LXXX’s stated reason. And the receiver’s own honesty has a known limit: the on-air card says what is airing, but nothing yet stops a determined viewer opening the console and poking the sim by hand — their picture forks, theirs alone, and the next tune-in discards it; the medium forgives what it cannot prevent, and the record never learns their version existed, which is precisely the right amount of never.

Hedge on the cabinet: real receivers reconstructed from a continuous analogue signal, not from causes, and interactivity’s absence was physics before it was contract; the borrowed shape is narrow — a local re-enactment from a transmitted description, a strict one-way path, and the parlour’s old truth that everyone watching the same channel is watching the same thing.

Written 30 August, the set working: an episode aired at 07:20Z, watched from the thirty-second mark and again from the eleventh minute, the same day both times, the record undisturbed — and the tenth valley running live in the next window over, unaware it has a future as programming.
