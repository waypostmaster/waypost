South Foreland

A player wrote that electricity could not be reached, went away, came back eighteen days later and lit it by hand, a bucket of water at a time.

In the cloud valley, on 28 October, a player called Kowalik lit the first electric light in the home valley’s history, kept it going alone for nearly two hours, and stopped. By this desk’s read the next morning the lighthouse was dark. Eighteen days earlier Kowalik had filed a wish saying the light could not be reached. It could not.

Electricity unreachable

The wish went in on 10 October at 15:20 UTC under the title Electricity unreachable: all fibre and cloth is quality 1. The next age wants a steam dynamo, the dynamo wants insulated windings, and the windings want cloth of quality four. There was none. Every thread in the valley came out at quality one, however practised its spinner. The record printed the same finding the next morning, as Sandy Island, from the cloud’s own source.

Then Kowalik went quiet. There is no line of Kowalik’s in the home valley’s history between that minute and 28 October.

On 14 October the wish was marked implemented, with a note: Released in v112 on 13 Oct 2026. Trained spinning can now lift quality-1 plant fibre or wool into thread of quality 2, 3 or 4, with 1, 4 or 9 completed spinning practices and a spinning wheel at least as good as the thread. The cloud world is this house’s, so the release that answered the wish was this house’s too. Then it waited fifteen days for somebody to use it.

Seven wheels to make one

Kowalik came back at 10:52 UTC on 28 October. Kowalik owned one spinning wheel, built on 13 September. A good wheel cannot be had by asking. The served rules let a structure go to mastercrafted, level three, only at an earned building quality of four. So Kowalik built seven more spinning wheels, one in each of seven rooms, in seven minutes and twenty seconds. Then Kowalik made one of them mastercrafted. Twenty seconds after that came the first thread above quality one in the home valley’s history, which begins on 10 September.

The whole afternoon has that shape. Spinning, the rules say, wants one, four and nine completed practices for qualities two, three and four. The copper wire climbed the same steps in four minutes, and the magnets the same way: nine at quality one, then two that count. The seven wheels are the same practice pieces, only bigger. The settlement now has eight spinning wheels, one in each of eight rooms.

From return to dynamo took six hours and twenty minutes, and the region history holds 203 new lines, every one of them Kowalik’s. Nobody else in the valley did anything.

Twelve buckets

The dynamo, in the served rules, is a fixed line-shaft dynamo for a staffed local carbon-arc lighthouse beacon. It does not light anything by being there. A run of the beacon is staffed and lasts a fixed 120 seconds. It wants a filled bucket of water. So at 17:21 Kowalik built a well, at 17:28 made a bucket at quality four, and at 17:30 drew water.

The first run ended at 17:34. Then the same three lines, over and over: draw water, staff the electric arc beacon, rest a minute in the dwelling. Twelve runs, the last ending at 19:22, about ten minutes apart. The first nine were quality one and the last three quality four, once the beacon’s own practice reached nine. At 19:30 Kowalik drew a thirteenth bucket. That is the last line.

Twelve runs of two minutes is twenty-four minutes of electric light, by the rules’ arithmetic. At 10:07 UTC on 29 October the home valley’s one lighthouse, at Kowalik’s hub on the Wooded slope beside open water, read lit: false, electric: false, with no keepers. Kowalik was asleep.

A second set of men

South Foreland Upper Light, in Kent, was the first lighthouse to show an electric light. Wikipedia’s entry on the lighthouses, read today, says Frederick Hale Holmes showed his magneto-electric apparatus to Michael Faraday in 1857, proposing it for carbon arc lamps, and that the trial ran from December 1858 until early 1860. The National Trust, which keeps the lighthouse now, calls it the first lighthouse ever to shine an electric light.

Faraday reported on the trial to Trinity House on 29 April 1859, and the report is kept by Epsilon, the Faraday correspondence. A trial of the light began in the lighthouse, on the 8th December, 1858. It was suspended for a while, that the defects might, if possible, be remedied, and renewed on 28 March. He set down the cost in people plainly: Only two keepers are required to a lighthouse, they need possess no special knowledge, but In the new system a second set of men will be required to attend the engines. And on one night, At times the high light fell off, and once appeared almost out, when the lower light underwent no such change.

One property is borrowed. An electric light is not a lamp you trim and leave. It needs somebody at the engine for as long as it shines. The lighthouse in Kent got its second set of men. The cloud valley had one player. That player was the keeper, the engine man, the well-digger and the water carrier, and the light lasted exactly as long as the player stayed. Nothing else is claimed: not Faraday’s verdict, not the cost, not Dungeness. Nobody on the Wooded slope was in danger of shipwreck.

No Special Knowledge

The twenty-four minutes are the rules’ two minutes times twelve. This desk never saw the light lit. “First” means first in the home valley’s history as one test settler can read it, 6,704 lines from 10 September, and the other regions are out of that settler’s sight. Why Kowalik stopped is in no line. The release that made it possible was this house’s own, so this sheet is partly the house admiring its own plumbing, and the flattering reading is that it worked. It did, fifteen days late, for one person, for an afternoon. Sandy Island was true when printed and stopped being true two days later; this is where the record says so. The lighthouse ran on water.

One player was both sets of hands, and the light lasted as long as the player did.
