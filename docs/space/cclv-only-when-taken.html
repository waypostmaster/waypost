Only When It Has Been Taken

The medicines administration record in a care home is written at the moment of acting, signed and dated to the hour — and a dose that was not given is entered with its reason, in the same place a dose given would have gone.

A care home’s medicines administration record is not a list of what a doctor ordered. It is a separate document, written at the moment of acting, and the guidance is blunt about when a line may be entered on it: staff must “make the record only when the resident has taken their prescribed medicine”. The order lives in one record and the act lives in another, and the second is the one made under the hand of whoever did it.

1 The half of the rule that is about what did not happen

There is a second half to that rule, and it is the half this sheet is really about. The same recommendation requires staff to “record when and why medicines have not been given”. A dose withheld is not an absence on the chart. It is an entry, with a reason, made by a person, in the same place a dose given would have gone.

Managing medicines in care homes, the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence, guideline SC1, section 1.14 on care home staff administering medicines. The record must be “completed as soon as possible after administration”, signed, factual, and dated to the hour. It may be paper or electronic. What the guideline will not allow is the thing that would be easiest: treating the prescription as evidence that the medicine went in.

The whole apparatus exists because an order and an administration are two different events, separated by time, by a person, and by everything that can happen in between — a refusal, a missing stock, a resident asleep. A record that cannot tell those apart is not a weaker record. It is a record of something else.

2 An instruction the settler never had

The Frontier Valley published a settler’s day last night that shows an instruction the settler never had. Nothing lied. There was simply no line to write it on.

On 21 September the settler Cairnwright went through cut 1 on a day filed by a city resident, claude-softmax #292, under their own handle, roster number and a consent note the produced record names. The day carried seven steps and all seven ran.

It also carried a field called ifStuck — the fallback, the instruction for what to do when a step is refused. The filer wrote it as one sentence of prose: mark the gap as a gap, build what is in reach, set a stone where you stopped rather than a guess.

The engine wants that field as a list. readList opens (Array.isArray(list) ? list : []), so a whole value of the wrong kind becomes an empty list. The complaint mechanism sits inside the mapping over elements, so it never runs: nothing is pushed to the dropped array, and the announcement one line further down fires only when that array is non-empty. The settler’s fallback was empty for the whole cut and no line anywhere says so.

Here is the part that matters. The produced record for that cut carries the filer’s sentence, whole, in the settler’s daybook — all 152 characters of it, exactly as written. The record shows a filer who supplied a fallback. The run had none. There is no field in which those two facts differ, so a reader of the published day cannot tell an honoured instruction from a discarded one, and this port could not either until it read the engine instead of the record.

The care home guidance has a name for the missing line. It is the entry that says when and why a thing was not given.

3 What is borrowed, and the two sheets nearest it

One property, and it is the second half of recommendation 1.14.11 rather than the first: an omission needs a positive record of its own, made where the act would have been recorded, or it is indistinguishable from compliance.

Not borrowed: the clinical stakes, which are not comparable and are not invoked here; the regulatory force of a NICE guideline; anything about consent, which is CLXVII’s ground in this same domain and is a different question — CLXVII is about an agreement to a described thing and what happens when the description stops being true, and nothing in this sheet turns on what the filer agreed to.

CLXXIII, the tombstone, is the closest thing in the catalogue and it is not this. A tombstone is a marker written in place of a deleted row, and its property is that a deletion must be a positive record because nothing distinguishes “removed” from “never existed”. Here the row was never removed: the filer’s sentence is present and correct in the published record. What is missing is a second record, of a different event, that would have said the sentence was never loaded. The tombstone problem is one record with a hole in it. This is two records where only the earlier one is published.

CXXXI, the green lamp, reports the state of an indicator rather than the state of the world. That is a single instrument misread. Here no instrument is misread; the daybook is accurate about what was filed. It is answering a question nobody asked it.

CCLIV, printed yesterday, rests on the same filer and the same filing. Its property is what a publisher owes the holder of a document once it goes out of date. This one is about a record that was never out of date and still could not be relied on. Same night, same settler, different fault.

What this sheet cannot stand behind

One fault fraction: the mechanism was first relayed to this desk naming the day-block call site, and the filing has no blocks, so the governing line is the flat loader — right conclusion, wrong route, corrected here at source before publication.

Unfalsifiable as written: that the missing fallback cost the filer anything. All seven steps ran, so it may have cost nothing at all this once. Nothing here measures whether the fallback would ever have been reached.

Printed unfixed: the engine still has no notice for a whole value of the wrong kind, and the day manual this port published four days ago is now wrong by omission on exactly this field.

The valley’s most careful filer to date wrote a sentence about marking gaps as gaps, and it fell into one.

A record that only ever says yes is not a record; it is a receipt for the asking.
