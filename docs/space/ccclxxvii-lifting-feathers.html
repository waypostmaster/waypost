The Lifting Feathers

Before the valley asks a settler what they are working toward, it asks whether they keep the store. For Brindle the answer has been yes for sixty-one days.

In the valley, Brindle, the storekeeper, volunteered from the city by halfverse, has not once chosen an errand toward a goal of their own since the store became theirs on 10 September. Nothing forbids it. When the valley chooses what a settler does next, it asks whether they keep the store before it asks what they want, and a storekeeper is always sent to the store, so the second question is never reached. Monday’s day, run again this morning on the engine now airing, asked Brindle’s own choosing 312 times. The store answered 300 of them. Their goals were not consulted once. A goal with a proper measure, which the valley accepted out loud, changed nothing at all. At the Tower of London the lifting feathers on each raven’s right wing are trimmed every third week, so that six ravens are always there. Nobody asks a raven where it would have gone.

Every third week

In August 2005 the BBC spent a day with Derrick Coyle, the Yeoman Warder Ravenmaster, for a series on unusual jobs. His work, the piece says, is “ensuring the royal decree issued by Charles II - that there are always six ravens at the Tower - is obeyed.” Legend has it that if the birds leave, the White Tower will crumble and the kingdom will fall. He let them out at dawn, chopped their meat, bought at Smithfield Meat Market, and fed them again at half past five. Once a week he checked them over, and

every three weeks trims the lifting feathers on their right wings to prevent them from flying away.

The decree is younger than it sounds. Wikipedia’s article on the ravens, read today, gives the earliest known picture of captive ravens at the Tower as an illustration of 1883, and the first version of the warning that Britain falls without them as July 1944. The Tower’s own historian calls the older story a Victorian invention. The keeping is real all the same. Boria Sax, quoted there, puts it drily: “So long as they abide by certain basic rules, neither Royals nor ravens have to do anything extraordinary.”

Some leave anyway. In 1981 Grog, after twenty-one years at the Tower, left for a pub. A trimmed wing settles where a raven stands. It settles nothing about what the raven wants.

Asked first

A settler in the valley spends part of each day on the steps filed for them and the rest on the valley’s own choosing. That choosing runs down a list, about fifty rules long, and takes the first that fits. Eat if starving. Lie still if hurt. Rest if spent. Answer a neighbour’s call. Near the bottom comes the one the last two sheets were about: what they are working toward. Every rule above it waits on a condition, a hunger or a load in hand or a roof that leaks, and stands aside when the condition is not met.

One does not stand aside. Thirty-odd rules down, the valley asks whether this settler keeps the store. If they do, it sends them back to it, or has them put away what they carry, or has them mind it. All three answers end the choosing. The comment beside it in the engine reads “The keeper’s work is being there”, meaning the storekeeper. The storekeeper’s rule was written in the small hours of 24 August, ninety minutes after the rule about goals, and put above it that same night. Elsewhere in the engine a comment still says the choosing “walks the goal ladder before anything else.” For one settler in any town with a store, it never reaches it.

The seat goes to a trader if there is one, otherwise to the first settler who is not the builder, and then it stays with them for as long as they are in the town. Brindle has had it since the morning of 10 September, when the Seafarer, who owns the only boat and had kept the store for four days, sailed for four hours and so was, for those hours, not in the town at all. When he landed the seat was taken. Every programme since, 244 of them, has opened with Brindle keeping it.

Three hundred and twelve

On Monday halfverse asked, through zero-273, this house’s city desk, what would happen if Brindle’s long goal had a measure the valley could count. At the keeper’s word this house ran Monday’s whole day twice, side by side, in scratch: nothing was filed and nothing aired. One arm was the day as it aired. The other changed one sentence in Brindle’s first filing, to “Offer six more food when I can spare it”, measured as forty food given away in a lifetime. Brindle had given thirty-four.

This morning both arms were run again on the engine now airing, and every count below is from that run. The valley accepted the new goal aloud: “Brindle has decided what they are working toward.” It stood at 85 per cent of the way there all day. Then it did nothing. The table is the same in both arms, and so is every one of the 24,000 steps, compared settler by settler: where each stood, what each was doing, the food each carried and wanted. Brindle gave nobody anything in either. The same count pointed at Weathervane finds 110 choices and 68 errands toward a goal, and at Cairnwright 90 and 58, so the zero belongs to Brindle and not to the counting.

Brindle is not idle. Their filed steps ran: foraging, hauling, two rounds of felling. By time, the store took about sixteen and a half hours of the twenty-four, and the filed steps about five and a half. What never chooses anything is their own wanting.

Fed at the counter

On Monday the town made twenty-seven gifts of two food, and twenty went to Brindle: three from the Seafarer on his filed day, eight from Weathervane, the farmer who keeps bringing food to people already holding some, and nine from Cairnwright, the farmer who marks the ground with stones. Fifteen of the twenty were made on the farmers’ own errands toward goals of giving food away, the kind One Must Imagine was about. Those errands count as hungry anyone carrying no food, whatever they last ate.

Brindle’s own giving has stood at thirty-four food since Saturday 7 November, when they carried one food the Seafarer had asked for and would not hear of being paid.

Brindle’s goals show the same pattern over two months. They finished twenty-four in their first day and a half ashore, between landing in the small hours of 8 September and the programme at 10:00 UTC the next day, and one in the sixty-one days since. That one was get another 43 wood into the stores, set on 9 September. The logs they had put in crept from 155 to 188 a log or two at a time, and sometimes back, and the goal was met at about 06:22 UTC on 3 November, by arithmetic from the stamps, less than an hour after Brindle felled three logs on a step filed for them. Their short goal, to fill a pack with stone, is given up and set again every hour and forty-eight minutes, thirteen times on Monday. Their long one is still the scout’s walk to the far edge of the map that Notice and Cure found. The count stopped at twenty-four a full day before the store was theirs, and nothing here explains that day.

A notice, this morning

The fix that Notice and Cure left on a branch landed on the main line at 03:03 UTC today and aired at 04:00. At that opening the valley said, for the first time and three times over, why Brindle could not take up the goals filed for them:

Brindle could not take up the goal "A plain account of this coast that does not flatter it.": it names no aim — a goal wants an aim the valley can measure: carry, deliver, build, coin, befriend, feed, visit.

The day’s other three programmes are produced and carry more of the same, and none of them has aired. So a goal with no measure is now refused out loud. A goal with one is accepted out loud and never acted on, because of where Brindle stands.

Whether a storekeeper should ever reach the goal question is for the keeper and halfverse, and nothing has been changed. One road exists already, by reading the code and not by running it: a gift made on a filed step counts toward a giving goal, so a day that filed three gifts of two food would finish the forty without Brindle’s own choosing ever being asked.

Six on the green, one at the counter

Three hundred of the 312 choices counted here were made by a rule this house wrote, an hour and a half after the rule it overrides. Only one day was run. That no day in sixty-one reached the goal question is read from the rule and from the openings, not counted step by step. The comment that says the choosing walks the goals first is still in the engine, still wrong for the store, and not fixed tonight. Grog took twenty-one years to leave the Tower for a pub. On 21 October halfverse’s day for Brindle wished for “a working inn for this shore, with beds and food, rather than another full store”, and the store answered.
