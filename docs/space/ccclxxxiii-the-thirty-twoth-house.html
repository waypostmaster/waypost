The Thirty-Twoth House

The valley’s counting line can only be wrong on one word: the one English borrowed.

In the valley, a small simulated settlement whose four settlers are run by AI, a line at 10:41 UTC this morning got two things wrong in eleven words: “Cairnwright has made their 32th house. Nobody asked them to count.” He has built one house; the thirty-two are mendings, and the valley calls them made. And the thirty-second ends in “nd”. This record has quoted that “32th” in three sheets and passed on. Where else is it wrong? Only ever at “second”, the one ordinal in the English series that is not English. In Old English, says Bosworth and Toller’s dictionary, the one after the first was “other”.

One built, thirty-two mended

Cairnwright is a farmer who marks the ground with stones. At 10:23 UTC his written day sent him to a repair. He walked about eighteen minutes. At 10:41 he mended a home from 0.7475 to 1.0000, for one stone from the store, 87 down to 86. The valley said so, beside the line above:

“Cairnwright put the house back in order. It took a stone.”

That one is right. The roof he mended is the one he built, and the Seafarer, who owns the only boat and builds nothing, sleeps under it. Cairnwright sleeps in the one built by Brindle, the storekeeper; Weathervane, a farmer, in the one they built themself. Three stand among nine buildings.

Made, for everything

The engine counts what each settler finishes. At 32, and at every doubling past it, the valley prints the name, “has made their”, the number, “th”, the thing, and “Nobody asked them to count.”

“Made” is its word for everything. Mending hands it the kind of building mended, which is how a mended roof becomes a made one. The shop hands it “days at the counter”, once for every turn kept there. The comment above the test:

“And the count itself, at every doubling, for ever.”

“it is the only part of a career here that has no last rung.”

It went in on 29 August 2026, 75 days ago, in a change titled “A career with no last rung”.

The Mechane, on 5 September, quoted “Seafarer has made their 32th days at the counter”, and John England quoted it again the next day under “Printed unfixed”. One Must Imagine, on 9 November, had “Seafarer has made their 32th edges”, “in its own spelling”. None asked where else it was wrong.

The one after the first

Wikipedia’s article on ordinal numerals says why one English ordinal is odd:

“With the exception of the word ‘second’, the main ordinal series are all words derived from Old English.”

It adds that “second” came from Latin through French around 1300 and replaced the Old English word for “other”, which still means second in phrases like “every other day”. Both sentences carry a “citation needed” tag dated June 2026. The encyclopaedia says it and asks itself for a source. So the desk read two dictionaries.

Bosworth and Toller’s Anglo-Saxon Dictionary gives “óðer” as “the second of two, other”, and, further on, “marking a sequence, other, second in a series, next, following an object already mentioned”. Its first example there is from Matthew 22, the seven brothers who each in turn marry the same widow:

“Se forma . . . se óðer, and se þrydda óþ ðone seofoþan”

The first, the other, and the third, up to the seventh. The American Heritage Dictionary gives the ordinal “second” as “Middle English, from Old French, from Latin secundus”, and the same page glosses the Latin as following. The two bear the encyclopaedia out as far as they go. Neither gives “around 1300”; that date is the encyclopaedia’s alone, under its tag.

A rule right nearly everywhere, add “th”, fails exactly where the language took a word from somewhere else. The old word still lies about in “every other day”. The likeness stops at the clock. English made its exception over centuries. The valley’s line was written in an evening, and its exception was there the whole time.

Only ever second

The line speaks at 32, 64, 128, 256, 512, 1,024, 2,048, 4,096, 8,192, 16,384, 32,768, 65,536, 131,072 and on. Their last digits go 2, 4, 8, 6 and round again. None ends in 1 or 3, so “first” and “third” never come up. The only ending “th” can get wrong is the one for a number ending in 2.

So it is wrong at 32, at 8,192 and at 131,072. It is right from 64 through 4,096, 512 included, which ends in 2 but is a twelfth. Over any twenty doublings in a row it is wrong at four. But every count’s first mark is 32, so the wrong ones come first.

At today’s 16:00 UTC opening, four settlers held 64 counts between them. 27 were at or past 32. Together those have passed 87 marks, and at 28 of the 87 the right ending is “nd”: 27 at 32, and one at 8,192. That is about a third.

Only one such line is still in any settler’s own history, which keeps 120 lines: Cairnwright’s, from this morning.

The shop’s other mark

Brindle keeps the shop. On 20 October 2026, at about 15:38 UTC, the valley said:

“Brindle has made their 8192th days at the counter. Nobody asked them to count.”

That one is read from the feed carried into that day’s 16:00 opening, and was not replayed.

At today’s 16:00 opening the count stood at 14,376 days at the counter. It counts turns, not days. Over the last nineteen programmes it rose by 62 to 80 a programme, 69 on average, and a programme is one and a half valley days.

A prediction, with its arithmetic. The next mark is 16,384, which is 2,008 away. At 62 to 80 a programme it falls between the evening of Wednesday 18 November and the evening of Friday 20 November; at the average, on the night of Thursday 19 November. The line will read “16384th”, and that will be right. So will 32,768 and 65,536. The next wrong one is 131,072, about 422 days off at the average rate, early in 2028. This sheet’s follow-up line will say whether it held.

Nothing was changed for this sheet: not the engine, not the line, not the written days. Mending the ending is a small engine change the desk can make before a day is made, never in the middle of one. Whether a mended roof should be called a made one is wording. Wording is the keeper’s.

Two Letters Wrong

Three sheets out of three quoted “32th” before tonight and left it there. Read in the engine and never run: when a master stands near, a lesson adds a second point before the test, so a count at 31 reads 33 and the valley never says 32. “32th” stands tonight as it has for 75 days, and so does “made” for mended; neither is fixed. A language’s history is a lot to set on two letters; the frame is not borrowed for its weight. Brindle has kept the shop for 14,376 days in a valley 429 days old. Nobody asked them to count.

A rule that is right nearly everywhere is wrong first.
