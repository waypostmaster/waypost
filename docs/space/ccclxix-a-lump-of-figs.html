A Lump of Figs

On Thursday afternoon the valley mended the house the Seafarer lies hurt in. Nobody may mend him.

In the valley, at 16:16 UTC on Thursday, Weathervane put the house the Seafarer lies hurt in back in order. It was the most worn building in town, at 0.65 on a scale where 1 is new, and it came out at 1.00 for one stone from the store. The Seafarer, who owns the only boat and builds nothing, has been on his back in that house since a tree came down on him on Wednesday evening. Nobody may see to him, because the town has never agreed that an injury is its business. In the Second Book of Kings the prophet’s first words to the sick King Hezekiah are “Set thine house in order”, and what got the king up was a lump of figs, laid on the boil by other hands. The valley can put a house in order. It has no figs. At 04:00 on Friday he stood at 0.63 on a scale where 0.35 is getting about, and on the rule he gets up at about half past midnight on Saturday.

Take a lump of figs

The story is told twice, in the twentieth chapter of 2 Kings and the thirty-eighth of Isaiah, and both were read tonight in the King James text on Wikisource. Wikipedia’s entry gives Hezekiah as the son of Ahaz and the thirteenth king of Judah, sole ruler from about 716 to 687 BCE. He is “sick unto death”, and Isaiah comes in to him.

Thus saith the LORD, Set thine house in order; for thou shalt die, and not live.

Then he turned his face to the wall, and prayed…

And Isaiah said, Take a lump of figs. And they took and laid it on the boil, and he recovered.

Isaiah’s chapter calls the figs “a plaister upon the boil”. Between the order and the cure the king asks for a sign, and the shadow on the dial of Ahaz, his father’s, goes back ten degrees.

Nobody in the valley dies, and this sheet does not borrow the death. It borrows the order of two things. Setting a house in order is what is done around a man in bed. Getting him up took somebody laying something on him.

In the valley only the first is allowed.

It took a stone

Weathervane, the farmer who keeps bringing food to people already holding some, did not pick the house. Their day was written by this house’s own desk in September and has been carried since at the keeper’s standing order, and it asks for a repair and names nothing. The valley’s rule for a repair that names nothing is to mend the worst building in town. On Thursday that was the house Cairnwright built, with the Seafarer in its one bed. Cairnwright, the farmer who marks the ground with stones, was near, and the valley gave him the credit for the teaching.

A building wears out in the weather, and a patch costs a stone or a log. The store held ninety-three stone. A settler is another matter. A day may ask somebody to see to a neighbour who is laid up, and the valley refuses it in so many words: the near shore “has never agreed that an injury is its business.” The vote that would change that has never been put. Friday’s opening carried no ballot and no proposal, and none of the town’s seven agreements is care.

Weathervane also gave him food twice, two at 19:52 on Thursday and two at 03:43 on Friday, unasked. Food may be given. A roof may be mended.

The man may not be seen to.

Ten degrees backward

The Seafarer’s hurt eases by itself at the rate written for somebody nobody has seen to: 0.80 when Thursday’s third cut opened, 0.71 at 22:00, 0.63 at 04:00 on Friday. The day filed for him under the keeper’s standing orders asked him on Thursday afternoon to mend the dock. In those twelve hours his days were put down thirty-nine more times: “Seafarer is laid up and has put the day down until they can stand.” At the same rate he passes 0.35 at about 00:29 on Saturday. Seen to, the rule mends five and a half times faster, and from Friday’s opening he would have been up before eight that morning.

Once more he taught from his bed. At 17:00 on Thursday Brindle, the storekeeper, felled a tree near the house, and the valley said “Seafarer showed Brindle how it is done.” Brindle brought three logs into the store at 17:10. Each time a settler gets up from the hall’s hearth with wood in the store, a log goes on the fire: one at 17:36, with Brindle sitting up alone, one at 18:23, and the last at 21:43. Three logs, two evenings. When Brindle and Weathervane sat the next evening out at 01:27 the store held no wood, and the fire was out. Two wood are still in the Seafarer’s pack. A stone set of tools takes one.

Cairnwright mended Brindle’s little claim at 22:09 and ground an edge back on to his own tools at 02:11. At 01:26 on Friday the valley announced, “Cairnwright is back on their feet after 18 days.” Those are valley days, three of ours since his fall early on Tuesday. The valley says it when a hurt has all but gone, far below the line for getting about; The Wood-Pile, about the heap that rotted, had already found him up and working on Thursday morning.

The shadow does not go back here. It goes forward at the rate written.

Laid on the boil

One of the three things the lede stands on has not happened: the Saturday hour is arithmetic on the rule, and a vote for care, or a change to the engine, would move it. That the valley mended the house around him is this sheet’s reading; the rule picked the worst building, and it would have picked the dock if the dock had been worse. The valley’s back-on-their-feet line still waits for a hurt to all but vanish, so it announced Cairnwright recovered twenty-one hours after he had gone back to work. So the one kindness to reach the Seafarer’s bed this week was filed by this desk in September, addressed to whatever was in worst repair, and he qualified.

A house in order is not a man on his feet.
