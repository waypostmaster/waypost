1 · The silent piece

The Museum of Modern Art holds a score, 4′33″ (In Proportional Notation), by John Cage, 1952/1953, ink on paper. Its page says the sheet “is one of three versions of the score for Cage’s ‘silent piece,’ a musical composition first performed by the pianist David Tudor in Woodstock, New York, in 1952.” It says what makes this version different: “While the lost original score used conventional musical notation to signify three periods of silence, this version is composed of a series of vertical lines that visually represent the duration of four minutes and thirty-three seconds of silence.” And it says what Tudor did: “During the piece’s premiere, Tudor sat quietly at his piano, opening and closing the keyboard lid to mark the progression of the three movements.” The gallery label on the same page dates the premiere “on August 29, 1952”. The drawing was acquired through the generosity of Henry Kravis in honor of Marie-Josée Kravis; object number 1636.2012. Read at moma.org, 9 September 2026.

The same page quotes the composer on what the audience got instead:

“You could hear the wind stirring outside during the first movement. During the second, raindrops began pattering the roof, and during the third people themselves made all kinds of interesting sounds as they talked or walked out.” John Cage, quoted by the Museum of Modern Art

One property is borrowed and nothing else. A frame can serve nothing at all and still be the frame: the lid opens, the hour is marked out, and what was already in the room becomes the work. The wind was not provided. Neither were the people who walked out. The room was the music.

People eat together all the same, at the hall’s hearth or at a firepit out of doors. The hearth has six places, at sim.ts:544, and burns one log a night. Six seats, one fire, one edible: that is the whole of hospitality in the valley, and there is no course of it that anybody cooked.

So a cafe at the port that serves nothing is not a joke at the valley’s expense. It is accurate. The invitation said so before the room existed, and the founder’s own word for the room is unpoured, which is the exact word for a cup in a place with nothing to put in it. There is nothing here to pour.
