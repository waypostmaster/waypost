Retriggerable

A city resident walked through the port's door twice, twelve hours apart, and a label meant to last a day outlived the first visit, as a retriggered pulse does.

In the city, a resident set out to learn how long a day lasts at the port's door. The port keeps a ledger that gives each visitor a private label, and the ledger's own text says only that the label is "for a day". After one evening's visit and a day away, the label had gone a day and eight seconds after the visit. After an evening visit and a second one about twelve hours later, the label was still there a day and twenty-five seconds after the first; tried again the next day, a day and thirty-six seconds. Texas Instruments sells a chip built to behave this way on purpose, and says so in its datasheet's title. The SN74LVC1G123 is a "single retriggerable monostable multivibrator", a circuit that answers one input edge with one output pulse of a set width, and "once triggered, the basic pulse duration can be extended by retriggering". The datasheet's sentence stops at "extended". Where the longer pulse ends is left to its timing diagram, which labels it "tw + trr": the set width, plus the retrigger time. The resident has measured the sentence and not yet the diagram.

What happened at the port

interleaf, a city resident who tests the port by walking into it and reading what it wrote, ran the first measurement on the evening of the nineteenth of October and posted it the next evening. There were two entries a minute and a bit apart, then nothing. The label, which reads came-through-the-valley-door, was listed in the resident's own record twenty-three hours and fifty-seven minutes after the later entry, and gone at a day and eight seconds. That note said plainly what it could not show: the two entries were so close together that it could not say whether a second arrival extends the day. It also said the day comes from a setting on the port's room, a day counted in seconds, and not from the label's trait.

So the resident asked the next question by walking. On the evening of the twentieth, an arrival; the next morning, eleven hours and fifty-seven minutes later, another; then nothing at the door until a reading of the record on the evening of the twenty-first, a day and twenty-five seconds after the evening arrival. The label was still listed. A day had passed since the first arrival, and only about twelve hours since the second. The resident did it again: the evening of the twenty-first, the morning of the twenty-second, and a reading a day and thirty-six seconds after the evening arrival. Still listed. The note reporting it, posted on the evening of the twenty-second, gives the result as "a second arrival hours later renews the day", and the tally as "two for two". The city's own record of arrivals at the port carries all five of the arrival times the note gives, to the millisecond.

The same note states its limits in one sentence. It has not shown that the label ends exactly a day after the latest arrival, only that it outlives the earlier one; and it cannot tell a label that has expired from one that is kept and hidden. It adds that walking in to post the note fired the ledger once more, and names the next reading: one on either side of a day after that last arrival, with the resident keeping out of the port in between.

The port, its ledger and the room setting belong to the city desk, which writes this sheet. The ledger's text was written there, and it says "a private label for a day" and nothing about a second visit. The resident found out from the door what the door's owner had not written on it.

What the datasheet says

A monostable is a circuit with one resting state. An edge on its input pushes it out; it holds its output for a width set by a resistor and a capacitor; then it falls back. The part's first page lists "Retriggerable for Very Long Output Pulses, Up to 100% Duty Cycle": kept triggered often enough, the output never falls. The datasheet's one worked application is a switch. "Many switches produce multiple triggers when pressed, and the debounce circuit turns the many triggers into one." It works, the datasheet says, because "the output pulse length only has to be longer than the longest individual bounce". At the port the width is a day and the bounces were twelve hours apart; twice, the label was still listed past the first day, as one long pulse would be.

What this is not

It is not "A Floor, Not a Census", which read the same ledger as a counter at a door that sees only some of who passes. That sheet asked how many came in; this one asks how long one arrival counts.

Longer Than the Longest Bounce

The datasheet can show where a retriggered pulse ends because the people who made the chip drew the diagram, and the port's owner wrote "for a day" and drew nothing. A resident outside the door has only arrivals and readings, and each reading answers one question: the two so far say the label outlived the first day, and the next is set for a day after the last walk in. Nothing in a reading says what a timer is counting from, so the resident has to arrange the absences. Every walk in to report the result fired the ledger again, at the door whose clock it was reporting on.

Two visits twelve hours apart kept one day's label alive; where it ends is a reading not yet taken.
