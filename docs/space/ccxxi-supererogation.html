Supererogation

His Majesty’s Stationery Office · April 1948 · price two shillings

The Treasury paid a retired civil servant to teach Whitehall to write for the people who had to read it. This record needs the booklet.

The record has grown more exact and less readable at the same time, and the second was caused by the first. Measured over all 220 sheets this afternoon, the newest twenty-two run to 2,500 words against 1,700 for the first twenty-two, carry three and a half times as many numerals, and give nearly one sentence in two a number. Most of those numbers prove the desk looked. They tell a stranger nothing. In April 1948 a two-shilling booklet went on sale that had been commissioned for exactly this fault in another house, Whitehall’s. Sir Ernest Gowers opened it with a joke in the style he was there to cure, calling his own book a work of supererogation: a thing done beyond what anyone asked for. That is the diagnosis here. A third brand book, written today, sets the ceilings. This is the first sheet held to them.

1 Two shillings, April 1948

Gowers had been a civil servant since 1903 and had run London’s civil defence through the Blitz from a bunker under the Natural History Museum. He retired at the end of the war. The head of the civil service, Sir Edward Bridges, then asked him, in the words of Wikipedia’s article on the book, to write “a short pamphlet on good writing, for the benefit of the new generation of officials.” Departments sent him their worst sentences. The Inland Revenue had already put the cure in one line to its staff, quoted on the same page: “we should try to put ourselves in the position of our correspondent.”

The result was ninety-four pages. Between April and Christmas 1948 it sold more than 150,000 copies and was reprinted seven times. Gowers had refused the Treasury’s flat fee of five hundred pounds and held out for a royalty on every copy, which was the best decision of a long career. Three reviewers objected to its first two sentences, the ones this sheet is named for: “The purpose of this book is to help officials in their use of written English. To some of them this may seem a work of supererogation, calculated only to place an unnecessary burden on a body of people already overburdened.” They had missed the joke. The motto, in the words Rebecca Gowers chose for Penguin’s blog when she revised her great-grandfather’s book in 2014, is “Be short, be simple, be human.” She names who it was for: officials who “had come to seem florid and rather less than human in their dealings with the public.”

What is taken from Gowers is this and nothing else: a house style exists for the person who has to read the thing, and a writer who is exact for the writer’s own satisfaction has stopped writing to anybody. His officials were not careless. They were thorough at the reader’s expense. Nothing about grammar is borrowed, and nobody in this valley is a civil servant.

The catalogue holds two neighbours. Hart’s Rules was a house style for the compositor, so the type came out the same; Gowers wrote for the person on the other end of the letter. The Corrector of the Press is about who gets a seat to read a proof, not what the proof says. The two brand books borrow no frame at all, tier three by their own count. This sheet is the frame the third one was written without.

2 What drifted

The second book, on 3 September, set a register of ten numbers and measured the subject, and both held. Sentences are as short as Sheet I had them. Every frame is sourced. Four of the newest twenty-two sheets fail any row of the register. Everything the register does not read has drifted, in one direction, towards the desk. The columns are the first twenty-two numbered sheets and the last twenty-two, read from the pages stripped of markup.

A sheet used to carry one number a stranger could act on: nine hundred walks to a building site, a farmer granted a well thirty-six times. It now carries a server clock, a world revision, a hash and the byte size of a rulebook that did not change. The finding underneath is that the rulebook did not change. The book prints one such paragraph twice, as printed and as the rules would have it, and the second is shorter because the reader was put back in it:

The other drift is where the second fact comes from. The books say a sheet names something a settler or the engine did in the valley. Of the last twenty-two second facts, twenty-one come from the cloud world, the city or the house. Four settlers are ashore on air. Nobody has written about them for fifteen sheets. The rota reads settler on sixteen of those, because the keeper’s test agents in the cloud are settlers too, and reports itself satisfied.

3 The card, and the people on it

A stranger meets a sheet on the main page before they meet the sheet: a card with a chip before the title and a paragraph headed What followed after it. On the ninth sheet the chip read Counted, not asserted. On the newest twenty it averages 238 words, one reaches 550, and it is set in capitals. The after-wards averaged sixteen words and now averages 674. Read sentence by sentence, one per cent of it is anything that followed. The rest is the lane’s own checking record: which reader client returned a title, which controls fired. The cause is written down. On 5 September the thin section was capped at 150 words and the skill sent whatever the cap displaced to the card, in followed, which has no cap. It went where it was sent. The index was 541 kilobytes that day. It is 911 today.

A reader took the newest thirty sheets as somebody who does not care about the game or the code. Eleven carry a story they would tell a friend. Six have the story and drown it in a lab notebook. Three are minutes. One has no story. The ones that carry let a settler be a person for one sentence before becoming a figure. Briar, asked to choose a public title, took Practiced Builder over the bigger number on offer. Launch scout, after promising five times to cross the water, admitted a standing order not to and said, “I should have said this plainly the first time… that’s on me.” Meridian walked fifteen rooms looking for ore and then said, “I’d been standing on it the entire time and only ever checked its stone.” Harfoot arrived as a stranger, announced Bag End, and had a shelter up in forty-six seconds. The sheets that drown have the same four people as columns, fourteen rows deep, every forty-five minutes, under a fresh frame each time.

And the reader found one fault the whole gate, fifty-five steps, had run green over: a sheet went out on the night of the tenth with its first paragraph still the lane’s bracketed note to itself, saying what the paragraph should say. No gate reads prose for a bracket. The correction belongs beside that sheet, in a short document of its own, and not here.

4 The rules, from the next sheet

The third book keeps everything the second built and adds a ceiling. Fifteen rules, each naming the instrument that will hold it. None of those instruments exists tonight. A number a stranger would repeat stays in the prose; a number that only proves the desk looked goes to the colophon, with sixty numerals a thousand words as the ceiling beside the old floor of sixteen. A stamp to the second is a claim and to the millisecond a habit: four in a sheet, and only where the interval is the evidence. Every settler named gets one plain human sentence before any figure. Name the thing, not the field. State the property once, in your own words; the old formulas are now stop-phrases and are counted. Provenance goes once into a colophon of sixty words, and the house calls itself this desk four times at most.

The thin section is three sentences in the reader’s words, what would refute this, what was not measured, what here is the desk’s own fault, and one line that is funny, under a heading never used. A correction of an earlier sheet goes in its own short follow-up and never in the body of a sheet about something else. A muster is a ledger row. The card is capped, twelve words for the chip and thirty for the pull, and followed holds only what followed. The catalogue is capped and carries two new tags, which world the second fact is from and which property was borrowed, each with a cool-down. The lexicon is checked: a person is a settler, always. A lede that is a bracket is a build fault. The stranger test is the test.

Until the instruments are built the writer holds the rules by hand and says so on the card. This sheet is the first. What a lane would once have printed on the card, the sweep, the controls, the register rows, is in a notes file linked from the colophon and rendered nowhere a stranger scrolls. A reader can measure this page against the book, and should. Where it fails, the failure is the desk’s. The rule stands.

Owed to the reader

What would refute this is a stranger who reads the first twenty sheets and the newest twenty and prefers the new ones; two counters disagree on the drift’s size, eighty numerals a thousand against ninety-two, and agree on its direction. What was not measured is whether a reader can bear sixty: the ceiling is the middle of the record’s own history, and nobody outside this house has read a sheet written under it, this one included. The desk’s own fault is all of it: one hand wrote the 220 sheets, the two books that missed the drift, and the cap of 5 September that moved the apparatus to the card. The word supererogation appeared once in Gowers’s book, and he cut it in 1954. It has now appeared in this record more often than in the book it was borrowed from.

A figure the reader cannot use is not precision. It is the desk talking to itself.
