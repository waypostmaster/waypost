Butter Mountain

Europe once kept 1,300,000 tonnes of butter in cold stores because a rule bought it and no rule let it go. The valley’s depot is full of wood, and the food is on the ground outside in the rain.

The near shore’s stores hold 180 places, and when today’s programme opened 178 of them were taken: 100 wood, 24 stone, 24 ore, 18 food and 12 planks. Outside the depot door, on the ground, lay 149 stone. Over the day the settlers put 176 food on that stack and the weather took 149 of it, one berry at a time, in a line the valley has written 14,592 times: the depot is full. Eight wood left the store all day. The butter mountain lends one property, read tonight in the European Court of Auditors’ own report: a store fills with what the rule takes in, and empties only when a rule lets something out.

1. A store that only took in

The encyclopaedia article says what the mountain was: a supply surplus of butter produced in the European Union because of government interventionism that began in the 1970s. Governments subsidised milk production by a guaranteed minimum intervention price for dairy products, so whatever the market would not take, the state bought and stored. The Court of Auditors, in Special Report 8/2000, gives the height and the way down: Public intervention stocks decreased from a high of 1300000 tonnes at the end of 1986, to 3000 tonnes at the end of 1998. The reason was not that anybody ate it. This has been mainly due to the introduction of milk quotas and less attractive rules and prices for public storage. The rule that filled the store had to change before the store could empty.

Only that is borrowed: a store governed by its intake. The subsidies, the quotas and the cheap butter sold off by the kilo are not claimed.

3. The way of things left out

The ground is not free storage. The engine’s comment on its own rule says why: they are still there, they are just in the weather. A roof is not a rule about where things are allowed to be. It is a roof. A stack outside loses a unit at a rolled interval that grows with the heap and the rain (outsideStep in src/sim.ts), and it loses them in a fixed order, OUTSIDE_ORDER: food first, then furniture, planks, charcoal, wood, blocks, and stone, ore and iron last, because a heap of rock does not much care.

So the stack is a filter. Food goes first. Settlers put 176 food on it today; 149 went into the mud, 9 were taken back, 18 were still there at the close. Stone went on it too, 21 units, and 27 stone rotted, which the order allows only when there is no food left to take. In all, 179 units of the valley’s day were lost at the door of a store with 92 wood in it. Every one of those berries was gathered by somebody. Printed unfixed: the store is governed by its intake and nothing in the valley lets wood out faster than wood comes in, and no granary or rack can be raised by the people who live here. Whether a builder could be seated was not asked tonight.
