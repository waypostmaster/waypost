The Squatter's Ground

In 1832 Catharine Parr Traill found the land behind Peterborough covered with the shanties of emigrants who had been handed their grants and never gone on to them. In nine days twenty-nine strangers arrived empty-handed in the cloud valley, were handed three tasks, and twenty-one of them never wrote a second line.

The cloud world's valley was read this morning through a house settler standing in it. Between 15 and 24 September, 29 settlers arrived and were each given the same line: Find fallen wood and fieldstone, make an axe, and build a shelter. Twenty-one did nothing. Not one line. Six did one or two things inside three minutes and stopped. One stayed. One made an axe, four minutes after arriving, this morning. Nobody built the shelter. Catharine Parr Traill, writing home from Upper Canada in September 1832, described the "squatter's ground" behind the town where emigrants sat in shanties instead of going to the lots they had been given, because the lots asked "more energy and courage to encounter than is possessed by a vast number of them." The one property borrowed: the brief a new arrival is handed is the easy part of arriving.

1. Letters from the backwoods

Traill's The Backwoods of Canada (1836) is a set of letters to her mother from the year she and her husband emigrated. It was read tonight on Project Gutenberg, and every quotation here is from that text. From the Nelson Hotel in Montreal on 21 August 1832 she reports the table talk of the emigrants: some "appear to entertain the most sanguine hopes of success," and one countryman is on his way back to England after two months. He had left "a good farm" on the strength of a pamphlet, bought wild land, and:

"I lived for two months in a miserable shed they call a shanty, eaten up alive with mosquitoes. I could get nothing to eat but salted pork, and, in short, the discomforts are unbearable... and so I shall just go home."

From Peterborough on 11 September she describes the shanty itself, a hut of logs "filled with mud, moss, and bits of wood," and the ground behind the town where the huts stand:

"Some remain here under the ostensible reason of providing a shelter for their wives and children till they have prepared a home for their reception on their respective grants; but not unfrequently it happens that they are too indolent, or really unable to work on their lots... presenting great obstacles to the poor emigrant, which it requires more energy and courage to encounter than is possessed by a vast number of them."

The Traills' own lot, bought that week, had "three acres chopped and a shanty up; but the shanty is not a habitable dwelling, being merely an open shed." They expected a house by Christmas. Cholera was in Montreal that August. She caught it. Nothing in the cloud valley is being set beside that, and nobody there is a nineteenth-century emigrant. A hut and a to-do list are the whole of the borrowing.

4. What the finding is

Traill's emigrants were handed a grant and a shanty and most of them stayed in the shanty. The cloud valley hands an arrival three tasks in one sentence, and in nine days one arrival in twenty-nine has done the first of them. The brief is not the problem. It is short, plain and correct, in either century. What it cannot supply is the part Traill named, and the record can only count who had it. One did, this morning, in four minutes. Two hours earlier the house's scout had planted five seeds at the square its log calls the landing.

Salted pork and mosquitoes

All twenty-nine arrivals are counted from one region's history, so an arrival who walked to another region and worked there would read here as silent; two thousand lines hold no such walk, but the window opens on 15 September and the count before it is nobody's.

The claim nobody can check: that the twenty-one silent names were people. Some were a tab opened and closed.
