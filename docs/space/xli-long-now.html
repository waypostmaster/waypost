The Clock of the Long Now

On a road that was voted into existence and never built, a slate of predictions that came back, and a settler who asked for a way to keep time without fire and was filed under the furnace — read through Stewart Brand, who wrote down why the slow layers are where anything holds.

1999 · run mtdotjm9-e4, tick 12,240 41 sheets · 3 predictions due · 3 confirmed
The finding The roads boon granted every settler in a settlement twenty-two per cent walking speed, everywhere, from the moment the meeting rose — and built nothing. A road you can walk on in the middle of a bog because a committee agreed to one. The track layer to draw a real one had been in the project the whole time: footfall counted, held, reported and rendered. It worked, and it only ever made doorsteps — forty-eight tiles in sixteen thousand ticks, every one within a few paces of a store, because a route spreads its footfall across twenty tiles and each gets a twentieth of the wear. What was missing was not the drawing. It was the shovel.

I Pace layers

Brand’s clock was designed to tick for ten thousand years, and the book he wrote around it is mostly about something else: that a working order has layers, that they move at different speeds, and that the fast ones get all the attention while the slow ones do all the holding. Fashion proposes; infrastructure disposes.

Fast learns, slow remembers. Fast proposes, slow disposes. Fast is discontinuous, slow is continuous. Fast and small instructs slow and big by accrued innovation and by occasional revolution. Stewart Brand, The Clock of the Long Now, 1999

This valley has the same section through it, and it can be measured rather than asserted:

The fault was that the boon wrote on the governance layer and claimed to have built on the infrastructure one. A vote is fast — one meeting, one afternoon — and it was producing a permanent change to how the whole settlement moved, with nothing standing to show for it. That is the single thing this project says it does not have.

II The road, built

So the vote now grants the right to lay one, and the road is an errand. mostTrodden picks the tile near home that the most feet have already crossed and nobody has laid — which is the catalogue entry’s own words made literal, made paths on the routes people already walk. One shift, by one person, lays one stretch. A route is a dozen shifts. Nobody can lay a road across the map, because nobody has walked one.

And the twenty-two per cent is paid on the ground now rather than by the ballot — wornAt(tile) >= 1, to anybody, on a path worn by feet alone exactly as much as on a made one. A beaten path is a beaten path. A settlement that has never held a meeting about roads still gets the benefit of the ground outside its own door, which it earned by walking on it.

One number wanted measuring rather than guessing: a laid stretch that nobody walks on stops being a road after forty-three game days. Laid at exactly the threshold instead, it dropped below on the next regrass sweep and stopped being a road within the hour — eight per cent of every settler’s life spent paving, for eleven tiles.

IV Perrin asked for a clock

A mechanical clock with no need for ore or fire to keep time. I’d give my left arm for a clock that doesn’t need a furnace to run, so we don’t have to wait for the sun to tell us. wish-623 · Perrin, forester · tick 10,617 · filed as smelter

A settler asked for a way to keep time that does not need a furnace, and the catalogue filed it under the furnace. If it carries, the settlement is granted a smelter — and the landing has one standing, so it would be granted a thing it already has, which is the wage fault this catalogue has been audited against since Sheet II.

What makes it worth a section is that both audits pass it. check-lexicon looks for a stored kind that disagrees with a fresh reading, and a fresh reading says smelter too, so there is no misfile. check-record looks for proposals with no entry at all, and this one has an entry, so nothing is unheard. Tonight both ran clean: 199 proposals, 0 open misfiles, 0 unheard, 2,252 negation pairs correct, no dead ends in the chain.

So there is a third category neither of them covers, and this is the first specimen: heard as the thing it explicitly said it was not. Not a wish the catalogue cannot hear, and not one it files inconsistently — one it files confidently, into the bin the settler named in order to rule it out.

Not fixed here, and deliberately. A word for a clock is a real entry with a real grant behind it, and the valley has no way to keep time but the sun. Filing it is the easy half; deciding what a settlement gets when it carries is the sheet after this one.

The last row is the one still open and it is not magic: it is the store shrinking around goods already inside it. `placesIn` scales what a building holds by its condition, so a settlement too poor to repair its only shed watches the ceiling fall below what it is already holding, and every haul after that tips its load on the grass. It is the likeliest reason Sula’s claim has not moved in eleven thousand ticks, and it is the oldest unfixed thing on this coast.

Which is the pace-layer argument turned on the project itself. The fast layer here is the catalogue — a word, an afternoon, forty-eight entries and counting. The slow layer is the weather, and the weather has been quietly winning against one settlement for a season while every sheet since has been about something else.
