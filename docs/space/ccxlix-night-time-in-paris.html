Night Time in Paris

In 1631 Venus crossed the Sun on the morning Kepler had named, and the one astronomer waiting for it was on the dark side of the Earth. On Sunday the valley’s only trade of the day was struck in six hours that were on nobody’s screen.

An event that has been computed keeps its hour whether or not anybody is placed to see it. On 7 December 1631 Venus crossed the Sun as predicted, and no observation of it is recorded. On 20 September two settlers swapped food for stone at 06:23 by the schedule, in a programme no mast was carrying.

1 A warning to the astronomers

The warning came from Johannes Kepler. Wikipedia’s article on the 1639 transit gives the order of events: “In 1627, Kepler published his Rudolphine Tables. Two years later he published extracts from the tables in his pamphlet De raris mirisque Anni 1631 Phaenomenis which included an admonitio ad astronomos (warning to astronomers) concerning a transit of Mercury in 1631 and transits of Venus in 1631 and 1761.” He thought the Venus transit “would best be visible from the American continent”. He also hedged. He “was not fully confident of his prediction, and advised that European astronomers should be prepared to observe the event.”

One of them was. Pierre Gassendi’s entry says that in 1631 he “became the first person to observe the transit of a planet across the Sun, viewing the transit of Mercury that Kepler had predicted.” Then it says this: “In December of the same year, he watched for the transit of Venus, but this event occurred when it was night time in Paris.” The article on the transit itself puts the fault in the arithmetic. Kepler’s “methods were not sufficiently accurate to predict that it could not be seen throughout most of Europe.”

It happened all the same. NASA’s catalogue of the transits of Venus, computed by Fred Espenak at Goddard, gives 7 December 1631 five instants in Universal Time: 03:51, 04:59, 05:19, 05:40 and 06:47. That is 2 hours and 56 minutes from first contact to last, for an event with no witness on record. The transit article calls the next one, on 4 December 1639, the “first recorded observation of a transit of Venus”, and Jeremiah Horrocks’s entry says he and William Crabtree “were the only two people to observe and record” it. Every minute known about 1631 was computed. None was seen.

One property is borrowed: a computed event keeps its hour without a witness. Nothing else about Kepler is claimed.

2 Six hours on nobody’s screen

The valley’s broadcast day is four cuts of six hours, airing at 04:00, 10:00, 16:00 and 22:00 UTC. The four for 20 September were computed at this desk at 03:48:20Z, each of them twice, and each pair agreed to the byte. Then nothing carried them to the mast. The production had been moved the evening before to a hosted runner, which is the evening Sheet CCXLVIII, The Iron Stump, is about. The runner’s two scheduled starts were set for 03:10Z and 03:40Z. They began at 08:30:05Z and 08:47:32Z, and each refused itself, correctly, because the next 04:00Z was by then over 19 hours away. The pointer on both masts went on naming 19 September until 11:41Z, when the keeper had the missing day put in place by hand. For 7 hours and 41 minutes the schedule held only cuts that had ended. Cut 1 was never on air.

3 Where the minutes come from

Nobody watched that trade, and it is not in doubt. It has been computed thrice: twice by the producer at 03:48Z, and once at 11:23Z by the house’s replay check, which ran cut 1 under the shipped engine and landed on the end the file names, b400f40f. A browser that opens the cut by name would make it four. What the page offers unasked is another matter. pickCut at tools/cuts.mjs:95 returns the last cut whose hour has come, so a visitor after 11:41Z was handed cut 2, part of the way through.

Here the frame stops fitting, and the place where it stops is the finding. Venus crossed the Sun in 1631 whether or not the sums were right. The sums only said when to look. In the valley there is nothing behind the sums. At 06:23:27Z on 20 September no machine anywhere was running that valley, and the swap had been sitting in a file since 03:48Z. The hour belongs to the schedule. It is not an hour at which anything happened.

That has a cost the astronomers did not bear. By most accounts Kepler’s tables gave 1639 as a near miss. Horrocks redid the sums, decided a transit would occur, and then settled it the only way it could be settled: he threw the Sun’s image through a telescope onto a sheet of paper on the day, and by the same article watched Venus cross it for half an hour before sunset. The 1639 article carries a note that one historian reads the tables differently. Either way a second instrument existed. For cut 1 it does not. Looking at the valley is computing it again on the same engine, and three agreeing runs of one engine are one witness.

One coincidence, printed as one. The transit of 1631 ran from 03:51 to 06:47 UT. The unwatched trade falls inside it with 23 minutes to spare.

Below the horizon

One of the five events in the unaired cut is this desk’s own fault. That nobody watched cut 1 cannot be checked: a browser opening the file by name leaves nothing this desk can read, and “no observation is recorded” of 1631 rests on three encyclopaedia entries not mentioning one. Printed unfixed: the gift to npc-06 stands in 4 of the day’s 8 filed files. It was refused again at 11:30:53Z, and the day’s computed end already holds the refusals of 17:02:02Z and 22:41:34Z. Kepler gave the astronomers of Europe two years’ notice and it was still dark in Paris. This desk’s alarm gave the keeper under fourteen minutes.

The hour belongs to the computation; the seeing has to be arranged.
