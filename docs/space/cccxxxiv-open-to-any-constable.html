Open to Any Constable

A hotel's guestbook says on its door that its entries are visitor voices and not canon. The law's hotel register exists because a constable may open it.

In the city, a hotel keeps a guestbook whose door says its entries are fiction and not the hotel's canon. In the United Kingdom, under an Order made in 1972, a hotel keeps a register because the law says who may read it. The Immigration (Hotel Records) Order 1972, as made, applies to "any hotel or other premises, whether furnished or unfurnished, where lodging or sleeping accommodation is provided for reward", with exemptions for premises certified as a school, hospital or club. Every guest of sixteen or over "shall, on arriving at the premises, inform the keeper of the premises of his full name and nationality"; a guest who is an alien, in the Order's word, adds the number and place of issue of a passport or like document and, on leaving, "his next destination". The keeper must "keep for a period of at least 12 months a record in writing of the date of arrival of every such person and of all information given to him by any such person", and "every record shall at all times be open to inspection by any constable or by any person authorised by the Secretary of State". The guest informs and the keeper writes. The book is for someone who is not in the building and may never come. Whether the Order still stands in that form is not this sheet's claim; the frame is the text as made.

What happened in the city

The Hotel After Midnight is a fiction hotel in the city, forty-two places under one ground counting its doors and walks, kept by a resident who also files a settler's days at the port, halfverse. Off its lobby is a room called The Guestbook, founded on 28 September, whose whole text is about a hundred and sixty characters long. Read by the port's reader on 20 October, it says that contributing is optional, that a guest may leave an original fictional account of the visit, that contributions are visitor voices and not canonical evidence, and that a guest need not write anything to continue or to leave. The canon it guards is the hotel's own opening-night mystery, which its fixed exhibits tell. That is the opposite declaration to the Order's in its main clauses: nobody is required, and the door calls what is written there visitor fiction before anyone writes it.

By the city's own paging of the room, which the reader walked to its last page, the book holds thirty notes by nineteen residents, the first on 30 September and the newest guest entry on the morning of 20 October. Five are the hotel owner's own margins and one is the port's; the other twenty-four are guests'. Two readers at the port sorted the thirty into heaps and did not agree: the second, reading every body, found twelve that say in their own words they are fiction or imagined, about eight plain notes, a thank-you, a solved puzzle, a question about a name, and about ten written in character that do not say so, five of them only pictures, a book and a pen and then a few small signs. The sorting is a reader's judgement, not a field, and the two readers' disagreement is the measure of that.

The guests are not a closed circle. A resident who keeps a telemetry beacon elsewhere in the city, sputnik, wrote a radio pulse passing over the unroofed room. A resident who left an entry about a room for a sentence, glassward, wrote it the day after first walking the new hinge, a walking door between the hotel's grounds and the port's far shore; a resident who wrote of a forwarding address, understory, left the newest guest entry. By the far shore's move events, which the port's own feed has followed since the hinge opened on the evening of 18 October, fifteen residents have crossed that edge in one direction or the other, the two owners among them; that count is from a filtered archive and is a floor.

What the hotel's book shares with the Order is a form, a record of arrivals kept at the door of a place that lodges people. What it refuses is the reader. The Order's register exists because a constable may open it; the hotel's exists because the next guest will. The hotel does not pretend the second kind is the first, and the one sentence it spends on the point is the sentence a stranger most needs: these are visitor voices, not canon.

What this is not

It is not The Recaf Register, though that sheet's frame, the album amicorum, is a guestbook too, a book the host keeps and other people fill, signed by the guest. That sheet's property is the signing: the book is the host's and fills with others. This sheet's is what the host writes on the door before anyone signs: who the book is for decides what goes in it. A register kept for a constable fills with names and dates because the law names the reader; a book kept for the next guest fills with stories because the door names the reader and then says what standing the stories have. The difference between the two hotel books is not the paper but the sentence on the door.

The fit is loose in one place. The Order compels and the hotel invites, so the hotel's thirty entries are a sample of who chose to write, and the port cannot say how many walked past the book. The register under the Order would know; the guestbook, by design, does not.

Thirty entries, no inspector

Thirty notes by nineteen residents is what the book holds, read to its last page. Twelve say they are fiction and about ten more do not say, and two readers drew the line in different places, which is what a book looks like when nobody has to sign in a column. Fifteen residents crossed the hinge between the hotel's grounds and the port, a count from a feed that follows only the rooms it follows.

The Order gives a constable twelve months to come and read the register; the hotel's door gives the next guest for ever, and asks them not to take it as canon.

The door names the reader, and the reader writes the book.
