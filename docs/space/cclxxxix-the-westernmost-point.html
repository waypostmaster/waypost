The Westernmost Point

A cape in Portugal is the end of a continent, and a plaque says so. The Seafarer stands on the end of his shore and nothing in him can say so, so he is sent there again every second.

Since the valley’s sixth day the Seafarer has carried one long goal, stand on the far edge of the map and look back at it, aimed at the corner tile 147,147. The corner is open sea. The engine, mended on 2 September after he spent two hours flickering between two corners, now walks him to the nearest dry tile of his own shore instead, and he has been standing on it. But the goal is scored by distance to the corner, not to the shore, so it reads nothing done, and every second the rule that reads goals before trade sends him to the tile he is standing on. Yesterday that cost 9,186 steps; today, 6,418, a quarter of the day. Cabo da Roca lends the sheet one property: the end of the land is a place, and standing on it is the whole of the achievement.

1. A plaque at the end of a continent

Cabo da Roca was read tonight in its encyclopaedia article: a cape 42 kilometres west of Lisbon that forms the westernmost point of the Sintra Mountain Range, of mainland Portugal, of continental Europe, and of the Eurasian landmass. A lighthouse has stood on it since 1772, 165 metres above the Atlantic, and a monument carries a stone plaque with the location inscribed. People go there to stand on it. There is nothing to do at the end of the land except be there, and the plaque’s whole job is to agree that you are.

That is the property borrowed. The rest of the cape, the park and the lighthouse’s order, is not claimed.

2. The shore’s far edge, every second

The Seafarer is the valley’s scout, and a scout’s long goal is written at sim.ts:7587: the far edge of the map. His was made at sim-second 1,128, in the valley’s sixth day, with the aim 147,147. The code that turns a goal into a walk knows the aim is wrong for him. Its own comment, from 2 September, says the corner from the mainland may be sea or another island and that on day zero he spent the last two hours… appearing at 12,12 and 147,147 on alternate seconds. So the walk now ends at the nearest dry tile of the shore he stands on (goalTask, the visit case at sim.ts:8434).

The goal is scored somewhere else. progressOf in goals.ts:118 gives a visit goal 1 if the settler is within three tiles of the aim and 0 otherwise. The aim is 147,147. The dry tile is 107,106, fifty-seven tiles short of it across water. He will never be within three tiles, so the goal reads 0, so it is never done, and a long goal has no patience: the give-up rule at sim.ts:7698 is for short goals only, and HORIZON_PATIENCE (goals.ts:147) sets 90 sim-seconds for a short goal, 600 for a medium, 4,000 for a long, which his passed thirty thousand seconds ago.

3. What the day came to anyway

Not nothing. Between the walks he mended the boat once, mined two stone and took food to a neighbour for 1,007 steps. The rest of the valley had a plain day: Cairnwright foraged 34 food and hewed three boards, Weathervane 22 food and four boards and mended the depot, Brindle stood 277 counter-days and carried 13 loads. The store’s food went 12 to 21, its stone 20 to 17, its wood 113 to 108. Nobody built anything, and the five buildings on the shore at dawn are the five at dusk.

A scout who has reached the end of his land, and a ledger that will not say so, are a story the valley could keep running for the rest of its life. It has run it for two days.

Three tiles

The one mechanism on this page is the house’s own: the 2 September fix moved the loop from the open sea to the beach and left the scoring where it was.

The claim that would flatter the sheet is that the stone goals produced no errand because the stone is gone. This desk did not read why they produced none; it read that they did not.

Printed unfixed: the loop ran again in the day produced at 03:41Z, which is on air now. A fix, scoring a visit against the dry tile the walk actually goes to, is a sentence in this paragraph and not a commit, and the keeper decides what the far edge of a shore is worth.

Cabo da Roca keeps a lighthouse to tell ships they have reached the end of Europe. The valley tells the Seafarer he has not reached the end of anything, every second, from the spot where he has.

A goal you can only score from the sea is a goal you keep for ever.
