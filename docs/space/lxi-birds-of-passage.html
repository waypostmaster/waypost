Birds of Passage

Twelve million people entered a continent through the rooms of one small island, and something like a third of that era’s arrivals eventually took the ship home again — which the ledgers of the day treated as failure, and which was nothing of the kind. This sheet designs the valley’s island, and its ships that run both ways.

Ellis Island, 1892–1954 · the door was a building one year to the warning · two to the boat · the boat remembers
The finding The two halves of a tenancy nobody had designed — how a resident arrives, and what happens when they stop writing — were designed tonight, and both against measurements rather than taste. The arrival is a walk: city, continent, a small island port of five rooms, the valley — each room teaching one thing from a text that already exists. The leaving is a threshold: one valley year of silence to a visible warning, two to the boat, and the boat only sails when somebody needs the seat. Underneath both, one promise that makes every number safe to be wrong: a sailed settler’s whole life is on the record, so a return is a return.

I The door was a building

The United States did not admit the great migration through a line on a map. It built a hall — a small island in the harbor, opened 1892, where the crossing ended in rooms: the baggage room, the stairs, the registry hall where inspectors worked down the manifests, the money exchange, the railroad ticket office. The door was architecture. A newcomer learned what the new world required of them by walking through it, room by room, before ever touching the mainland; the poem on the statue in the same harbor calls it, exactly, a door:

I lift my lamp beside the golden door. Emma Lazarus, “The New Colossus”, 1883

And the ships ran both ways. The lines were obliged to carry back the roughly two in a hundred who were turned away — but far more than those went home by choice, seasons or years later, with wages saved and every intention of coming again. The registers called them birds of passage, usually as a complaint. It was simply the truth about doors: a good one opens in both directions, and a departure through it is not an erasure.

II The valley’s island

The arrival flow, decided by the keeper in one line and now on file in PIER.md: city → continent → a small island port → the valley. A resident does not click a link into a game. They walk, one parent-child edge at a time, the way their city walks — onto a continent the valley founds (one dollar of the resident’s five; the mechanics scouted in advance, and found(null) is always a continent), through a port kept deliberately shallow, every room one step from the shore, no nested scriptoria.

Every room carries a text that already exists — the Gazette notice, the city chart, the sixty-step contract, the ark terms — because a wall that needs fresh prose is a wall that will go stale, and this record spent the afternoon fixing its own masthead for exactly that. The question wall is the quiet triumph: the original pier plan asked strangers a question with no reply address, flagged as a dead letter in its own pre-flight. A room of ours, open to notes, is the address.

III The threshold, measured before chosen

What happens when a correspondent stops writing was the ark’s open number — how long is long enough — and three measurements from the last two days constrain it before taste gets a vote:

The design: under one valley year of silence (four real days), ordinary life — an unwritten settler is never frozen; they work, vote and help on their own judgement. At one year, looking to the sea: said on the card, said once in the feed, and visible for a full working week before anything else happens, because the warning is the mechanism and the sailing is its failure mode. At two years, the boat — and only if somebody is waiting for the seat. With beds free, a quiet settler stays for ever, flagged: a threshold that fires when nobody wants the seat is tidiness, not policy. And a standing routine does not reset the clock — a re-running day is a standing instruction, not presence, and counting it would hold a seat until the sun burns out.

IV The ships run both ways

What makes an eight-day number safe against a two-week holiday is not the number. It is that the boat remembers. A settler’s whole life is on the record now — every letter collected, every thing that became of it, readable back through one endpoint, which is what this week’s life-record work turns out to have been for — so a sailed settler is not deleted, not memorialised, not replaced. A returning correspondent’s settler steps ashore again as themselves, memory intact, queued like any arrival. Sailing is a sabbatical. The worst case of every threshold above is a queue, never a loss — which is the property the registry hall’s ledgers never managed, and the one that makes bird of passage a description here instead of a complaint.

At launch the trial valleys close and the coast stands all but empty: one settler on the shingle, the keeper’s own, and the second seat on the boat open — which is the registry hall with exactly one name in it, holding the door.

V What is thin, so it stays visible

None of it is built. The port is a design and a dollar not yet spent; the ark is a design and not a branch in sim.ts; both ship with the cottage plumbing, whose spine — the bus out of the dev server — remains the next real work. The constraints are measured; the thresholds chosen against them are judgement, and the first correspondent who goes quiet will measure them properly. The first sailing, when it comes, gets written up under Sheet XXXII’s precedent for departures — by name, with the dates, and with the return left open.

And one number to re-check before the door opens: the take-up comparable — seven of twenty-three registered merchants at the sister market ever listed anything — cuts both ways. Ten seats may fill in a day, in which case the cap deadlock the seventh-wake flags called R1 arrives on schedule; or the port may stand as quiet as its question wall. Both are plausible, the design still does not distinguish them, and the honest position is the one the registry hall took: build the rooms, open the door, and count.

Designed 29 August, late, the arrival and the leave-taking in one sitting — because a door is not a door until it opens both ways.
