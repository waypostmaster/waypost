Every line above is a real count out of agents/log.jsonl. The most-said sentence in the history of this valley is a plan to walk to a building that does not exist, and the second is a request for materials for a building that cannot exist, and both are said by a woman who is starving on top of a hundred logs.

Act II

What the settlers asked for

Three hundred and sixty-six wishes were filed that the catalogue had no word for. These are real. These were typed by people made of arithmetic who have two stones and a boat.

“A chainsaw for forestry, to fell trees rapidly and safely without tiring out the forester.”
“A woodchipper to process sawdust into pellets or lumber for construction.”
“A water testing kit to check for safety before drinking lake water.”
“A moisture sensor to determine when cut wood has dried sufficiently.”
“A way to see through the lake to the bottom, enabling the discovery of stone or ore deposits.”
“A designated driftwood collection area with natural tide-driven accumulation patterns.”
“A natural soundscape at night, where the water's gentle movement is the dominant sound and not the creaking of wood and stone.”

The settlement that filed a request for a moisture sensor could not, on that day or any other, make a plank. It had been unable to make a plank since the boat. It would remain unable to make a plank for four thousand eight hundred and sixty-six ticks, which is the entire recorded history of the place. It wanted the sensor for the planks.

Two of the three hundred and sixty-six were things this game genuinely had no word for and now does: a notice board, asked for seven times, and a ledger of who has done what for whom, asked for fifteen. Both are built. Both do something you can measure. That is the part of this document that is not a joke.

Act III

Seven hundred and fifty-six times, nothing

The single most common event in a nine-hour run of this game was a sentence with no subject and no object.

756 × “«name» could not do that, and turned to what they would do instead.”
Could not do what. Instead of what. For seven hundred and fifty-six consecutive occasions the game declined to say, and the model on the other end of the wire was therefore asked to learn from a silence.

It had twenty-odd different reasons available to it and used none of them. This report noticed the same line one sitting ago, at fifty occurrences, described it as “a message that names nothing”, fixed the two things behind it, and then left the message alone. It is now fifteen times larger and it says exactly as much.

Act VI

The actual fault, which is very stupid

Nine settlers in ten were holding nothing. For the whole run.

bare-handed                                14,952 of 16,439   (91%)
snapshots with nobody holding a tool       660 of 1,629   (40%)
boards, ever, in the landing                 0 of 1,629
bars of iron, ever, in the landing           0 of 1,629

A house costs ten wood and nothing else. Everything else on this coast wants a board, and cutting a board wants an axe, and making an axe wants an axe's worth of attention that nobody was paying. A house is the only thing fourteen empty-handed people can build. They built nine of it. Then eleven of it.

The game already knew. The settler thinking for themselves puts tools above the work and the comment says why — “A builder without an axe cannot make a saw.” Left alone, this valley equips itself by tick 86 and is cutting boards by tick 286. Hand it to a model and that branch is never reached, because a settler on the bus runs the plan they were given, and the plan was:

280 × haul   245 × chop   227 × rest   146 × goto
1 × build   0 × craft   0 × hew

Bare hands are now the first thing a settler on the bus sees to before they will take an instruction. On a valley directed exactly the way the night was directed: before, no stone was ever mined and no tool was ever made in four thousand ticks. After, stone at tick 65 and an axe at tick 69.

It was first written one level higher than that, where it also applied to the settlers thinking for themselves — who were never the problem. That version got every settler tooled up, thirteen of thirteen, and cost the valley its furnace, its deep mine, its yard, its mill, all of its charcoal and every bar of iron it would ever have smelted. The number being watched went up and the thing it stood for went away. It is the sixteenth fault in the table above, committed twice in one morning, four hours apart.

The game is fixed

Every refusal now says what it was and what stopped it. Every settler can get an axe into their hand whoever is thinking for them. The ladder is walked from bare hands by a check that fails the build and prints what the whole thing is standing on, which turns out to be one line of code that lets you hack a plank out of a log with an axe, badly.

Eight of the twelve buildings in this game and three of its nine goods rest entirely on that one line. Nobody knew. Now something does, and it says so every time you run the checks.

This is the sixteenth time. The seventeenth is presumably running right now, in a browser tab, on the old code, building a house.

“More wood for the hall.”
— Mara, forester, three hundred and thirty-six times, about a hall that was never proposed, never priced and never possible, while standing on a hundred and seven logs she could not lift a plank out of.
