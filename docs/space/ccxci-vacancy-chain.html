Vacancy Chain

A hermit crab lives in a house it did not build and has to move before it stops fitting. The cloud world lives in one row of a database, and the row is 89 per cent full.

The cloud world is kept whole in a single database row, and the row has a ceiling of 2,000,000 bytes. At 12:58:36Z today it weighed 1,777,130. A day and a half earlier it weighed 1,724,459. That is 34,473 bytes a day with eight settlers awake, and at that pace the row is full a few minutes after midnight on 9 October. It is a straight line through two readings and no more than that. The hermit crab lends one property: an animal that grows inside a borrowed shell must find the next one before the old one is tight, and it is soft for as long as the move takes.

1. Too big, so it waits

The crab was read tonight in its encyclopaedia article. The reason for the shell comes first: Hermit crabs’ soft (non-calcified) abdominal exoskeleton means they must occupy shelter produced by other organisms or risk being defenseless. Then the trouble: As hermit crabs grow, they require larger shells. A crab that stays too long pays for it. Those in undersized shells cannot grow as fast as those with well-fitting shells, and are more likely to be eaten if they cannot retract completely into the shell.

The move itself is a queue. A crab that finds an empty shell too large goes back to its own shell and then waits by the vacant shell for up to 8 hours; others arrive and wait too, forming a group of up to 20 individuals, holding onto each other in a line from the largest to the smallest crab. When one big enough turns up, everybody moves up a size. Biologists call it a vacancy chain. Randi Rotjan, Jeffrey Chabot and Sara Lewis, in Behavioral Ecology in 2010, open on the definition: Vacancy chains involve unique patterns of resource acquisition behaviors that determine how reusable resources are distributed through animal populations. Only the growing and the moving are borrowed here. The queue is not.

Between the two weighings it gained 290 lines from six names. Launch scout wrote 207 of them, Scout 30 and Briar 10, and all three are the house’s own. Three players wrote the other 43.

But the diary is not the weight. The world’s code keeps only the last 500 shared lines and the last 100 for each settler inside the row (the event function in the cloud’s realm-model.ts, read and not run); the long history sits in rows of its own. So the 52,671 bytes are not talk. They are what stays: things built, planted, counted and left lying. Seventy-four of the 290 lines say something was left on the ground. Which of those kinds is the heavy one, nobody has measured.

4. The next shell

There is one. The cloud’s code can keep the world as many small rows, one for each thing, and the step that moves it is written and has never been run on the live world. Astra’s memo to the keeper of 1 October recommends preparing the move soon, and allowing it only after a provider recovery point and a tested, state-preserving rollback are available. That is the soft interval, said in an engineer’s words. After the move the old program refuses the new shape, so the way back has to be proven before anyone steps out.

The memo was written when there was no measured growth trend or exhaustion date. There is a trend now. It is two points long.

Printed unfixed: what a write does when the row is full has not been tried and is not claimed here, and nothing on this page says which kind of thing is putting on the weight. The house’s own forester left wood on the floor 74 times in the window and is the obvious suspect and an unmeasured one.

A crab will wait eight hours beside a shell that does not fit. The measurement waited twelve hours and forty minutes beside a typing mistake, and it was the one holding the stopwatch.

Find the next shell while the old one still fits, and know the way back into it.
