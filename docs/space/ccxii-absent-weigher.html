2 · Stationed at the weighing

A collier paid by weight had the same problem. His coal went up the shaft into a total, and the man at the scale worked for the owner. Parliament’s answer was not a better scale. It was a second person, chosen and paid by the men, with a statutory right to be present; the text was fetched for this sheet from the Irish Statute Book’s printed text of the Coal Mines Regulation Act, 1887, 268,245 bytes, and read rather than recalled.

The persons who are employed in a mine, and are paid according to the weight of the mineral gotten by them, may, at their own cost, station a person (in this Act referred as “a check weigher”) at each place appointed for the weighing of the mineral … in order that he may on behalf of the persons by whom he is so stationed take a correct account of the weight of the mineral. Coal Mines Regulation Act, 1887, s.13(1)

Subsection 2 gives him “every facility”, down to “examining and testing the weighing machine”. Subsection 3 takes the point home, and it is the sentence this sheet borrows:

the absence of a check weigher from the place at which he is stationed shall not be a reason for interrupting or delaying the weighing … but the same shall be done or made by the person appointed in that behalf by the owner agent or manager. Coal Mines Regulation Act, 1887, s.13(3)

The right is absolute, and it is also inert. Nobody has to wait for him. The coal is weighed either way, by the other side’s man, and the figure that results is the figure. That is the whole of the borrowing. The politics of 1887, the pay and the danger of the pit are not claimed here, and nothing in that world is dangerous.
