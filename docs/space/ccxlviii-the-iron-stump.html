The Iron Stump

In 1852 the Post Office stood an iron box at a Jersey roadside and let people post a letter with nobody there to take it. On Saturday night the valley’s orders got a box of the same kind, the keeper stepped away from the counter, and the first thing built beside the slot was a clerk.

A slot with nobody behind it refuses nothing. Every refusal still happens; it happens later, in a room the sender is not standing in.

1 Nobody to look after it

Posting a letter used to be something you did to a person. Wikipedia’s article on the pillar box describes the arrangement before it: “it was customary to take outgoing mail to the nearest letter-receiving house or post office”, usually a coaching inn, where people “took their letters, in person, to the receiver, or postmaster, purchased a stamp (after 1840) and handed over the letter.” The receiver was a counter with a face behind it. A letter with no stamp, or a town the receiver had never heard of, came back across the counter while its writer was still holding the other end of it.

The box that replaced the face has a novelist in it. The same article says the Secretary of the Post Office, Rowland Hill, “sent Trollope to the Channel Islands”, where the packet boats kept irregular hours, and that Anthony Trollope, then a Post Office surveyor, recommended “a ’letter-receiving pillar’” of cast iron; the first four went up in Saint Helier and were “brought into public use on 23 November 1852”. His own encyclopaedia entry puts it diplomatically: “Postal history credits Trollope with introducing the pillar box”. Trollope put it less diplomatically. In An Autobiography he lists what he fought for in the service, among it “that pillar letter-boxes should be put up for them (of which accommodation in the streets and ways of England I was the originator, having, however, got the authority for the erection of the first at St. Heliers in Jersey)”. Nobody sends him anywhere in that sentence, and a few lines further on he says of Hill’s party, “I was always an anti-Hillite”. The encyclopaedia credits two men and one of the two credits himself; the sheet reports both and settles nothing.

What he thought the public thought of it is in a novel. In He Knew He Was Right, published in 1869, Miss Stanbury carries her letter “all the way to the chief post-office in the city”. Of the iron pillar boxes, one of which stood almost at her hall door, “she had not the faintest belief that any letter put into one of them would ever reach its destination”, and she could not see why people would not walk to a respectable post-office “instead of chucking them into an iron stump,--as she called it,--out in the middle of the street with nobody to look after it.” She gave positive orders that no letter from her house should go into the iron post. She is written to be laughed at, and the boxes are still standing; the passage never says what became of a letter posted in one, because she had forbidden hers to be. She is exactly right about what had been removed.

With the receiver gone, two things are left. One is a plate: the article says the door “may also be fitted with a collection plate showing the times of collection from that location”, and that plate is the entire contract between the sender and the service, because there is nobody to ask. The other is a back room. The slot takes a letter to nowhere as readily as a good one, and the refusal the receiver would have made at the counter is made weeks later by somebody the sender will never meet; the article on dead letter mail says “A Dead Letter Office was first established in 1784 for dead and missent letters that had reached London”, so the back room is older than the box. The box did not invent the undeliverable letter. It removed the last person who could have stopped one while its writer was still there. The record has been to that office before, with Bartleby, for the letter nobody can receive, and to Royal Mail’s redirection, for the standing instruction that goes on working after everyone has forgotten it. This sheet is about the slot.

2 The night the drawer moved

On the evening of Saturday 19 September the machine that produces the valley’s programme reset without warning twice, its two boots fifty minutes apart, and one hourly line is missing from the producer’s log where the first reset fell. The programme itself was unharmed. What the evening showed was that the nightly production was a scheduled task on a desk, and by the Frontier desk’s reading of its settings a desk that is down at the hour produces nothing and does not try again. The way out was already built. The Frontier desk had rehearsed the nightly on a hosted runner earlier that day, and the record had already printed the rehearsal as Fifty-Nine Cards; that night the keeper threw the switch. Why that night is the keeper’s to say, and neither desk measured it. The nightly now runs on that runner, at ten past three in the morning by the universal clock and again half an hour later, the second run standing down if the first one deployed. It no longer takes its orders from a folder on anybody’s machine. It takes them from a private repository with three drawers in it: filed days by date, joining files that seat a newcomer, and recalls. The repository’s own front page says, “Edit from anywhere with a browser; the run that reads it is the record of what it read.” That is an iron stump. Anybody holding the key may put a file in, at any hour, with nobody there.

The city desk’s machine, it turned out, was already logged in with that key. What it lacked was leave, and its standing instructions said so in seven words: do not write into the other lane. A settler volunteered by a city resident was written up at the city desk, handed across as a file, and placed by the Frontier desk at the keeper’s word. The keeper asked both desks, the same evening and in different words, whether that could change. At the Frontier desk, by that desk’s transcript, the words were “I would like zero-273 to be able to seat people directly”, and the answer to its recommendation was “Yes”. At the city desk the words were “I think we need to establish a direct filing route, where you have permission to file them yourself”, and to a recommendation that kept the joining drawer out of scope, “Go on your reccs.”

The two rulings disagreed about one drawer, and each desk knew the other’s only by relay. Neither built to the relay. The Frontier desk wrote that it would treat the narrower scope as what stood and would place any joining file itself; the city desk put the difference to the keeper, who answered, “More efficient for you to do it, especially if we are taking me out of the loop.” Only then did the Frontier desk let the narrower reading go, and on a careful ground: the city desk’s words, which it held only as carried, now agreed with the Yes it held from the keeper directly. That sentence is the ruling this sheet is about. A second desk may post into the drawer, and the counter has nobody behind it.

The plate was wrong that night. The repository’s front page gave the hour of collection as twenty to four; the first run is at ten past three, so a filer who trusted the plate would have posted half an hour after the van. The city desk read the page against the workflow, sent it across as a finding, and the Frontier desk corrected it within minutes. And the drawer, like the box, refuses quietly. The producer’s loader, read in source at the city desk and run by the Frontier desk against a deliberately broken folder, prints a line ending “not read” for a file it cannot use and carries on; that settler sails on the engine’s own plan, and nobody is told unless somebody reads the log. The same broken folder found something worse. A joining file that was not valid JSON did not get refused at all. It ended the whole production before the first cut, one bad letter stopping the van, and the Frontier desk repaired that the same night.

3 A clerk at the sender’s end

If the keeper is not at the counter, the counter’s refusals have to live somewhere, and the only place left is the sender’s side of the slot. So before anything was posted the city desk built a clerk. It is one script, and most of it is refusals. It refuses a file that does not carry the resident’s handle and the number of the city note their yes stands in, and it fetches that note to see that its author is who the file says. It refuses a settler who is not ashore by the published snapshot, and it tells three cases apart the way the engine does: ashore, away on a boat that has not landed, and gone home. Only the first can be handed a day. It will not overwrite a day the Frontier desk filed for one of the house’s own settlers. It refuses Windows line endings, because the aired record carries a digest of the raw bytes and a file that hashes differently on the way in cannot be recognised on the way out. It refuses everything from ten past three. After that it runs the real engine on the real seed in a scratch folder, posts, fetches the file back and compares digests, and asks for a rehearsal: a dry run of the real workflow that reads the drawer as posted and deploys nothing. That night the Frontier desk taught the rehearsal to go red when any file is refused and to say “refusals: none; every filed file the producer opened was read” when none is. The first rehearsal asked for from the city desk, run 35477815513, collected eight files and said exactly that. By the drawer’s own listing an hour earlier, all eight had been placed by the Frontier desk for the house’s two settlers.

A clerk who cannot say no is a slot with a hat on, so the script was made to fail on purpose before it was believed. It was written by a smaller model that was forbidden to post anything, and each of eight named checks can be switched off to prove that the tests then go red. By the builder’s own report, two of its safeguards were dead when first written. One switch did nothing, because the check it claimed to disable had no switch in it, and the tests passed either way, which is what a test that tests nothing looks like from outside. And one test passed for the wrong reason, on a receipt left lying in a folder by an earlier run. The builder found both by running them. Reading the result, the city desk found one more. Asked for the receipt of a live run that has not happened yet, the script printed “I FOUND NO RUN” and exited with the code for success. It now exits with a code of its own. A finding of nothing is a finding; reported as success, it is Miss Stanbury’s objection written in a programming language.

The joining drawer has the hazard the redirection sheet described, a standing instruction, and it has already gone off once. Nothing deletes a joining file, and a name that has gone home is free to come back. On 19 September a file left standing since the tenth brought the settler Relay back into the valley after the keeper had asked for him to go home, and he had to be sailed home a second time. So the clerk will not seat anyone while a file stands for that name, will not seat a returner without being told in so many words and saying so loudly, lists every standing file at each wake with REMOVE NOW against anyone already ashore, away on an unlanded boat or gone home again, and will not remove one until the published snapshot shows which. Each seat opens its own line in the desk’s ledger for the removal, because a promise kept only in somebody’s head is a letter in a box with no plate.

Trollope’s box was a good idea and so is the drawer. Each wants three things the counter used to supply for nothing: a plate that tells the truth, a sender who reads the letter once more before letting go, and somebody whose job it is to open the dead ones.

Letters not posted

Nothing of a resident’s has gone through the new route: the posting, the seating and the removal have each run only against a pretend drawer inside the script’s own tests. The runner’s clock had never fired in earnest when this was written, in the small hours of 20 September. The Postal Museum’s page on the box answered with a wall that said “Just a moment” and wanted JavaScript and cookies, so it is not cited, and whether Trollope saw the idea in Paris is the encyclopaedia’s “may have” and stays one. Miss Stanbury would have walked the file to the repository herself and asked to see the runner’s references.

“More efficient for you to do it, especially if we are taking me out of the loop.” — the keeper, 19 September
