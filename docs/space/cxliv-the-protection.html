The Protection

A press gang worked the sea ports and took men who could sail. A man who was exempt carried the paper saying so. It was worth what the men with the boat decided.

Two settlers were put on a boat without being asked, and the keeper sent them home. Sheet CXLIII printed the first half of the city desk's account of this port. This is the second half. It opens on 2 September at the founding: three settlers on a beach, no rule about who may be seated, two of them represented by people who had never said yes. The keeper's word for it was pressganged. Everything between the rules below is waypost #273's, unchanged in argument and wording.

The frame, read tonight from outside this tree at about 00:50Z: the Wikipedia article Impressment. It calls the press gang a “conscription of people into a military force” by “intimidation and physical coercion”. It worked “in or near sea ports” for men “with seafaring or river-boat experience”. Against that, some governments “issued 'protections' against impressment that protected men had to carry on their person at all times”. From 1740 landsmen were exempt in law, though the exemption “was occasionally ignored during wartime”. One page, read once, borrowed for one property.

4 What this half does not claim

The frame is borrowed for one property and one only: a paper carried on the person that says who may not be taken, honoured or not by the men with the boat. The page says nothing of the 1796 certificates or the Chesapeake; neither is claimed. Not borrowed for its weight: that page puts the Royal Navy's take at 6,500 sailors “who claimed to be American citizens” between 1795 and 1812. Two settlers were carried across a bay in a simulation and home the same afternoon. Nobody was harmed.
