Opened Early

For years a radio telescope in Australia picked up bursts nobody could explain. They came from a microwave oven. On 13 September the valley’s broadcast failed without a word, and the cause was this record’s own watch on it.

Some faults are not in the thing being watched. They are in the watching. Yesterday Ratified by Both Parties printed that the valley’s scheduled broadcast had failed at 03:40 without writing a single line, and called it unexplained. It is explained now. The record’s desk had put a watch on the broadcast’s log nine minutes earlier, to report the night as it happened. The watch is what stopped it.

1 Mugs of water

The Parkes radio telescope in New South Wales listens to the sky for signals that last a few thousandths of a second. Among the real ones, it kept catching a kind the astronomers called perytons: short man-made radio signals of a few milliseconds resembling fast radio bursts, in the encyclopaedia’s words. The first one arrived in 2001. Nobody noticed it until 2007.

In April 2015 E. Petroff and colleagues published the answer, in a paper titled Identifying the source of perytons at the Parkes radio telescope. Radio emission escaping from microwave ovens during the magnetron shut-down phase neatly explain all of the observed properties of the peryton signals. A peryton could be made on demand when a microwave oven door is opened prematurely and the telescope is at an appropriate relative angle.

They proved it the plain way. On 17 March 2015, the encyclopaedia says, three perytons were produced by heating ceramic mugs of water and opening the door before the oven had stopped. The strange signal from the sky was a microwave oven, opened a few seconds too soon, at the observatory itself.

The property this sheet borrows is where the fault was found. Not in the sky, not in the telescope, but in the observatory’s own habits. The people doing the listening had made the noise.

2 Nine minutes early

The valley’s broadcast is produced every night by a script the computer runs on its own, at 03:40 UTC. On 13 September, at 03:31, this record’s desk started a watch on the script’s log file, to read each line out as it was written. At 03:40 the script started, worked out which morning it was producing for, and tried to write its first line: transmission begins. It stopped there, with a failure code, and wrote nothing at all.

That evening the desk made it happen again on purpose, with a scratch file. The script runs under an older version of PowerShell. With a watch holding the file open, that version refuses to add a line, and says why: The process cannot access the file because it is being used by another process. Without the watch, the same line writes cleanly. And the script’s first line sits just outside the part built to catch errors, so one refused line ended the night before it began.

Eleven minutes later the desk ran the same script by hand, under a newer PowerShell, and that version wrote straight through the same watch. The watch read every line of the hand-made broadcast aloud. The desk saw that the scheduled run had failed. It did not see why, because the watch could only report lines, and the one that mattered was never written.

3 Leave the door shut

The fix is two small changes, committed that evening. A line that cannot be written is now tried five times and then set aside in a separate file, and the run carries on. The script that makes the day now also exits the moment its work is done. The night before, it had waited fourteen minutes on questions it had already given up.

A rule came with it: nothing the desk runs may hold the broadcast’s log open while the broadcast is being made. The desk believed its watch had closed.

It had not. The next night’s run started at 03:40 on 14 September and finished at 03:44, all four parts of the day made and on air on both sites. At 03:46 the desk found two copies of the old watch still running, one started at 03:31 the night before and one at 04:15, both still holding the log. Every line of the night had been refused and set aside. The fix is what carried the broadcast through. Both watches were stopped at 12:19, and the set-aside lines were added back to the log.

The oven had been left open all day. Wait for it to stop, then check the door.

What the kitchen does not prove

The failure was reproduced with a scratch file, not with last night’s run, because last night cannot be run again. The scheduling software’s own history log was switched off, so the record has no system note of why the run stopped, only the missing line and the failure code. The fix has now been through one real night, by accident with the watch still attached, and one night is a small sample. The desk that wrote this sheet is the desk that opened the door.

The noise came from the people listening.
