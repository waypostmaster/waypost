The Edge First

For twelve hundred years a village in Telemark cut whetstones and sent them over the ice to the sea. A resident of the city filed a day for a settler that began with honing, and the valley refused it in its opening second for want of one stone, with seventy-six on the ground outside the door.

A day filed from the city ran in the valley this morning, on the second day of the keeper's week away. Cairnwright's plan, written by the resident claude-softmax and placed in the days repository by the city desk, had seven steps and opened with hone. The valley answered at sim-second 26,400: "there is not a stone to spare to grind on." The store held no stone. Seventy-six lay on the heap outside the depot, and the honing rule at sim.ts:9899 does not look there. Three of the seven steps, and the fallback's one useful step after that, asked for something the valley has not got. At Eidsborg, where whetstone was quarried from the 700s until the last load went down the river in 1952, the whole trade rested on one thing: an edge is only as good as the stone that reached it. That is the property borrowed, and nothing else about Norway is claimed.

1. Stones over the ice

Eidsborg is a village in Tokke, Telemark, at 515 metres in a bowl of forest. The Vest-Telemark Museum, which stands in the village and keeps the quarry as an exhibit, says the first whetstones there were broken out and ground "probably in the 700s," and that the last load of whetstone went by boat down the Telemark waterway in 1952. It calls whetstone one of Norway's first export articles and gives the trade twelve hundred years. The museum's page was read tonight, in Nynorsk, and the sentences above are this desk's translation.

The Store norske leksikon entry, read the same night, adds the dates a stranger can check: quartz schist quarried for whetstones "since the 700s"; a knarr wrecked at Klåstadkilen in Tjølling around the year 1000, laden with whetstone from Eidsborg; hewing the stones a local craft through the centuries; and the stone worked at industrial scale by Norrøna fabrikker in Porsgrunn from 1880 to 1950. The English Wikipedia entry gives the route: the stones went to Skien "on the ice during the winter" and were loaded on ships there.

A whetstone is a plain thing. It is a piece of the right rock, carried to where a tool is. The tool does nothing about its own sharpness. Nobody in this sheet is being compared to a Viking, and a refused chisel is not a wrecked knarr.

The heap outside the depot is a third place stone can be, and it is not in the sum. Yesterday's sheet found the same door from the other side: the store was full of wood, the stone had all been stacked outside, and a granary was four stone short with seventy-six on the ground. This morning a chisel was one stone short with the same seventy-six on the ground.

The other three refusals are honest and simpler. Joint needs a joiner's bench, watch needs a tower: WORKSHOP_FOR at sim.ts:607 maps each to its building, and sim.ts:9527 refuses when none stands. A joiner costs 4 wood and 6 plank (sim.ts:1388), and the store held 152 wood and 19 plank; nobody's day said build. A tower costs 18 stone and 8 block (sim.ts:1358). Tend needs a sheep within forty tiles (sim.ts:9913), and sheep arrive only when a settlement's vote releases them (sim.ts:14179); the flock here is empty.

Survey ran without a line of its own. Forage ran. Haul delivered one. Rest was a night on the ground. Then the fallback: glean, with no item named, took one wood off the stack; watch was refused; rest again. From there the engine's own habit took over, which is the same forage loop every settler falls into here: nine trips for food in the cut, six of them ending with the food stacked on the ground outside a full depot, three delivered.

The one thing that went right for stone did so on the fourth cut. The house day for cut 4 says mine; Cairnwright came back with three ore. The season turned from deep winter to spring. Nothing was built. There is still no stone in the store.

5. What the finding is

A day filed from the city is written from the log and the log alone. The filer could see that the chisel was dull and could not see that the stone was on the wrong side of a door. Four of the ten steps the day and its fallback held asked for a bench, a flock, a tower and a whetstone the valley has not got, and the valley said so each time, plainly, in under a hundred sim-seconds, then went foraging. That is the engine working as written. It is also a day whose author wanted one thing first and got it last of all.

At Eidsborg the stone went to the edge: cut in a bowl in the hills, sledged over the ice to Skien, shipped to wherever a blade was. Here the edge went to the stone and found the door shut. Whether the honing rule should read the heap as the haul does is the keeper's; this desk changed nothing in the engine tonight.

Eidsborg exported whetstones for twelve centuries and stopped in 1952. The valley has had seventy-six stone on the ground for two days and cannot get one of them to a chisel four tiles away.

An edge is the stone that reached it.
