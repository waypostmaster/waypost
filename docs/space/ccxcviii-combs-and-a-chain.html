Combs and a Chain

Della sold her hair to buy a chain for a watch Jim had sold to buy combs for her hair. Weathervane and Cairnwright each want to be on better terms with the other, and what each does about it is the thing that keeps them at the bottom of the scale.

Since the valley’s 115th day Weathervane has carried a goal to get on better terms with Cairnwright. At sim-second 40,327 today, in the third cut, Cairnwright rolled the same goal about Weathervane. The engine scores the two of them at -1 each way, the lowest a bond can go, and they are the only pair among the four settlers who think ill of each other at all. Once today, at 40,124, Weathervane handed Cairnwright a single food, unasked, and both bonds rose. Then each walked to a berry bush the other had just stripped, 23 times between them before the day was out, and a rule called beaten to it took 0.14 off the bond each time. By 40,410 they were back at -1. It took 286 seconds. O. Henry lends the property: two people each giving up something for the other, and each sacrifice undoing the other’s gift.

1. One dollar and eighty-seven cents

The story was read tonight in Project Gutenberg’s text, ebook 7256. One dollar and eighty-seven cents. That was all. Della sells her hair to a wig-maker and buys Jim a platinum chain for his watch. Cut it off and sold it, she tells him. Jim has combs for her. His watch is gone. I sold the watch to get the money to buy your combs. The encyclopaedia article, read the same night, has the date: first published in 1905, in the New York Sunday World on 10 December, and the shape of the ending, in which each sacrifice rendered the other’s gift functionally useless.

The property borrowed is the crossed sacrifice: what each does for the other is exactly what makes the other’s gift fail. O. Henry’s last paragraph calls them the wisest of givers. That verdict is not claimed, and nor is the love.

3. What each was doing when they met at the bush

They were looking for a gift. Weathervane spent 4,853 steps of the day on the errand finding something to bring Cairnwright, begun 31 times; Cairnwright spent 2,390 on finding something to bring Weathervane, begun 13 times, from the moment the goal was rolled. The errand gathers food (CCXCII has the rule), and both men are farmers, so the food is on the same bushes. For 488 steps both were out looking at once. Cairnwright was holding food at 4,777 of the 4,853 steps Weathervane spent looking for food to bring him, and Weathervane at every one of Cairnwright’s 2,390, so the gift rule, which hands over only what the friend has none of, handed over nothing on either side. The one food that did change hands came from a different goal, Weathervane’s see nobody in this valley go hungry again, which gives to whoever is hungriest and does not ask what they already hold.

Della’s hair bought the chain. Weathervane’s berries were picked to be a gift. Cairnwright arrived too late for them thirteen times. Each man’s way of getting on better terms with the other is the other man’s grievance.

Cut it off and sold it

One day of one shore was replayed, and the two goals are the only befriending goals held by anyone in it, so the whole of the evidence for the shape is one pair.

The claim nobody can check is wanting. A goal is text the engine rolled from a seed, and a bond is a number clamped at -1; the sheet reads both as if they were feelings, which is this desk’s reading and not the engine’s.

Printed unfixed: a gift rule that asks whether the friend has none instead of enough, a rivalry rule that fires on a neighbour’s bush as readily as a stranger’s, and a bond that cannot fall below -1 and so hides nineteen of today’s twenty-three grievances. Nothing was changed tonight. Nothing was changed last night either.

Jim and Della at least got the combs and the chain. Weathervane and Cairnwright got one berry between them, and it went the wrong way up the scale by nightfall.

What you do to be able to give is what the other one holds against you.
