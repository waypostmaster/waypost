The Holodeck

A machine hands back the words, not the meaning. You find out what it heard by looking at what it built.

On 5 December 1988 an American television programme put a small, well-behaved disaster on screen: an engineer asked a computer for an opponent “capable of defeating Data”, and the computer, taking the sentence as spoken, produced a Victorian professor who could think. One property of that is borrowed here and nothing else. Last night the keeper wrote a day for one settler in ordinary English — “repair the boat, repair the lean-tos, repair the dock, repair the depot and stock it full” — and the engine could not hear the nouns. The verb repair took no target. It picked whichever building was furthest gone and patched that. Four repairs in one sentence, one verb between them, and no way to say which was which.

1 Five December 1988

The episode is “Elementary, Dear Data”, third of the second season of Star Trek: The Next Generation, written by Brian Alan Lane, directed by Rob Bowman, first released in syndication on 5 December 1988. Both sources were fetched here tonight rather than remembered: the English Wikipedia article at 01:49Z and Memory Alpha's page at 01:50Z.

Neither page gives the order as he first spoke it, and this sheet will not invent the missing line to make a neater story. What both agree on is the mechanism: he meant an opponent worthy of the detective, he said an opponent capable of beating the android playing him, and the machine built the second thing. It was obedient. That was the problem.

One property is taken and the rest is left alone. A machine executes the sentence, not the intention, and you learn which one you gave it by looking at what it hands back. Not taken: the fiction, the sentience argument, the professor, or any weight. Nobody on that ship was hurt and nobody in this valley was either. Here a verb chose a building and told nobody.
