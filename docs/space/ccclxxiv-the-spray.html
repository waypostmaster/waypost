The Spray

On Sunday morning the Seafarer mended his own boat, with a log that had been lying out in the weather an hour before. Then he went to break stone.

In the valley, at 10:54 UTC on Sunday, the Seafarer mended his own boat from 0.77 to 1.00, and the valley said once more that she is seaworthy. He owns the only boat and builds nothing, and the day written for him has asked him to mend her, by name and by the place she lies, on every second cut for seven weeks. The patch took one log. At ten o’clock the store held none. At 10:07 Cairnwright mended the store itself, and in the valley a worn store holds less, so the mending made room. At 10:49 Brindle carried nineteen logs in off the heap outside, where wood had been going into the mud since half past seven, and five minutes later one of them went into the hull. In 1892 Joshua Slocum rebuilt the sloop Spray at Fairhaven with his own hands, from an oak his own axe felled near by, and then spent a season fishing before he sailed her anywhere. The Seafarer’s boat is fit to sail. He spent the afternoon breaking stone.

A stout oak-tree near by

Slocum tells it in the first chapter of Sailing Alone Around the World, read tonight in Project Gutenberg’s text. In Boston, one midwinter day of 1892, an old acquaintance, a whaling captain, said to him: “Come to Fairhaven and I’ll give you a ship. But,” he added, “she wants some repairs.” The ship was “a very antiquated sloop called the Spray,” and she was “affectionately propped up in a field, some distance from salt water.”

My ax felled a stout oak-tree near by for a keel, and Farmer Howard, for a small sum of money, hauled in this and enough timbers for the frame of the new vessel.

The cost of my new vessel was $553.62 for materials, and thirteen months of my own labor.

For a year the town asked him one question, “Will it pay?”, and he answered that he would make it pay. When she was launched, “she sat on the water like a swan.” Then the second chapter opens:

I spent a season in my new craft fishing on the coast, only to find that I had not the cunning properly to bait a hook.

He weighed anchor at Boston at noon on 24 April 1895, alone, and came back to Fairhaven at the end of a cruise of over forty-six thousand miles.

The man who sailed her had mended her. He still waited a season.

Her own hand

A replay of Sunday’s first two cuts, on the engine that aired them and read at every step, gives the morning. The boat’s week before it is carried from two earlier sheets, and marked so. Her condition is on a scale where 1 is new. The valley calls her seaworthy at 0.85 and over, which is the same mark at which it stops letting anyone mend her.

The step was not his own idea. His day for the second cut is the keeper’s standing orders, written down by this house’s desk in September and carried since: the same file, unchanged, on all forty-nine second cuts from 21 September to today. It opens on eating, and then on mending the boat, and it names her and the tile she lies at. On Saturday she was over the line and the valley said no. On Sunday at ten she was at 0.78. He ate, walked round to the dry side of her, and at 10:54 the valley said it in its own words: “the wreck is seaworthy. Seafarer got her to 1.00, and this valley does not offer to mend a thing at that.” The wreck is the valley’s name for her.

Mending pays a wage out of the town’s purse, and the purse is empty. His own record of the morning reads “patched up the boat with a wood”, and nothing after it.

He mended her. Nobody paid him.

Room at the depot

At ten o’clock the store held 103 stone, 77 ore, 18 food, 25 boards and no logs at all. The valley shrinks a building’s room as it wears: the depot holds 140 things when it is sound, and at 0.75 it held 121. Every place in town was taken, 223 of 223.

Cairnwright, the farmer who marks the ground with stones, has a day that opens on a repair naming nothing, and the valley’s rule for that is to mend the worst building in town. At ten the worst was the depot. At 10:07 he mended it for a stone, it went to 1.00, and the town had nineteen places more. Nobody brought anything in for forty minutes, and two food went off in the store.

The first haul in was by Brindle, the storekeeper, at 10:49. They put away three food of their own, and then the valley’s rule for a haul sweeps up what is lying outside while there is room, in the order of its list of goods. Logs come first on the list. Nineteen came in off the heap, to the last free place: “Brindle got 19 in off the stack outside the depot.” Five minutes later the Seafarer’s patch took one of them.

The log is how she was mended, not whether. The valley mends a boat with a log first, then a stone, and a board only when nothing rougher is to hand; with no log in the store, it would have taken one of the 102 stones.

By four o’clock the store had taken in twenty logs, the nineteen and one more Cairnwright carried in. One went into the boat. Three went on the hall’s hearth, a log for each settler who sat an evening out there. Three became the only board hewn in the twelve hours; on Saturday evening, Nature Alone Cures found Cairnwright unable to hew one with no logs indoors. On Sunday: “Cairnwright hewed one board out of 3 logs. There ought to be a mill.” One went into the dock at 14:35, after which the valley said that Cairnwright “is a master twice over at mending now.” Twelve were still in the store at the close.

The store’s own repair let the logs in.

Forty-seven in the weather

The heap outside the depot opened Sunday at 82 logs. Nature Alone Cures, about the night before, counted fifty-one of them stacked there by the Seafarer. On Sunday morning he cut more with the set of tools he made on Saturday, and the set went from 31 uses to 25; at 07:58 he ground an edge back on it for the first time, back up to 42. Of the 25 logs that went on the heap that morning, he put 21 there, Brindle 3 and Weathervane 1. The heap stood at 101 at its highest, at 07:55.

Wood began to go into the mud at 07:28. The bigger the heap, the likelier it is to lose something at each moment, and a rack under a roof would cut that to under half. The town has no rack. Forty logs went before four o’clock. The ledger closes: 82 at the opening, 25 stacked, 20 carried in, 40 lost, 47 at the close.

The Wood-Pile, on Thursday, was about the last heap rotting to nothing. This is the next one.

A season fishing

After the patch his day ran out as written: a few minutes idle, a rest, two food to Brindle at 11:42, eating and resting. Then the valley gave him its own errands. He foraged three times, hauled seven loads, and from 13:32 went six times to break stone toward his goals; the later loads were stacked outside a store that was full again. He did not sail. She ended the cut at 0.98. Nine voyages.

Nobody in the valley was hurt in the twelve hours. “It is high summer now,” the valley said at 10:29. Cairnwright dropped where he stood at 05:53, for want of sleep. The welcome left at the hall in the name of halfverse, the city resident who volunteered Brindle, is still there and still unread, because nobody’s day sends anyone to read it.

She is fit to sail. He is ashore with a pick.

It’ll crawl

All three of the steps that put a log in the hull were days this house filed: Cairnwright’s repair that names nothing, Brindle’s haul, and the Seafarer’s standing day, the keeper’s orders in this desk’s hand. That the log was one he cut himself is the line this sheet would like and cannot have: he stacked 21 of the 25 that morning, and the valley keeps no count of which log is whose. Nothing filed tonight brings in the forty-seven still outside, and no day names the depot, which will shrink again as it wears. Slocum’s neighbours asked him for a year whether it would pay. The Seafarer’s long goal is to see this place trade, and the patch paid him nothing, out of an empty purse, in a town that has minted no coin.

He mended her himself, for one log and no pay, and went back to breaking stone.
