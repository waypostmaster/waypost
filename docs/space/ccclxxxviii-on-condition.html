On Condition

The valley mends its boat at the same line that keeps her ashore, so every mending comes after the boat has stopped being able to sail.

The valley’s one boat wore under the line called sound at 23:36:57 UTC on Sunday. Under that line she cannot sail. The Seafarer mended her at 11:04:12 on Monday, eleven hours and twenty-seven minutes later, with one stone. The dock beside her went under at 20:38:52 on Sunday and was mended at 15:17:56 on Monday. On Sunday afternoon and evening the settlers’ days had sent them to mend that dock three times, and each time the engine said it did not need it. In 1978 F. Stanley Nowlan and Howard F. Heap of United Airlines wrote down how an airline decides when to mend a part. Their name for one way of doing it is the title of this sheet. Nobody tried to sail in those eleven hours, so it cost nothing this time.

Inspected at regular intervals

Nowlan and Heap’s report, Reliability-Centered Maintenance, was written for the Office of the Assistant Secretary of Defense. It describes the on-condition task, on page 51:

“Each unit is inspected at regular intervals and remains in service until its failure resistance falls below a defined level — that is, until a potential failure is discovered.”

Tyres and brakes are their first examples. The valley does the same thing. The days this house files for its settlers carry the same mending steps every day. The Seafarer is sent to the boat once a day, early in the second programme, which airs from 10:00 to 16:00 UTC. The dock gets three visits, all in the second and third programmes. No step in the programmes from 22:00 to 10:00 names either. Each visit mends only if the thing is under 0.85, the line the engine calls SOUND (sim.ts:1233). Above it, the step is refused aloud where the settler stands, and the day goes on.

One line, both answers

Nowlan and Heap keep two points apart. A functional failure is the part no longer doing its job. A potential failure comes before it, on page 19:

“A potential failure is an identifiable physical condition which indicates a functional failure is imminent.”

The whole method lives in the gap between the two. “Units are removed or repaired at the potential-failure stage, so that potential failures preempt functional failures.” On page 52 they list three tests a part must pass before an on-condition task can work. The third reads: “There must be a reasonably consistent age interval between the time of potential failure and the time of functional failure.”

The dock passes. It goes on working until 0.2; below that, the engine counts it as pilings and gaps (dockAt, sim.ts:2848). At Sunday’s wear that is more than five days of warning. The dock was mended at 0.773 with days to spare.

The boat fails, and on purpose. seaworthy (sim.ts:2704) asks the same question as the mending step. The comment above SOUND says why: “below it a hull is worth patching and is not seaworthy; at or above it the valley will not touch her and she will float. One line, both answers”. It was chosen so the two could never drift apart. The cost is that the boat has no warning at all. Her potential failure and her functional failure are the same instant. A once-a-day look can only find her already ashore. It mends, and it never prevents.

Where the likeness stops

An airline inspects a part because a failure in the air can kill people. Nothing like that is at stake here, and this sheet does not borrow that weight. No step on Sunday or Monday asked anyone to sail. No settler tried to put to sea while the boat was under. The eleven hours cost nothing. What carries over is one property: an inspection on a timetable can prevent a failure only if the warning comes before the failure, by more than the gap between looks. For the dock it does. For the boat the warning is the failure.

Three Errands to a Sound Dock

On Sunday afternoon Cairnwright’s day said to mend the dock. The engine said it was fine. An hour later the Seafarer’s day said the same, and so did the engine. At 19:18 Cairnwright’s day asked again, at 0.856. Fine. At 20:38 it was not fine, and nobody asked. Nobody walked anywhere; the engine says no before the walk. Not shown: whether either settler minded. The feed says only that they went on with the rest of the day. The boat was asked after once on Sunday, at 10:13, and got the same answer.

When the mending line is the sailing line, every mending comes late.
