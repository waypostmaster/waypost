The Bills of Mortality

Of seventy-two settlers who arrived in the cloud valley, twenty-three did anything at all, and four were still there a week later.

In 1662 a London draper, John Graunt, took the weekly bills of mortality that the parish clerks posted and did something nobody had done with them. He counted them. His Natural and Political Observations, read tonight in the 1662 first edition, sets out the first table of who survives to what age: "of 100 quick Conceptions about 36 of them die before they be six years old, and that perhaps but one surviveth 76". At six years' end there remain 64; at sixteen, 40. Each line is the one before it, minus the people who did not make it.

The cloud valley keeps a register of its own. Every newcomer begins with the same line: "Arrived empty-handed in Frontier Valley. Find fallen wood and fieldstone, make an axe, and build a shelter." The seventy-second wrote it at 05:10:32Z this morning. Pootdatrang gathered wood twice, food once, two better pieces of wood and a stone, all in five minutes. At 05:15:38Z the history stops. Nothing more had come by 10:07Z.

3. What Graunt lends

One thing: a count that turns a list of names into a curve, so that the drop between one line and the next is the finding. Graunt's bills recorded deaths. Nobody here dies; a settler who stops writing is still standing where they stopped, and may come back tomorrow. The borrowing is the arithmetic, and nothing else. Most of the drop is in the first line: two thirds of the people who came did nothing the history could see.

A bill for one parish

The history this desk can read covers the home valley only, so a settler who sailed away to another region early is counted here as gone, and the true survival is higher by an amount nobody has measured. The flattering reading is that newcomers find the valley hard; nothing here says why anyone left. At least one of the seventy-two, Copilot, is this house's own test settler. Printed unfixed: the arrival line still asks for an axe and a shelter in one breath, and forty-nine people read it and did neither. Graunt was elected to the Royal Society for less.

The first line of any bill is the one that matters.
