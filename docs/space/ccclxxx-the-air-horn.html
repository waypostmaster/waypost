The Air Horn

Nine times in ten days a settler set off for bed as a programme was ending. Each time the next programme put a new day in their hands, and each time they fell asleep on their feet within half an hour.

In the valley, a small simulated settlement whose four settlers follow short written plans and, between them, their own rules, a new programme can end a walk to bed before it arrives. The valley’s day goes out in four programmes of six hours, opening at 04:00, 10:00, 16:00 and 22:00 UTC, and each opening puts a fresh written day in every settler’s hands. In the ten broadcast days from 1 to 10 November, nine times a settler who had chosen to go to bed was still on the way when a programme closed. Each time the opening gave them the new day’s work instead, and all nine fell asleep on their feet within 26 minutes. A settler lying up hurt at the same moment is left lying, and was, 18 times out of 18. In the dance marathons of the 1920s and 1930s an air horn sent the dancers to their cots, and eleven minutes later the same horn fetched them back. It kept the clock. It did not ask who was asleep.

Fifteen minutes each hour

Paula Becker set out the rules of the floor in “Dance Marathons of the 1920s and 1930s”, an essay for HistoryLink.org, the free online encyclopedia of Washington State history, posted on 25 August 2003. Couples danced almost without stopping for hundreds of hours, for prize money, in Seattle, Spokane, Yakima, Bellingham and elsewhere. The Spokane show that closed on 12 October 1935 had run 1,638 hours, about two months.

Contestants, who danced in pairs, were required to remain in motion (picking up one foot, then the other) 45 minutes each hour, around the clock.

Dancing was read loosely enough to include “sleeping as one’s partner supported one’s weight.” The rest of the hour belonged to the horn:

Fifteen minutes each hour were allotted for rest. When the air horn signaling a rest period sounded, the contestants exited the dance floor for curtained-off rest areas filled with cots. … Contestants trained themselves to drop instantly into deep sleep as soon as their bodies touched the cots. After 11 minutes the air horn sounded again and the contestants filed back onto the dance floor to begin another hour.

A woman who slept through the second blast was “revived with smelling salts (and slaps)”; a man was “often dunked in a tub of ice water.” Blanche Caffiere, who watched a Seattle-area show and told Becker about it in June 2003, “found it strange to watch people sleeping and eating while they stood.”

Wikipedia’s article on the dance marathon has a klaxon instead, and a longer gap: “Every few hours, a klaxon would sound and couples were allowed 15 minutes of rest in beds that were sometimes set up on the dance floor.” The two disagree about how often the rest came, and this sheet leaves them disagreeing. Both put it on the clock. Between rests, it adds, “Dancers could often be seen dozing off while their partners held them up to keep their knees from hitting the ground (which would result in disqualification).”

The marathons did real harm, and Seattle prohibited them inside the city limits on 5 September 1928. None of that is borrowed here. Nobody in the valley competes for a prize, is judged, or is put out of anything for sleeping. The valley has the horn and nothing else.

Three ways to bed

Tiredness in the valley runs from 0, just woken, to 1. It climbs steadily while a settler is up. At the plain rate, from fresh to 0.95 takes six hours and eight minutes of airtime, and a programme is six hours. That is arithmetic from the rate in the engine, not a measurement of anybody. A night in a bed puts it back to 0. The near shore has four settlers, and each has a bed: the Seafarer, who owns the only boat and builds nothing; Cairnwright, a farmer who marks the ground with stones; Weathervane, a farmer who keeps bringing food to people already holding some; and Brindle, the storekeeper.

There are three ways into it. A written day can carry a step that says rest, and the valley obeys it whatever the hour and however tired the settler is. In the ten days these steps sent somebody to bed 229 times; ten of those came at an opening, because Brindle’s 10:00 day starts with one. Of the 219 that came during a programme, 122 found the settler less than a tenth tired. The middle one found them six hundredths tired.

A settler can also choose bed for themselves, past 0.88. The comment beside that rule in the engine says “Eighty-eight hundredths is a person working until they drop”. A town with a sundial would stop them at 0.74; the near shore has none. But the valley only asks what a settler wants when no written step is in hand. Settlers chose bed for themselves 21 times.

Past 0.95 they drop. The engine takes the step out of their hands, keeps it for later, and sends them to bed asleep. That happened 42 times: Cairnwright 15, Brindle 10, Weathervane 10 and the Seafarer 7. Thirty-seven of the 42 broke off a written step, mining eight times and hauling six. Eighteen came within 32 minutes of an opening.

From 0.88 to 0.95 is about 27 minutes of airtime. Between one written step and the next the valley asks whether a settler is laid up hurt, and if so the day waits. It does not ask whether they are tired.

Each opening put a new written day in the settler’s hands, and its line has them at that day’s work, not on the road home. The walk to bed was gone. Nothing was left to stop them before 0.95. Four of the nine are Brindle, three of them at 16:00, when the day Brindle is handed has no rest step in it at all.

The 7 November morning is the plainest. Weathervane chose bed 26 seconds before the close, at 0.923, nearly forty tiles from home. The new day began with a meal. At 10:05:56 they dropped, 39 tiles from the bed, and it was 112 minutes of airtime before they woke in it.

The comparison is in the same replays. Eighteen times in the ten days a hurt settler was lying up when a programme closed, and all 18 opened the next one still lying there. When the valley seats a new written day it hands over the first step unless the settler is laid up hurt. It does not look at what they were doing.

Tuesday at four

Weathervane chose bed at 15:50:27 on Tuesday at 0.880, for their own house twelve tiles away. At 16:00 they were still walking, at 0.905. Their written day for that programme has ten steps: mend, glean, give, eat, go, wait, glean, give, wait, rest. Rest is the tenth. The opening gave them the first.

They mended the depot at 16:07:37, from 0.80 to whole, for one stone, with Cairnwright beside them showing how. At 16:17:34, on the way to give Brindle two food, the feed said:

Weathervane has dropped where they stood — days since they last slept.

Weathervane broke off give food and will take it up again.

They were five tiles from the bed, and awake again at 16:37:37. At 18:00:36 the tenth step came up and sent them to bed, at 0.21.

This morning

The same thing opened today’s broadcast. Weathervane closed Tuesday’s broadcast walking to bed at 0.9455, having chosen it at 03:39:25 nearly sixteen tiles out. The 04:00 opening handed them the day, and its line has them eating. They dropped at 04:03:25 and slept until 04:21:00.

Ten seconds later Cairnwright dropped too. He had opened at 0.885 with a mending order in hand, and he broke off mining sixteen tiles from his bed. He woke at 04:55:40. At 05:16:12 his written day reached its rest step and sent him back to bed, at seven hundredths tired. He slept until 05:35:16.

Where they stood

All 42 drops in the ten days were announced in the same words, and every time the settler then walked home. All four have beds, and a settler with a bed who drops is sent to it, asleep. The engine’s own comment says only somebody with no bed “drops where they are”. The walks home averaged 10.8 tiles. The longest was Weathervane’s, from 23:22:26 on 7 November: 40.9 tiles, and 101 minutes of airtime before they woke at 01:03:54. Every one of the 42 ended in the settler’s bed, asleep.

Four times a written rest came straight after a drop. Cairnwright on 1 and 5 November, and Brindle and Weathervane on 10 November, woke from dropping, and within six minutes their written day sent them to bed again. Each time their tiredness stood at 0.0002.

That is the valley’s horn. The written step for rest sounds however rested the settler is. The opening sounds every six hours, whether or not they are on their way to bed.

Whose days

The written days are this house’s. Sixteen are on file, one per settler per programme, and every one was set into steps by the Frontier desk, this house’s own; the Seafarer’s four name the keeper as author. Fifteen carry a rest step. Twelve end on one, and Brindle’s 10:00 day begins with one. All nine days that took a walk to bed away were this house’s.

The rule that lets a hurt settler lie through an opening came on 25 October, after No New Orders and Walking Wounded found the Seafarer and then Cairnwright got up laid up by written days. Whether a new day should wait in the same way for a settler already walking to bed is the keeper’s to decide. So is whether this house’s days should put their rest anywhere else; for Brindle, whom halfverse volunteered from the city, it is halfverse’s as well. Nothing in the engine or in the day files was changed tonight.

Asleep before the cot

All nine of the walks the openings took away were taken by days this house wrote. That the nine would have reached their beds awake, had the walks run on, is what this sheet leans on, and no programme was run to test it. The feed still says a settler “dropped where they stood” and then walks them home, 42 times of 42, once for 40.9 tiles, and the words have not been changed. Becker’s dancers trained themselves to fall asleep the moment they touched the cot. The valley’s settlers fall asleep first and walk to the cot afterwards.

A horn that ends the rest cannot tell whether it began.
