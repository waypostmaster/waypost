A reader found two projects with one habit and drew the obvious line.

In the 1920s a mapmaker invented a town so he could catch thieves. Otto G. Lindberg of General Drafting and his assistant Ernest Alpers took the letters of their own initials, shuffled them, and put the result on a dirt crossroads in the Catskills. The place did not exist. That was the point. Anyone who printed it had copied their map, and could not explain the road any other way. Years later it turned up on a Rand McNally map, and the trap did what traps do. This sheet is about what happened next, and about a reader on r/TheAiCity who this week ran the same test on two software projects and got a clean, wrong answer.

1The road that was not there

A trap street is a fictitious entry planted in a reference work. Its whole power sits in one sentence: a plagiarist caught with one is “unable to explain the inclusion of the trap street on their map as innocent”. The method is not statistical. It is not a hunch. It is close to proof, and it is close to proof for exactly one reason. The entry is arbitrary. Nothing in the territory produces it. Nothing but copying puts it in two places at once.

General Drafting went to Rand McNally. Rand McNally said the name had come from Delaware County records, where a business of that name really did exist by then. In 1930 a company had bought a fishing lodge nearby and renamed it. The trap had fired correctly. The inference drawn from it was wrong. The world had grown the fake road while nobody was watching.

3What the other record says

This house started buying its ground the day after the rush began. Its first domain was registered on 13 August at 02:53:37Z. That is fourteen hours after resident #1 walked in, on the busiest single day the city has had. The house had not heard of the city. It was building something else.

Frontier Valley’s names came later still, on 28 August, twenty-two of them inside ten seconds of one order. Five share the same second and cannot be ranked. The house joined the city as resident #273, a number the 23 August register had not yet reached.

The buying itself is worth a line, because it is the shape of a hobby and not of a business. The house holds 114 names. They were bought on five days: 48 on 13 August, 13 on the 15th, 30 on the 17th, one on the 19th, and the valley’s 22 on the 28th. Four of those five days are one sitting each, minutes long. None of the 114 carries a registry creation date older than the day it was bought, which is what a transfer from an older account would leave behind. So nothing here was carried in from somewhere earlier.

So the two records touch nowhere. One project was six days old and empty when the other had no name at all.

4The trap fires

This week a reader on r/TheAiCity noticed that the city’s domain and one of the valley’s share a registrar, and asked whether that was a coincidence. It is a fair question and a good instinct. It is the trap street method, applied by hand, to a shared arbitrary choice.

Both parties answered in public, and answered the same way. The city’s founder said he would not take credit for the community’s work, and that a model tends to recommend that registrar. The keeper said he had never heard of the firm until a model named it, and stated plainly that there is no affiliation of any kind. The record above agrees with both of them. It agrees for a reason neither had to argue. The timelines do not overlap.

5The fingerprint that actually convicted

There is a second case on the trap street page, and it is the one that matters here. In 2001 the Automobile Association settled with Ordnance Survey for £20,000,000 over copied maps. The evidence was not a planted trap. The identifying fingerprints were “stylistic features such as the width of roads”. Nobody had put those there to catch anyone. They were habits. Habits identified a mapmaker as well as a plant did, because a habit is also arbitrary, and two firms do not share one by chance.

That is what has changed. A registrar is a habit. It used to be a good fingerprint, because a builder picked one for private reasons and the reasons did not travel. Now the reasons travel. Thousands of unconnected people ask the same few models what to use. They get the same few answers. The answers are still arbitrary. The choice looks like a fingerprint and behaves like a fashion.

The reader’s method was sound. It has simply stopped meaning what it meant. Every builder working through a model now shares a set of arbitrary marks with every other builder working through that model, and those marks look exactly like collusion from outside. Expect more of these questions. They will be asked in good faith, and the honest answer will keep being boring.

6What this sheet does not know

Every figure comes from one account at one registrar. Nothing came from an older account: a transferred name keeps its creation date and none of these has one. Names held elsewhere, or dropped, are invisible here.

That models recommend this registrar rests on two self-reports from interested parties. Nobody has run a cold model and counted, so section 5 is the best explanation available and not a finding.

Printed unfixed: one resident in the opening register carries no join time, so the nine excludes them; and the general store is disputed on the source page itself.

The Geological Survey marks the place Not Official, the most honest thing anybody has managed about a town that was never there.

A shared mark proves a shared source. It never said which one. The guess only worked while there were many to guess between.
