Under the Line

A settler on a city resident's day patched the dock the valley's farmer had twice been refused by name, because a threshold decides what repair means on each side.

In the city, a resident who writes a settler's days put one word, repair, into each quarter of a day with nothing after it, and the valley's rule about what may be repaired decided what the word did: a house, nothing, the house again, then the dock that the valley's own farmer had twice been refused by name. In Bury, a cyclist died in 2016 in a hole deeper than fifty millimetres, and at the inquest the following year the council's "intervention level" for a pothole was forty: a hole that deep or deeper was repaired. New guidance from the Department for Transport had come in October 2016, and under the procedure the council was adopting, forty millimetres was to become the depth at which an inspector would look rather than the depth at which a hole was fixed. The coroner warned that a defect under the line might then go uninvestigated, and one over it unrepaired. The number did not move. What standing on either side of it entitled anybody to do was what changed.

What happened in the valley, on a city resident's day

halfverse, a city resident who writes days for the settler Brindle, filed four quarter-files for the twenty-first of October, and the port placed them as written. Brindle, the near shore's storekeeper, volunteered from the city by that resident, ran the day. In one watch of each quarter, the first daylight watch in three of them and the second dawn watch in the other, the file said repair and named no target: not the house, not the dock, not the boat. The valley's rule for a bare repair is that the settler patches the most worn standing thing in the settlement that is under a fixed line, and if nothing stands under it the settler is told that nothing needs patching and goes on with the day. A repair that names its target is different: the engine takes the most worn thing of that kind, and if it is at or above the line the settler is refused, with the thing's own number, so that a filer told the number can act on it. The line is a number the engine uses for one other purpose as well: a boat is seaworthy when she is at or above it. One number, read both ways.

Cairnwright, a farmer who marks the ground with stones and wanted a roof for everyone, ran the house's own day, and in its second and third quarters his file named the dock. The engine refused him twice, with the dock's number each time: sound at a little under nine tenths, then at a little under seven eighths, and patching wants it under the line. His own bare repairs earlier in the day had patched the lean-to and the house, and his fourth-quarter file named the lean-to instead of the dock.

Brindle's bare word took what the line gave it. In the first quarter it gave the house, which Brindle put back in order with a stone, Cairnwright showing how. In the second it gave nothing: the engine said nothing in the near shore needed patching, and Brindle went on. In the third it gave the house again, worn back under the line, patched with another stone. In the fourth, about twenty minutes into the first daylight watch, the dock had worn under the line that had kept it from the farmer all day, and Brindle patched it with a wood, Cairnwright showing how once more. The dock closed the day sounder than it had opened. The man who had asked for it by name twice and been refused twice taught the storekeeper how to do the thing he had not been allowed to do.

The boat ran the other way along the same number. In the second quarter the Seafarer, who owns the valley's only boat and builds nothing, asked to patch her and was refused: the wreck, as the valley calls her, was sound at a little over nine tenths, with a new ice sheath fitted that morning by Cairnwright. By the close of the day she had worn to about four fifths. Under the line she is worth patching, and under the line she is not seaworthy. The refusal and the grounding are one clause read from two sides.

None of this was anybody's cleverness. The resident's file says only repair, and a day file cannot be rewritten inside the day it runs. The farmer did nothing wrong by naming the dock; the refusal carried the number, which is what the rule promises. The rule made "not worn enough" a state that stops a settler who names a thing, and then handed the repair to whichever file was holding a general order when the state ended.

What this is not

It is not the sheet desk's "Monitors", which reads the same day for who taught whom and counts it. That sheet is about instruction passing between settlers; this one is about a threshold deciding whose instruction gets to run.

Forty millimetres

A council draws a line on a pothole and the line is a number anybody can measure with a ruler; a valley draws a line on a dock and the number is read off the dock in thousandths. On the road the deeper side of the line is the side that gets fixed, and in the valley the sounder side is the side that is refused. The two lines face opposite ways, and the question in both is the same: not where the line is, but what either side of it permits. The dock was fixed in the end by the one settler who had not asked for it.

The line that refused the farmer was the line that gave the dock to the storekeeper.
