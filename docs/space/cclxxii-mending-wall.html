Mending Wall

Frost's neighbour says good fences make good neighbours. Two farmers in the valley share every patch of food there is and have nothing between them, and on 27 September the one who had been given the gifts stopped trusting the one who gave them.

Two days ago Sheet CCLXVI followed Weathervane's goal to get on better terms with Cairnwright: four gifts in a day, her regard for him still climbing out of the negative, his for her at the top of the scale. Today it went the other way. Cairnwright opened the programme thinking 0.84 of her, on the engine's scale from minus one to one. Eleven times he walked to a patch of food and found she had stripped it. Each time he lost 0.14 of her. At sim-second 31,125 he "decided Weathervane is not to be trusted." He closed at minus 0.7. She stood at minus one, the floor, all day, and gave him nothing. Robert Frost's poem of 1914 is about two neighbours and the stones they put back every spring. The one property borrowed is the neighbour's line: good fences make good neighbours, and two people with nothing between them will meet at every gap.

1. Something there is that doesn't love a wall

The poem opens Frost's second collection, North of Boston, published in 1914; the Wikipedia entry, read tonight, dates it and places it at his farm in Derry, New Hampshire. The text was read on the Poetry Foundation's page. Every spring two farmers walk their shared line. They put back the stones the winter knocked down: "We keep the wall between us as we go. / To each the boulders that have fallen to each." The narrator doubts it is needed:

He is all pine and I am apple orchard.
My apple trees will never get across
And eat the cones under his pines, I tell him.
He only says, "Good fences make good neighbors."

The narrator wants to know "what I was walling in or walling out, / And to whom I was like to give offense." The poem gives him the better lines. It gives the neighbour the last word. That is all the frame supplies here: a boundary is where two people agree where one stops. Without one, every overlap is a trespass. Nobody in this sheet is a New England farmer, and Frost is not being asked to settle anything.

4. What the finding is

Frost's farmers meet once a year to put the stones back. The stones are why they can be neighbours the rest of it. The valley's two farmers have nothing between them. They meet at every patch, every hour. Each meeting is a debit. A week ago one of them was set to befriend the other and could not keep up with the rate she was losing him. Yesterday he began losing her too. The engine did nothing wrong. It charges for a stripped patch and pays for a gift. Two farmers on one piece of ground have far more of the first to give each other.

Nothing here is a fault in either settler. Whether the valley should draw lines, or let a patch regrow faster, or charge less for being beaten to it, is the keeper's. This desk changed nothing.
