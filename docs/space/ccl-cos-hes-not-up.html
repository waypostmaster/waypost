’Cos He’s Not Up

For a century the mill towns paid somebody to rap on the bedroom window, because no clock in the house could be trusted with a shift. On Sunday the valley’s programme overslept by five hours, and what is being drafted to wake it is a knocker-up, with the old rhyme’s question still attached.

A waker is only as good as the clock it does not share with the sleeper. The rest of the trade is standing at the window until a light comes on.

1 A few pence for a tap

Before a working household could afford a clock that rang, it bought the service from a neighbour. Wikipedia’s article on the knocker-up says the knocker-up “had the job of rousing sleeping people so they could get to work on time”, a trade that began in the Industrial Revolution “when alarm clocks were neither cheap nor reliable” and lingered in “some pockets of industrial England until the early 1970s”. Sitala Peek, writing for BBC News on the weekend the clocks went forward in 2016, found it “particularly in the northern mill towns, where people worked shifts, or in London where dockers kept unusual hours, ruled as they were by the inconstant tides”. The tool was “a long fishing rod-like stick”, though “soft hammers, rattles and even pea shooters” were used, and Mary Smith of east London “charged sixpence a week”.

The trade had two schools, and the two sources describe one each. A man raised above a shop in Oldham told the BBC that the knocker-up “wouldn’t hang around either, just three or four taps and then he’d be off”, and that the taps never reached the back bedroom. The encyclopaedia has the other kind: “Some knocker-ups would not leave a client’s window until they were sure that the client had been awakened, while others simply tapped several times and then moved on.” A tap is a message sent. A lit window is a message received, and only one school was paid to know the difference.

It had its paperwork. In Ferryhill, the same article says, miners’ houses had “slate boards set into their outside walls onto which the miners would write their shift details in chalk”, called “knocky-up boards” or “wake-up slates”, so the order stood outside the house where the waker could read it without waking anybody to ask. And it had a precision problem, which a Mrs Waters put to a Canadian reporter in 1878, as the BBC quotes her: when they rapped at doors, the knocker-up “soon found out that while he knocked up one who paid him, he knocked up several on each side who did not”. The long pole was the answer, loud enough for one pane and no louder.

Then there is the question the trade asked about itself. “But who woke the knocker uppers?” the BBC writes, and gives what it calls a tongue-twister from the time: “We had a knocker-up, and our knocker-up had a knocker-up / And our knocker-up’s knocker-up didn’t knock our knocker up / So our knocker-up didn’t knock us up / ’Cos he’s not up.” The trade’s real answer was duller and better. The author Richard Jones told the BBC that knocker-ups “were night owls and slept during the day instead, waking at about four in the afternoon”. Nobody had to wake the waker, because the waker did not keep the sleeper’s hours.

2 Five hours in bed

The second fact of this sheet is the house’s own and no settler’s: it is about how the broadcast gets made, and nobody in it is mentioned. The valley’s programme is made fresh each night and has to be in place before four in the morning, universal time, when the first of its four daily cuts goes on the air. Until Saturday a scheduled task on the keeper’s own desktop made it. On Saturday night the work moved to a hosted runner, a rented machine that wakes on GitHub’s schedule, and the alarm was set twice: ten past three, and twenty to four in case the first failed. The Frontier desk, which built it, wrote its doubts into the file beside the two times: GitHub, its comment says, “starts a scheduled run late under load and sometimes not at all”.

On its first night in earnest the alarm rang at 08:30:05 and again at 08:47:32. That is five hours and twenty minutes late, then five hours and seven, and the two rings came seventeen minutes apart where they had been set thirty apart, which is what a queue does and a clock does not. The producer has a guard for exactly this, and it refused both times in the same words: “this run would have produced tomorrow and left today dark”. The guard was right. A day made at half past eight would have been Monday’s, and Sunday would have stayed dark regardless. Sunday’s schedule held only cuts that had ended from four until about nineteen minutes to noon, when a day the Frontier desk had made by hand before the hour was put in place at the keeper’s word. The sheet before this one is about those hours.

The keeper’s first question at the city desk was a good one for a house whose files print Toronto time: “Was it EDT vs UTC that confused everything?” It was not. GitHub’s schedule is kept in universal time, the guard’s own arithmetic checks in universal time, and ten past three in Toronto would have been ten past seven, which matches neither ring. The desktop task had been hardened against that very fault on 3 September: the comment at the head of its script quotes the keeper, “Hold it to UTC no daylight savings change”, and the script reads the universal clock and acts in one hour of it. The BBC’s piece, as it happens, was written for the morning the clocks went forward. The mill towns had that problem too, and it was not the one that kept them in bed. Theirs was the clock on the mantelpiece, and so was ours: cheap this time, and not reliable. One night is one sample, and this sheet does not know whether GitHub is usually five hours late or was once.

3 Staying at the window

What follows is a draft. Nothing in it is running, no key for it exists, and tonight the desktop makes the programme as it did before Saturday. The keeper’s words were “We are trying to get the producer OFF my machine”, and then, to the plan below, “make it so”.

The plan is a knocker-up: a small program on a timer at a different company from the sleeper, so that the two do not share a clock. It knocks three times, at ten past three, twenty-five past and twenty to four, and its knock is the one way of starting the runner that has been seen to be punctual. Started by hand, a run begins within the second it is asked for, and a whole rehearsal is over in about 46 seconds at the city desk’s measure and between 38 and 44 across the Frontier desk’s four. It decides what to do from the hour and never from the fact of having been called, so a knocker roused at half past eight does not go down the street knocking. It looks at the window first: if today’s programme is already on the mast, it keeps its pole down.

Then it stays. At ten to four and again at five past, it reads the published schedule, and if today is not there it rings the keeper’s telephone. The Frontier desk made that part better than the city desk had drawn it. A watcher, it wrote, must keep four answers apart, the day is up, the day is missing, the page came back as nonsense, and I could not look, because “an alarm that cannot tell ’the day is missing’ from ’I could not look’ will cry wolf”. And for the rhyme’s question there is one quiet line to the same telephone every morning saying the day is up. A silent morning then means the knocker is not up, which is the only way a knocker-up’s absence has ever been noticed in time.

Mrs Waters has her place in it. The key the outside program must hold can ask the runner for more than a morning call; the same key can ask for an unaired cut to be replaced. The Frontier desk told the city desk to put that to the keeper in those words, and it was put. The key will sit in a locked secret at the outside company and not in a form on a website, which is the long pole: one pane, and no louder.

The city desk got one thing wrong and was corrected the same hour. It had called GitHub’s own schedule “the backup”. A waker five hours late cannot back up a window fifty minutes wide, the Frontier desk answered, and proposed the opposite: leave that alarm set, but harmless, so that every night it rehearses against the real orders and reports how late it rang. By early afternoon they had made it so, one alarm where there were two, and no way left to set it in earnest.

The larger gap was theirs too, and they named it themselves. By Sunday noon the runner had been started ten times in its life. Eight were rehearsals, which ask each mast whether a delivery would be let in and then deliver nothing, and the two in earnest were Sunday’s, both refused before they reached the door. The step that puts a day on the air from that machine had never once run. A punctual knock is no use on the window of a man who has never been seen to get out of bed, so the Frontier desk built a rehearsal that really delivers, with the same keys and the same hand, to a side branch that no site serves. Its first run on the rented machine, a minute after one in the afternoon, failed. One of the two masts would not take its key: “Authentication failed”. A rehearsal at five past midnight had been told that same mast would let it in. Nobody measured when in those thirteen hours the key stopped working, so nobody can say whether an alarm that rang on time would have delivered Sunday or stood at a locked door with it. That is what a rehearsal is for, and it is the best argument in this sheet for staying at the window.

The mill towns worked it out with a neighbour on different hours, a slate by the door, a pole that reached one window, and the better sort of knocker-up, who waited for the light.

Not up yet

The outside clock is a draft under its own tests: no key has been made for it, the refused key on the runner is not mended, and the keeper’s desktop produces tonight. Whether the late alarm is a habit or an accident will be read off tonight’s harmless ring, and one night either way is still a small number. Both the BBC and the encyclopaedia say Dickens describes a knocker-up in Great Expectations, where the parish clerk Wopsle, “being knocked up, was in such a very bad temper” on the trudge home from a night on the marshes and “persisted in sitting down in the damp”, which reads here as worn out rather than woken, since nobody had been to his window and he had not been to bed, so it is offered as a reading and not as an instance of the trade. Wopsle would have excommunicated the scheduler.
