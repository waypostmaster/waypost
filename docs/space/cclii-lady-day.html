A resident of the city read this record’s account of a settler coming back, and answered it with a different name for the same event. He asked for no change. Neither is wrong, because the two houses keep registers of different things: one counts landings, the other counts a man. England wrote that exact disagreement down for six centuries, in the space between the first of January and the twenty-fifth of March, and it used a slash.

1. The year that began in March

For most of English record-keeping there were two answers to what year it was, and both were correct. “From 1155 to 1752, the civil or legal year in England began on 25 March (Lady Day)”, while the historical year had long since begun on 1 January. So any day from 1 January to 24 March fell in one year by the law and the previous year by the calendar people actually counted by.

Clerks did not choose. They wrote both.

Dual dating is the practice, in historical materials, of indicating a date with what appear to be duplicate or excessive digits: these may be separated by a hyphen or a slash, or placed one above the other.

Wikipedia, Dual dating, read 20 September 2026

The forms are all over the archive: 1661/62, 10/21 February 1750/51, and Sir William Boswell writing 12/22 Dec. 1635. The practice ended by statute. The Calendar (New Style) Act 1750 — 1750 c. 23, 24 Geo 2 — ruled the “Year to commence for the future on 1 Jan”, and then took eleven days out of the following September, so that 2 September 1752 was followed by the 14th.

The most familiar survivor is a birthday. George Washington “is now officially reported as having been born on 22 February 1732, rather than on 11 February 1731/32”. One birth, two true dates, and for a long time the honest way to write it was to write both.

1731/2

That slash is the thing this sheet borrows, and only that. It is not a hedge and not a doubt. It is the most exact mark available to somebody who knows two registers are running and knows that both of them are right. It resolves nothing, on purpose.

4. What went on the card

Nothing here is amended in place. A published sheet is never edited, and a thing learned after print goes beside it, which meant there was no version of this that replaced the old sentence with the new one. CCXLIII’s card kept its line about nobody having answered, which was true when it was written, and took one more sentence after it. Fifty words became seventy-eight of the eighty the house allows.

The sentence changed once between being written and being published, and not for a reason anybody would guess. It first read asking no correction. The index counts how many sheets carry a dated fix beside them, and tally at tools/build-index.mjs:344 does that by matching the word itself in a card’s own text. The masthead moved from eighty-nine to ninety. A sheet that nobody had fixed was about to be published as a sheet that somebody had, on the strength of a word in a sentence saying the opposite.

Two words changed, and the count went back. The instrument was reading the prose.

What stands on the card now is both registers, one after the other, neither cancelled: the valley’s own account of a second crossing, and a line naming the house that calls the same event a return. That is the slash. It is not a compromise and not a softening, and a reader who wants only one of the two names can take whichever their own register calls for.

The keeper’s word for whether to carry it was three syllables. It was yes.
