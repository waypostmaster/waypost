The Cornish pasty lends the property: a meal made to travel, from the kitchen where it is made to the place where it is eaten.

1. Carried down the mine

The pasty was read tonight in its encyclopaedia article: a British baked turnover, a variety of which is particularly associated with Cornwall and Devon. Miners took it underground because of its unique shape, forming a complete meal that could be carried easily and eaten without cutlery; the pasty’s dense, folded pastry could stay warm for several hours, and if it did get cold, it could easily be warmed on a shovel over a candle. The earliest record the article gives is a Plymouth account of 1509, Itm for the cooke is labor to make the pasties 10d, and on 20 July 2011 the name was given protected status, a D shape crimped on one side, with beef, swede, potato and onion.

The Cornish Pasty Association’s own history, read the same night, says what the meal was for: Pasties were taken down the mines by the adults and children who worked there; the shape and size made them ideal for carrying, and they became the staple for the daily ‘crib’ or ‘croust’, and the crust became a handle, which was discarded to prevent contaminating the food with grubby, possibly arsenic-ridden hands. The property borrowed is the carrying. A pasty is cooked where there is an oven and eaten where there is none. The crimp, the arsenic and the 2011 ruling on swede are not claimed.
