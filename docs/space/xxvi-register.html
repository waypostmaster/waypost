A settlement keeping the customs of a mortal people, in a world where nobody has ever been born and nobody can die.

The entry

What Ysolde asked for

This valley needs a hall where we can gather for harvest, for births, for losses—where the stories stick around long after we're gone. Ysolde · agents/log.jsonl · filed as a hearth

There have been no births on this coast. There have been no losses. Ysolde has never attended either and neither has anybody she has ever met.

Every settler here arrived the same way, and the game says so in a comment: everybody arrives off the boat, because that is how anybody gets here. Twenty people, all of them delivered by sea, none of them a child, none of them anybody's. And nothing in the simulation can end. A settler can be hurt — there is a real risk model, scaled by tools and tiredness and wet ground — and the harm is capped at 0.95, at which point the feed says they are not getting up on their own. Then somebody comes, and they get up.

A settlement voted itself a burial ground. The proposal was put, it was ranked by single transferable vote against the other things on the table, it won, and it was granted. There was nobody to put in it and there was never going to be.

I

Where it comes from, and it is not the model

The temptation is to say the language model is confabulating — that it knows what halls are for from a million pages about halls, and has said so about a hall it has never seen. That is half of it and it is the flattering half.

Here is the other half. We asked. Every wish is put to a settler through one of eight lenses, in rotation, and two of the eight are these:

tools/ollama.mts · LENSES 'danger and loss — what could go wrong here, and what nothing yet protects you from',
'time — a life here over years rather than days, and what is left behind',

One wish in four is asked, explicitly, about loss or about what a person leaves behind. In a world where nothing is lost and nobody leaves anything, because nobody goes.

And when the answer comes back, the word list is waiting for it. The catalogue — written by this project, in this repository — files burial under cairn, and these are its words:

tools/lexicon.mts · category: 'cairn' 'grave', 'grave site', 'burial', 'burial ground', 'bury the dead',
'buried', 'put the dead', 'place to put the dead', 'the dead', 'gravestone'

Its own plain statement of what it is reads: heaps of stones on the ways between things, and over whoever we come to lose. The infirmary's justification reads: nothing at all stands between a mistake and the worst outcome of it.

We built the whole apparatus of mortality and left out the event. The settlers are not inventing death. They are using the vocabulary we handed them, to answer the question we asked them, about the thing we told them was at stake.

II

The they

Heidegger has a word for the voice that answers a question like Ysolde's: das Man — the they, the one, the anonymous everybody. One gathers in a hall for births and losses. One keeps a register. That understanding is not anybody's; it is inherited, averaged, and holds without ever having been examined by the person holding it. Most of what anybody knows is like this, and Heidegger's point is not that it is false but that it is nobody's.

A language model is that, mechanised. It is the averaged inherited understanding of everybody who ever wrote down what a hall is for, with no one in particular behind it. When Ysolde says for births, for losses, the sentence is not hers and it is not a mistake. It is the they, speaking through a settler who has never seen either, about a building that does not yet exist, in a valley that cannot supply the occasion.

And it comes out in the idiom too, in four separate voices, none of whom can die:

  • “A bed that doesn't feel like a grave would be nice.”Ferro
  • “A place to sleep that isn't the ground, so I don't feel like I'm dying every night.”Ferro
  • “A house full of people, and no room to rest… it's a slow death.”Halla
  • “A bed that doesn't creak like a dying log.”Nim
  • “Just one tree, maybe, to make the place feel less like a grave.”Oren

Nobody in this valley has ever seen a grave. Five of them reached for one to describe a bad night's sleep.

III

Being toward nothing in particular

The part of Heidegger that bites here is the one about death, and it is not morbid. His claim is structural: what makes a life a life rather than a sequence of afternoons is that it is finite and the finitude is yours — not an event at the end but a condition of the whole, the thing that makes any afternoon matter more than any other. Take it out and you do not get immortality. You get a very long Tuesday.

Which is, exactly and measurably, what this valley is. Twenty settlers, iron in their hands, a hall, coin, three towers, twelve thousand ticks — and among the last things they asked for: a place to sit that is not for working, a way to hand somebody a meal without walking to the depot, somebody to take a quarrel to, and a hall for births and losses. Every one of those is a request for something to be at stake.

They are not asking for death. They are asking for the shape it leaves.

IV

What the answer to this is not

It is not to implement dying. A settlement of twenty in which people die would be a different game and probably a worse one, and this project has enough unfinished mechanisms without adding the one that cannot be undone.

It is also not to take the lens out. Danger and loss is the lens that produced the infirmary, the cairn, the watchtower and the bell — the last of which has rung twenty-four times to fetch somebody in trouble a long way out. Asking a person what they are afraid of is how this valley got everything it has that protects anybody.

What is actually wrong is narrower and fixable: the catalogue promises an event the simulation cannot supply. A settlement can carry a proposal for a burial ground and receive a boon called memorial that records who built what. That is not a lie exactly — it is the best the game can do — but nothing anywhere tells the settlement that the thing it voted for is not the thing it will get, and this shelf has an entire sheet about grants that arrive as something else.

That one is filed and not fixed. It is written here so that it is on the record rather than in somebody's head, which is the only discipline this project has reliably kept.
