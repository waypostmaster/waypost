Boxgrove

Seven minutes and nineteen seconds. Three weeks ago the same man in the same fix was sent into the trees bare-handed, past forty-three wood on that heap, and a tree came down on him. The rule was changed on Tuesday, and this was its first use. The frame is the horse butchery site at Boxgrove in Sussex, where some 480,000 years ago people made their knives beside the carcass, from flint off the cliff behind them.

Ten or fifteen minutes

Boxgrove is a quarry in West Sussex. One corner of it is known as the Horse Butchery Site, which the archaeologist Matt Pope describes in his own account in The Conversation as “a spot where a large horse was slaughtered and processed some 480,000 years ago.” A tide covered it in silt before anything moved.

Pope’s part was the flint, “more than 1,750 pieces of knapped flint”, and his job was to fit the chips back together. He explains why that works: “Each stone flake removed by an ancient human will only fit, uniquely, to other flakes removed from the same block of flint immediately before and after it.” Put back together, the chips showed “the manufacture of eight large cutting tools” on the spot. He puts a time on it too. A knapper spent “perhaps ten or 15 minutes making each tool.”

The flint was near. Wikipedia’s article on the site puts the horse on a mudflat 40 to 60 metres from the chalk cliffs the flint came from. Even the hammers were to hand. Pope again: “Parts of the horse was also used as bone tools”.

That is the property this sheet borrows. Nobody at Boxgrove went home for a knife. The makings were within sixty metres and the making took a quarter of an hour.

Four minutes to two

Past the heap

It did not go this way before. Sheet CCCLXVII, A Hole in the Bucket, printed the Seafarer’s last time: tools gone at 20:30:46 on 4 November, sent to fell a tree at 20:35:02, hurt under it at 20:50:52. The store had no wood that evening either. The heap had forty-three. The rule read the store and his pack and never looked outside.

It happened again on Monday. Brindle’s stone tools gave out at 04:38:09 on 23 November. On air Brindle chopped for the makings from 05:05:24, was hurt at 05:48:10, and had a new set at 08:48:28. Four hours and ten minutes, and an injury.

The mend was written on 5 November by the keeper’s ChatGPT runner and landed at the keeper’s word at 03:12 UTC on Tuesday. A settler with no working tools now looks at the heap first and takes only what the set is short of (ownChoice, sim.ts:6260; the new branch is at sim.ts:6687). Tuesday gave it nothing to do. Nobody’s tools gave out. Wednesday did.

Where the likeness stops

The people at Boxgrove chose where to knap. The Seafarer chose nothing. He had no filed day in hand at those steps, so the heap was picked by the engine’s rule for a settler nobody has told what to do. This sheet does not say he was wise. It says the rule that walked him past forty-three wood now stops at eight. One man, one afternoon, one piece of wood.

Nothing to Refit

One of the two places the mend touched has now run on air, once. The other sits on a path the Seafarer did not take on Wednesday, and has not. The claim nobody can check is the flattering one: that Wednesday would have gone the way of 4 November under the old rule. He would have been sent to the trees. Whether a tree would have fallen on him is a roll that was never made. Printed unfixed: at 14:03:25 he went straight back to mining stone for a store that was full, with 154 stone already lying outside it. And Boxgrove kept its chips for 480,000 years while the knives walked off. Here the tools stay and there are no chips. A stone axe is two stones in and one axe out, which leaves Dr Pope nothing to fit together.

The heap was at the door both times. This time the rule looked.
