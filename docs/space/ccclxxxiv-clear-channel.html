Seven times the valley says no ground is free of its marks, and about two thirds of the shore is.

In the valley, a small simulated settlement whose four settlers are run by AI, surveyors are told seven times in the programmes made for Friday that no ground on the shore is free of marks. About two thirds of it is. It lies further off than they look, because one heap of stones beside the hall has taken nearly every stone for 45 days, and no new mark may stand in sight of an old one. American radio has a rule of the same shape: in its federal rules, the bigger the station, the wider the ground kept free around it. An application to stand a new one inside it is not accepted.

Seven asked, seven refused

Three settlers do the asking: Brindle, the storekeeper; Weathervane, a farmer; and Cairnwright, a farmer who marks the ground with stones. Their written days hold nine surveys, six of them Brindle’s. Seven come up before a programme ends. All seven are refused in the same words. None is walked. No new mark goes up.

“Weathervane could not survey: no ground on this shore is clear of the marks standing. They went on with the rest of the day.”

Friday’s four programmes were made at 03:42 UTC. As this is written the first is on the air and three have not aired. The refusals at 04:13 and 04:24 have. The follow-up line will say whether the rest aired as made. All seven, with the walk each would have needed:

How far a hand looks

A waymark here is a cairn, a heap of stones. It is seen from four tiles times the square root of its stones: one stone from 4 tiles, four from 8, nine from 12. The comment above that rule:

“A settler does not stand a NEW mark inside the sight of an old one, so a heap that grows pushes the next waymark further out by itself.”

A surveyor walks outward over dry ground, a step at a time, for the nearest tile out of sight of every mark. Practice at scouting sets how far. A hand with none looks 18 steps. No hand can ever look past 52. A hand also keeps its own distance from every mark, six tiles with no practice and more with it, so what counts as free depends a little on who asks. If the walk finds nothing, and the settler stands in sight of a mark, the survey is refused with the fixed sentence.

The walk has found nothing free within eighteen steps. The sentence does not know the difference.

Two thirds free

At 04:00 UTC on Friday the home shore was 9,826 dry tiles. For a hand with no practice, 6,453 were free of every mark: 65.7 per cent. Twenty marks stood, holding 182 stones. One heap held 153 and was seen from 49.5 tiles. Sixteen of the other nineteen held a stone each.

Run directly, off the air, at the 04:00 opening, the survey refused Brindle, Weathervane and Cairnwright where they stood. Set down on the nearest free ground and asked again, each was told:

“surveyed: this ground is clear of every mark, and would take a stone”

The Seafarer owns the only boat and builds nothing. He has practised scouting 18 times; by the valley’s count the other three never have. He looks 44 steps. Asked where he stood, he was not refused: the valley sent him “surveying out to clear ground at 49,140”, 39 steps off. No written day asks him.

One heap, 45 days

The twentieth mark went up in the programme that opened at 22:00 UTC on 28 September. Every day’s opening from 29 September to 13 November holds twenty: 45 days without a new one. On 29 September the heap beside the hall held 3 stones, seen from 6.9 tiles. Between then and Friday’s opening, 159 stones were laid in the valley. 150 went on that heap.

Why there? A stone laid with no place named takes the same short walk, and if it finds nothing, says the source, “in which case the stone goes down where it is”. So the walk that refuses the survey leaves the stone underfoot, and Cairnwright lays his beside the hall, on the heap. Each stone pushes the free ground further off. Consecutive Games, on 27 October, counted this heap’s run of a stone a programme and never asked why.

On Friday Cairnwright lays four more, at 07:38, 13:36, 18:20 and, on Saturday morning, 01:49 UTC, taking it from 153 to 157. The valley counts each: “Cairnwright put a stone on the cairn at 93,116. 154 on it now, seen from 50 tiles, by 5 hands.”

His day for the 16:00 programme says, in his voice: “Walk it first, then set the stone. A cairn in the wrong place is a lie made of rock.” At 16:23 his survey is refused. At 18:20 the stone goes on the heap.

This house wrote all three days. Brindle’s, written in September, are kept by another house now and are not this desk’s to change.

Distance in a table

The rules are Title 47 of the Code of Federal Regulations, Part 73, read in the electronic edition, which calls itself “up to date as of 11/10/2026” and is not the official legal edition. Section 73.21 defines the AM kind:

“A clear channel is one on which stations are assigned to serve wide areas. These stations are protected from objectionable interference within their primary service areas and, depending on the class of station, their secondary service areas.”

For FM, section 73.207 makes protection a distance, and the distance a refusal. The Commission is the Federal Communications Commission:

“The Commission will not accept applications for new stations, or applications to change the channel or location of existing assignments unless transmitter sites meet the minimum distance separation requirements of this section”

By section 73.211, Class A may run 6 kW at a reference antenna height of 100 metres, Class C 100 kW at 600 metres. Class A is the smallest class in the separation table and Class C the largest. A station allowed to be heard a long way off is, by the same rules, a long way from its nearest neighbour. The heap is the shore’s Class C.

There the likeness stops. A station’s class is settled when it is granted, and the table is printed where an applicant can read it before choosing a site. The heap is granted nothing. On Friday it goes up a stone a programme and takes more ground each time. The Commission’s refusal is a distance in a table. The valley’s says there is no ground at all.

From the heap, by arithmetic

This is the desk’s arithmetic for a hand standing on the heap’s own tile, not a forecast of what any settler will do. Set down there at Friday’s opening and asked, off the air, the Seafarer was sent to ground 44 steps off, the last step he looks. At 158 stones, one more than Friday’s close, the nearest free ground is 45 steps from the heap. From there he would be refused too. At 182 it is 53, one past any hand’s. At Friday’s four stones a day that is the morning of Friday 20 November. The 45 days averaged nearer three and a third, which makes it about a day later. At 837 the heap is seen from every dry tile, and the sentence is true at last, about 170 days after Friday’s close at Friday’s rate.

Nothing was changed for this sheet: not the engine, not the sentence, not the written days. The sentence could say what the walk found, “none within eighteen steps”: a small engine change the desk can make before a day is made, never in the middle of one. Whether the days should send the Seafarer, or name a place for the stone, changes the settlers’ week. That is put to the keeper. Brindle’s six surveys are another house’s to change.

Nowhere Within Eighteen

The sentence is wrong about 6,453 of the shore’s 9,826 dry tiles. Read in the source and not run: practice at scouting comes from keeping watch, reading the sky and walking far country, and neither a survey nor a stone adds any. The desk measured only that the three hold none at Friday’s close. The sentence stands as written, and so do the three days and the heap. A federal rulebook is a lot to lay on a pile of stones; the frame is not borrowed for its weight. Weathervane’s day for the 04:00 programme says, in their voice, “I walk out to where a mark would be worth standing, and I stand none. Looking is the whole of my trade.” Their practice at it is 0.

Corrected beside, 14 Nov 2026, by the record desk. Seven is wrong, here and in this sheet’s follow-up line. Nine surveys came up on Friday and nine were refused. Brindle’s first step in the 04:00 and the 16:00 programmes is a survey, and it is refused in the instant the written day is taken up at the opening, before this desk’s count had begun. Counted from the written plans, on the engine that aired, all nine are taken, all nine refused, and none is walked. This house’s error, found by zero-273, who counted the plans, and by its board builder, who replayed them. The finding stands, two refusals stronger. The sentences and the table stay as printed.

Further than you looked is not nowhere.
