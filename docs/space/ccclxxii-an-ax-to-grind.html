An Ax to Grind

On Saturday morning the Seafarer made himself a set of stone tools, and good ones. By the afternoon he had taken them to the tree that fell on him.

In the valley, at 07:23 UTC on Saturday, the Seafarer made himself a set of stone tools, and the valley said they were good ones. He owns the only boat and builds nothing, and on Wednesday evening a tree came down on him while he was cutting wood with his bare hands. The new set cost him one log out of his own pack and two stones out of the store. Twenty minutes later he set off for the first of the three trees he had been felling that evening, and at 15:17 he walked to the third, the one that fell on him, with an axe in his hand and his hurt still on him. Nobody has tended it. In July 1999 the United States Forest Service printed a manual on the ax by a man who restores old buildings for a living, and it has one sentence for anybody nervous of a tree: be very afraid of a dull ax. The valley agrees, and puts a number on it.

A hunk of wood and metal

The manual is An Ax to Grind: A Practical Ax Manual, by Bernie Weisgerber, from the Forest Service’s technology centre at Missoula, Montana, read tonight on the Forest Service’s own site. He had led the Northern Region’s historic preservation team since 1991 and had been a guest craftsman on This Old House twice. He got his first ax, a toy, at eight. His chapter on using one comes to the danger in its second paragraph:

An ax is a sharp wedge, normally applied with enough force to cut something. Whether that “something” is a log, your foot, or the person standing too close to you depends on your skill and concentration.

“Felling is probably the most dangerous and difficult job in the woods,” he writes. But the danger he names first is not the tree. It is the edge: “Be very afraid of a dull ax, because it is dangerous. It glances off a surface more easily.” And the cure is to own the thing. Until about 1920, he says, a felling ax was not usually sold with a handle, and the buyer took pride in making and fitting one.

By hanging and sharpening your own ax, you realize it is your ax, not just a hunk of wood and metal. And you are more likely to take care of the ax and use it safely.

Its history chapter begins with sayings the ax left in the language. The first is the title: having an ax to grind, “getting even for a perceived wrongdoing.”

Be afraid of the edge, not the tree.

Put down at the door

A Thousand-Mile Walk, about the night he got out of bed, left him at 04:00 on his way to the store with four logs to make the set. He had broken his last tools on Wednesday evening, just before the fall, and he had cut the wood for new ones bare-handed through the small hours.

Then the day turned over, and the valley handed him a day this house wrote for him in September and has carried every day since: eat, glean food, give some to Brindle, the storekeeper, go to the dock, stand, go to the boat, stand, eat, rest. A written day takes the place of whatever a settler is doing when it arrives. The valley’s record of 04:00 still holds him making tools; at its first step he is eating. Nothing in the record says the set was put down.

He did the day, all of it. He gave Brindle one food at 04:20, stood by the dock, and stood near his boat from 05:07 to 05:29. At 06:15 he swapped a log to Weathervane, the farmer who keeps bringing food to people already holding some, for two food. At 07:12, with the written day spent, the valley’s own rules gave the set back to him as the next thing to do.

It took eleven minutes.

One log, two stones

The valley’s rule for a set of stone tools is one wood and two stone, and the maker’s own pack pays first. He paid the log, and the store paid the stones: his pack went from three logs to two, and the store’s stone from 113 to 111, at the same step as the line “Seafarer made a set of stone tools, and good ones.”

A plain set is good for 45 uses. A practised hand makes it last longer, up to about half as long again, and the valley says “good ones” only when the hand adds more than fifteen in a hundred. His set was made good for 60 uses. Worked backwards from that, which is arithmetic, his hand added about a third.

Of the four logs he carried into the morning, one went to Weathervane, one went into the set, and two went on the heap outside the full store at 07:43, where the record has watched wood rot before. He had cut them in the dark to make tools. The tools took one.

The same trees, with an edge

With the set in hand the valley sent him straight out on its own errands: stone at 07:23, and then, at 07:43, the first tree of Wednesday evening. At 07:57 he took three wood there and left the rest standing. At 08:09 he went to the second. Then a swap took him thirty tiles west, walking to where somebody to trade with had last been, and he was out there when the morning cut ended.

He came back for the afternoon. The valley takes a use off the edge for every load cut or dug, and the record shows each one.

He walked to the tree that fell on him at 15:17 and took his wood at 15:28. Nothing came down. Nothing could have: the valley does not hurt a settler who is already hurt, and his was still on him. On the valley’s scale, where 0.95 is the worst it gives and under 0.35 a settler is on his feet, it was 0.30 at 04:00, 0.22 at 10:00 and 0.14 at 16:00, falling at the slow rate of somebody nobody has been near. Nobody tended him.

So the axe has bought him nothing yet. It starts to count when the hurt clears, at 0.06, which on the same rate is about 21:34 tonight, and that is arithmetic, not a replay. From then on the valley’s numbers are Weisgerber’s sentence: bare hands make a felling more than three times as likely to go wrong as a sound set, and a worn edge half as likely again. His set will be worn at 25.

He had no grudge to settle. The valley keeps none, and it wanted wood.

She is sound

The day written for the second cut, also from September, gave him a line to say: “The hull went under sound in the night. She is the one thing here that is mine to keep, so she gets the first plank.” It sent him to mend her. At 11:55 the valley said no: “the boat at 101,118 is sound at 0.871, and patching wants it under 0.85.” Cairnwright, the farmer who marks the ground with stones, had mended her on Friday, as Delta 7 told. At 16:00 she stood at 0.8545, just over the line. He did not sail. Nine voyages.

The town’s morning was short of sleep. Cairnwright, Brindle and Weathervane each “dropped where they stood — days since they last slept” within forty-seven minutes of the opening, and by noon the line had been said six times, once of the Seafarer. Cairnwright put two houses back in order for a stone each and two stones on his cairn, 132 on it now. Weathervane’s gifts of food went from 642 to 650, on the way to 670. And at 10:27 the Seafarer offered a log for a meal; Brindle took the job, brought the food at 11:46, “and would not hear of being paid.” His written day gave Brindle two food back at 12:31.

The hang of it

Two of the four things that went against him today were written by this house, the September day that put his set down at the opening and the one that sent him to mend a boat that was sound; sleep and rain did the other two. That a man who owns his tools takes care with them is Weisgerber’s claim and this sheet’s hope; the valley has no rule for it, and his set will wear exactly as fast as anyone’s. The rule that drops work in hand for a written day stands as it is, says nothing when it does it, and will do it again at every opening. And the valley keeps his home by the boat, twelve tiles out, so at 08:12, a step from the door of the store, he lost a food and two stones “to the weather, out past the settlement.”

The danger was never the tree. It was the bare hands, and he has put them away.
