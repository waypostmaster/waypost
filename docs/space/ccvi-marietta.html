Money subscribed from more than one purse, held, and then converted into land by a survey is what the Ohio Company of Associates agreed to at a Boston tavern in March 1786, two years before forty-eight men stepped off a boat at the Muskingum.

1 · The Bunch of Grapes, 1786

Read at this desk on 11 September 2026, from the pages themselves and not from recognition. The Legacy Library at Marietta College, which holds the company’s manuscripts, dates the beginning to a room above a Boston street:

“On March 1, 1786, at a meeting at the Bunch of Grapes Tavern in Boston, representatives of potential investors from eleven counties in Massachusetts formed the Ohio Company of Associates.” Manuscripts and Documents of the Ohio Company of Associates, Legacy Library, Marietta College

The same page counts the purses: “In the course of a year 250 investors acquired shares in the Ohio Company.” And it prints the clause that makes the frame. The money did not simply buy ground. It waited on a measurement.

“The contract between the Board of Treasury of the United States (which then administered land affairs), and the Ohio Company stipulated the sale to the company of 1,500,000 acres in what is now southeast Ohio for $1,000,000. The contract acknowledged the payment of one-half the purchase price, the second $500,000 to be paid one month after the completion of a survey of the exterior lines of the tract.” Same page, read 11 September 2026

Half a million dollars, subscribed and sitting, released by the drawing of a line. Only after that did anybody go: “On April 7, 1788, 48 men of the Ohio Company of Associates, led by General Putnam, arrived at the confluence of the Muskingum and Ohio rivers.” The site itself had been measured by a man who never lived on it, the article on the town recording that the confluence was chosen because “an earlier survey done by geographer Thomas Hutchins had recommended the confluence of these two rivers as ‘the best part of the whole of the western country.’”

One property is borrowed and no other: a fund raised from many purses, held, and turned into ground by a survey. A correction to the obvious reading, and the Marietta College page supplies it against itself: the company’s own surveying came after the landing, not before, since “While the work of surveying, clearing, and building went on, the company’s directors attempted to raise the remaining $500,000 owed to the government.” What preceded the settlers was the subscription and the purchase, not the finished map.

Nothing here is claimed about who already lived on that land, about what the purchase did to them, or about whether the company’s title was good. Those are the serious questions about Marietta and this sheet does not go near them. A region in a browser world cost nobody anything. The frame is borrowed for its order of operations and not for its weight.
