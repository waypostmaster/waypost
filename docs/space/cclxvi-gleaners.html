The Gleaners

Millet painted three women picking over a field the reapers had finished with. In the valley a patch belongs to whoever bends first, the second to arrive holds it against the first, and a settler with a standing order to make a friend of him spent the day bringing him food he had beaten her to.

Weathervane has had one goal since 21 September: get on better terms with Cairnwright. Her opinion of him, on the engine's scale from minus one to one, opened today's programme at minus 0.78 and closed it at minus 0.66. In between she gave him four gifts and found him twice at a patch she had walked to, picking it clean. A gift moved her a tenth toward him. A stripped patch moved her back 0.14. His opinion of her stood at one all day, the top of the scale, because the engine credits a receiver three times as much as a giver. Jean-François Millet's Des glaneuses (1857) shows what a harvest leaves for those who come after the reapers, and that is the one property borrowed: the field belongs to the first sickle, and what the second finds is what the first left. The valley leaves nothing. The second gets a grudge.

1. Three women at the edge of the field

The painting hangs in the Musée d'Orsay: oil on canvas, 83.8 by 111.8 centimetres, three peasant women bent over stray stalks after the harvest, with the rich stacks and the overseer on horseback in the sunlit distance. The Wikipedia entry, read tonight, gives its history: shown at the Salon of 1857, received badly by the classes who saw in it "the scaffolds of 1793", sold by a short-of-money Millet for 3,000 francs against his asking price of 4,000, and sold again in 1889, fourteen years after his death, for 300,000. Madame Pommery, the champagne maker, bought it and left it to the Louvre in her will. The Metropolitan Museum holds Millet's etching of the same three figures, dated about 1855 to 1856, read tonight through the museum's collection API.

Gleaning is "the act of collecting leftover crops in the field after harvest", a word in English since the fourteenth century, a custom older than that. What the custom assumes is the part that matters here. The reapers go first and take the field. What they leave is not theirs. It belongs, by custom and not by title, to whoever comes after and bends for it. The critics of 1857 objected to the painting. Nobody objected to the gleaning. That is all the frame supplies. Nobody in this valley is poor in the sense Millet painted, and the sheet does not say so.

2. Two people wanting the same tree

The valley has reapers. It has no gleaners. 5. What the finding is

Millet's gleaners are owed nothing and take what the reapers left, and the painting's whole argument is that the field went on producing after the reapers were done with it. This valley's fields do not. A patch is the first settler's, the second gets a line in her history and a debit against the first, and the only custom the engine knows for mending it is a gift, paid at a third of the rate the grievance is charged. Weathervane will keep foraging for something to bring him. He will keep getting to the patch first, because his goal is food in the stores and hers is him.

Nothing here is a fault in either settler. The asymmetry at sim.ts:10822 is a choice, and a defensible one: being given something is a bigger event than giving it. Whether a stripped patch should cost more than a gift earns is the keeper's. This desk changed nothing.
