Fram

The ice came to the near shore at 14:45:11Z, on the second the calendar gave. The boat met it plain. Her sheath needed eight boards, and the store never held more than six.

The near shore's bay iced in on time, and the boat had not been fitted for it. An ice ship is fitted in the yard and not on the floe. Fram was launched from Colin Archer's yard at Larvik on 26 October 1892. Her hull was hardwood "60cm thick", as Baird Maritime records it. She had "an outer layer of greenheart wood to withstand the ice", in the words of the encyclopaedia, because Nansen's plan was to let the pack freeze around her. Every board of that was on her before she sailed. The valley gave its crew the same order of work and two days' warning. The boards went on the roofs instead.

3. The logs were there

Nineteen logs sat in the store all day, untouched. A board can be hewn by hand from three of them (case 'hew', sim.ts:9736). Four more boards would have cost twelve logs, which leaves seven against the sheath's four. Every material the sheath needed was on the near shore. Nobody hewed, and there is not one hew line in the day.

The reason is this desk's own rule. The crew fits the sheath unasked only when the store already holds every board, the test at sim.ts:6863. Nothing in it tells anyone to go and make them. The rule was written on 12 October and waits for boards to arrive. On this shore boards do not arrive. Somebody makes them.

4. Nobody asked the sea

The Seafarer's filed day opened the second cut with "The hull went under sound in the night", and asked for a patch. The engine refused it by name: the boat was sound at 0.915. By the end of the day she was at 0.824. She now wants a patch before she can sail, and a sheath before she can sail in winter. The thaw on this calendar comes at about valley day 255.3, three valley days after this one ended.

Greenheart on order

The one fault in this sheet is this house's, written the day before: a rule that waits for boards instead of making them. The flattering claim is that the crew "could" have hewn them. No settler here has ever hewn a board for a purpose two days away, and nothing says they would. Printed unfixed: the unasked fitting still waits for eight boards in the store, and nobody makes them. The replay ended on the produced world exactly, so the hours above are the engine's, not this desk's arithmetic. Nansen fitted his ship for three years in the ice. This crew had two days and spent them on a lean-to.

The ice keeps its appointments. The boards have to be made.
