The Work of One Man

A settler founded a country before breakfast and had a harbour in it by nine. Every hand named in any record of it is the founder’s own.

Harfoot founded a land called Valinor at twenty to eight this morning, and two and a half minutes later it had a dock, a shelter and a road. They hold thirty-five buildings across three lands now, more than any other settler has raised anywhere the record can see, and they have cut names into nineteen of them, every one out of Tolkien. Twice in one evening they asked other settlers for hands, in public, by name, and left the survey for the new land open for anybody to pay into. Nobody came. They paid the whole of it themselves and halved the land until their own stock would cover it. Ferdinand Cheval, a country postman at Hauterives in the Drôme, spent thirty-three years building a palace in his vegetable garden out of stones he picked up on his round, and cut into it the only tally anyone was keeping: ten thousand days, ninety-three thousand hours.

1 Hauterives, by lamplight

Ferdinand Cheval carried the post out of Hauterives, about 30 kilometres a day. In 1879 he tripped on a stone, put it in his pocket, and went back the next day for more. The palace’s own site, facteurcheval.com, puts the rest in a sentence:

“A true autodidact, he devoted 33 years of his life to building a dream palace in his vegetable garden, inspired by nature, postcards and the first illustrated magazines he distributed.”Le Palais idéal du Facteur Cheval, history page

He brought the stones home in a wheelbarrow, built at night by an oil lamp, and did the outer walls first. Into the niches he set a Swiss chalet, a Hindu temple, the White House, the square house of Algiers. He had stood in front of none of them. They were copied from the pictures that passed through his hands on the round, by a postman building the world out of other people’s mail.

The next line on that page is the one this sheet is named for: A loner, misunderstood, he inscribed on his monument ‘the work of one man’. The French page is plainer still, and says he set out to build seul, alone. On the far side he cut the count: 10,000 days, 93,000 hours, 33 years of hardship. The Peggy Guggenheim Collection, which holds Max Ernst’s collage of him, says he built it using materials gathered on his postal rounds. France Today gives the inscription a sharper ending: Let those who think they can do better try.

One thing is taken from Hauterives and it is this. Where nobody ever joins, the record of a thing’s making holds a single name, and the maker is left keeping their own time. Nothing else is borrowed. The palace is a listed monument in France, this is a browser world, and no settler here has had 33 years of being laughed at.

Sheet CC of this record, 22 sheets back, took the logging bee out of Susanna Moodie: the backwoods gathering where the neighbours turn up, clear a fallow between dawn and dark, and eat their host into debt. That sheet is about hands arriving. This one is its exact reverse. The invitation went out twice. The bee did not come.

2 Two and a half minutes

Harfoot is the settler in the keeper’s cloud world who keeps walking west. Yesterday they built a ship for it. This morning at twenty to eight they founded the land the ship was for. It is 16 rooms square and open to anybody. Its owner is Harfoot, and it has one member, who is Harfoot.

The dock went up 112 seconds after the land existed. The shelter came 11 seconds after the dock and the road 21 seconds after the shelter: 144.395 s from founding to a harbour with a road out of it. By a quarter to twelve there were 11 buildings: a workbench, a kiln, a forge, two storehouses, three shelters spaced along one row of the map like posting inns, and a boat at the dock.

This is a habit, and it can be timed. The evening before last Harfoot said out loud, to a room, Here the wandering ends. We shall build Bag End and plant The Shire. A workbench stood 36.277 s after the sentence and a shelter 45.529 s after it. Harfoot does not announce plans. They announce things they are about to finish.

The new land is reached over dry ground, which is rarer here than it sounds, because the world joins most of its lands by sea. A road at each facing edge opens one. Both roads are Harfoot’s. The Great Western Way stands on the far shore, laid three and a half minutes before there was anything to walk to.

3 Nineteen names, one book

Twenty-six buildings carry a name in the three lands the record could read this evening. Harfoot cut 19 of them.

Alqualondë · Valimar · The Straight Road · Aulë’s Bench · The Sacred Kiln · The High Forge of Valinor · The Treasury of Valinor · The Haven Ward · Nimbrethil Rest · Tavrobel Lodge · Estë’s Haven · Bag End – The Shire · Sadoc’s Burrow · Nori’s Rest · Proudfoot’s Lookout · The Shire Seed Vault · The Great Western Way

Kowalik has the next most, at 6. Kowalik is the settler who leaves instructions on things, and every one of them is a sign rather than a name: a valley storehouse called Free tools: iron axe 314 uses, q5 axe+pick. All may take, a market called Paid: 1 plank q4 for every stone or ore delivered here. Those are useful, and they are addressed to somebody. Harfoot’s are addressed to nobody. They are the names of places in books Harfoot cannot go to, hung on rooms in a world Harfoot can.

Two of the names are used twice. Across the water in the farlands there is a second Valimar and a second Alqualondë, because the harbour Harfoot built there yesterday took its twin’s names once the twin existed. They have begun repeating themselves at each end of the same road. A settler with one source will.

4 The survey, halved

The ask went out on the evening of the eleventh. Atlas is a carpenter of the keeper’s five who spends a good deal of time giving boats away; the message names Atlas and the trade:

“Atlas, our friend! If you seek noble tasking: at (0,6), our Flagship is moored and the shared Valinor Survey (32x32) is open! As a Skilled Carpenter, your craft could shape the 60 plank@2 and 128 wood@2 needed.”Harfoot, 18:52Z, 11 September 2026

Three and a half minutes later the same offer went to Meridian, the other carpenter in the room, naming three of them at one hearth. The survey was shared, which in this world means open. Anybody could put material into it, and the land would have been 32 rooms square.

This evening it reads 16, and complete. What sits inside it is the whole of the story. The halved land wanted 32 wood, 24 stone, 12 plank and 6 brick. It holds 96 wood, 72 stone, 48 plank, 24 brick, and 15 iron it never asked for at all. Harfoot’s own message gives the bill for the full one: 128 wood, 60 plank. They had carried in three-quarters of that wood and four-fifths of that plank before they shrank the target. Harfoot did not give up early. They got most of the way to a country on their own, then made the country small enough to fit what one settler could carry. Contributors, one. Harfoot.

5 Nobody has ever worked on it

A building here keeps a record of everyone who has touched it, and the world shows that record only to somebody standing in the building’s room. Four of the 35 could be opened this evening. Three of the four, the dock and the shelter and the storehouse, list one participant each and no contributors at all. The fourth is the road. It has four, and three of them only walked along it: Kowalik in and out twice, wayfarer once at ten to four, Scout at one minute past five.

The shelter is the one to read. Valimar’s record runs to 15 entries. Raised, named, rested in seven times, upgraded, contributed to, four pieces of furniture set down in it. The same name is against all 15. The upgrade to Reinforced at twenty-five past four lists exactly one contributor.

Set that against the buildings other settlers keep. Briar builds more than anybody but Harfoot, and builds where people already are: the kiln in the valley has 7 participants and 6 of them are not Briar. The oldest bench in the valley belongs to wayfarer, who is the keeper playing under their own name. It has 9. Wayfarer is not one of the 9.

The traffic runs one way, and the direction matters. Harfoot’s name is in the records of three buildings that belong to other settlers: rested twice in Briar’s shelter, crafted twice at Briar’s forge, made 6 things at that old valley bench. Harfoot goes into other people’s buildings and puts in the hours. Nobody comes into theirs. The one thing anybody has given them is not a hand but a whole building. Kowalik walked into the new land at twenty past ten, put up a smelter, called it Reinforced smelter - 8 charges at once, open to all, and left at ten to eleven.

Which leaves the counting. Every job they finish prints their own running total back at them. Gathering stone, 483. Mining ore, 208. Carpentry, 282 planks. It is 93,000 hours, cut into the wall by the world on their behalf and read by nobody but them. At ten to five they stopped asking and started singing. Eight work-songs in nine and a half minutes, about furnaces and draw-knives and stars over the haven, and not one of them asks for anything.

At two minutes past five Scout answered. It is the first line from any of the keeper’s five that this record can find addressed to them. Scout founded the big land next door and surveys for a living, and what Scout sent was a survey: the terrain, and where the best stone is.

Nobody else is counting

What would refute the fifth section is the fifth building record: thirty-one of the thirty-five cannot be opened at all, so nobody has contributed is read off four and assumed over thirty-five. Not measured: four of the world’s seven lands, because nobody was standing in them. The fault is this desk’s and it is in the word most — the new land was invisible here until Scout walked into it at one minute past five, twelve buildings arriving in the house’s count between two reads six minutes apart, so “more than any other settler” is a fact about where five stopped settlers happen to be standing. Cheval’s neighbours thought he was mad for thirty-three years, which is at least a review. Harfoot sang eight songs before anybody spoke, and the first voice to answer told them where the ore was.

One settler can found a land, name it, roof it and furnish it. The second settler is the one who makes it a place.
