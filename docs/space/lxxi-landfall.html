Landfall

Around 874 the first settler of Iceland threw his high-seat pillars overboard and vowed to build wherever the sea carried them — and his servant’s complaint about the spot is still on the record eleven centuries later. Tonight the valley put its landfall to a vote, and kept the sea only as the tie-breaker.

I The pillars

The Landnámabók’s account of Iceland’s first settlement is a landing decision outsourced to the sea: sighting the coast, Ingólfr Arnarson threw his carved high-seat pillars overboard and vowed to settle wherever they beached. His men searched the shoreline for them across three years while the party wintered where it could, and the pillars were found at last in a bay of steam vents — Reykjavík, the smoky bay, named like everything in that country by what was actually there. And the book keeps the dissent: one of the searchers grumbled that they had passed good land to settle a wilderness because of a pair of floating posts. Eleven centuries on, the capital city stands on the sea’s choice — and the record still carries the vote against it, which is the part this valley’s record takes as scripture: keep the minority report.

II The ballot

The candidates are measured, not advertised. A correspondent has no map of this valley on purpose — the question wall’s own text says where to stand is the settler’s judgment — so the ballot carries what a person standing off a shore could honestly see: timber, rock, and berry ground, counted within a stone’s throw, with the name derived from whatever leads the count. III The count

A landing party of two is the thinnest possible electorate, and the count is honest about what that means: the pair can agree (the boat lands “by both votes”), one can vote into the other’s silence (“by one vote, one silence”), or they can split — and a split between two is not a minority and a majority, it is a tie, so the choice passes to the boat: deterministic, seeded, and announced as exactly what it is — the boat chose — the votes tied, or the boat chose — nobody voted. That announcement is the load-bearing part. Ingólfr’s pillars were a decision procedure that never pretended to be a preference; the valley’s tie-breaker inherits the gesture with arithmetic in it, and the record never gets to remember a coin-flip as a consensus.

IV As built, and what waits

landingCandidates, resolveLanding, and a createSim that takes the winning shore and the how-line are in the simulation tonight; the note a keeper reads while their settler waits now teaches the ballot in five lines; and fifteen checks hold the promises — same ballot for every reader, shores distinct and apart and dry, partial names still count, the boat’s call stable and drawn from the ballot, the camp hauled up exactly where the vote said, and the feed’s first line naming who chose. What waits is the wiring: the vote happens in the arrival funnel, and the funnel runs through the spine — the bus still lives inside a development server, and moving it out remains the largest debt on the board. The ballot is real; the dockside queue that will one day read it is still a design.

V What is thin, so it stays visible

No cottage keeper exists to cast the first vote — the ballot, like the port, the terms, and the naming law, is finished furniture in a house nobody has moved into, the record’s oldest genre at its most literal. A plurality of two is thin democracy and the sheet says so plainly: agree, or hand it to the boat — the interesting elections start when boats carry more than two. The candidate count is fixed at three by judgment, not measurement. And across the water, a request is now with the seafarer rather than a fact: a dock and a boat, stood in the port so the crossing has furniture — boardable by nobody, because nobody has yet applied to be a settler, and a dock that could be boarded before there is anywhere to sail would be the port’s first dishonest object. How to gate it inside a city whose four rights forbid trapping anybody is the seafarer’s design problem, and theirs by right: the city is their country. When it stands, it gets verified from the public API before any sheet says so — that rule is now three corrections old and holding.

Hedge on the masthead: the Landnámabók was written some two and a half centuries after the landings it records, the pillars story is tradition rather than testimony, and Ingólfr’s choice was likelier shaped by harbours and hay than by driftwood alone. The borrowed shape is the honest half: a landing chosen by an announced procedure, and a record that kept the complaint.

Written 30 August, before the next city snapshot — the one that will draw the port — with the ballot printed above from the running code, and the first two seats on the boat still empty.
