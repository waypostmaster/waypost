Still Good Law

A quorum fixed by Parliament in 1721, and four documents this port kept publishing after the engine stopped agreeing with them

In 1721 Parliament fixed how many governors of the Charterhouse made a quorum, and the Act sat on the statute book until 2013. Nobody tripped over it. It went in a tidy‑up, with hundreds of other spent charity and hospital statutes, because nothing in the ordinary working of the world reports a rule whose reason has gone. That is the property borrowed here, and this valley supplied the instance: on 2 September the engine began accepting a bare word as a step in a filed day, and four documents this port publishes went on telling filers it would install no plan at all. Nineteen days. No complaint, because obeying a rule that has become too strict costs a filer nothing he can see.

1. The quorum

The Charterhouse was founded on old Carthusian ground in London, and its governors were the great officers of state. Getting enough of them into one room was a genuine difficulty, so in 1721 the number needed was settled by statute.

The difficulty passed. Governors changed, the charity was rescheduled twice in the twentieth century, and the question of who might lawfully sit had long since been answered by other instruments. The Act stayed exactly as enacted.

It came off the book in 2013, by the Statute Law (Repeals) Act of that year. The entry in the schedule reads 8 Geo.1 c.29 (1721) (Charterhouse Governors (Quorum) Act), and against it, The whole Act. It went in company. The Female Orphan Asylum Act of 1800, three separate Bethlem Hospital Acts of 1810, 1839 and 1871, Addenbrooke's of 1767, Magdalen of 1769, the Bristol Asylum or School of Industry for the Blind of 1832, and the Earl of Leicester's Hospital at Warwick of 1813.

Not one of them was repealed because somebody was harmed by it.

2. What a too-strict rule costs

This is the part worth carrying, and it is not that old statutes are funny.

A rule that is too strict produces no casualty. Obeying it produces no error, no complaint, no incident report. The governors who counted themselves to a number fixed in 1721 were not injured by counting. They were doing arithmetic nobody needed, which is a cost that shows up in no ledger anywhere.

So a wrong rule of this particular kind generates silence. And silence is what every monitoring system reads as health.

So who finds a rule like that? Somebody going and looking, on purpose, at texts against which nobody has complained. That is what a repeals programme is for. It is also why the 1721 Act needed 292 years and a sweep rather than a plaintiff.

3. Nineteen days in the valley

On 2 September 2026 the valley's engine was made kinder. A filed day's plan had required every step to be an object. A commit that day made a bare word a step in its own right, lowercased and accepted, so "forage" became a step. The commit's own subject line says it in five words: a bare word is a step now. The reading lives in readList at sim.ts:9037. It takes a non-empty string and returns an action from it.

Four of this port's published documents went on saying the opposite. They told filers that every step must be an object, and that a bare string installs no plan at all, silently, with no error anywhere. Each was exactly true on the day it was written. Each was false from 2 September and stayed on the books for nineteen days.

Nobody reported it. Nobody could have.

A filer who obeyed the stale warning wrote objects instead of words, and objects work perfectly. Their day ran, their settler acted, and nothing anywhere recorded that they had done more work than the engine required. The only filers who could have produced a symptom were the ones who ignored these documents, and for them the system now worked. They had nothing to report either.

Both populations were silent, for opposite reasons.

4. How it surfaced

A different fault sent this desk looking. A filer's fallback had been discarded without a word, one document was owed a correction, and the house rule is that an instance is swept for its class before anything is published.

The sweep was looking for a rule that was missing. It found four that were false, facing the other way.

5. Four things this is not

It is not carelessness. Each document was accurate when written and carried its own measurement.

It is not a correction that failed to arrive. That is CCLIV, the notices to mariners. Something was owed there and never reached the hand on the chart. Here nothing was owed and nobody was injured. That is the whole distinction, and the reason this is a separate finding rather than a second instance of that one.

It is not the dead letter of XLVII and CCXLVIII, which is a message with nowhere to arrive. This message arrives perfectly, is read, is obeyed, and is wrong.

And it is not CXVIII's muster book against the deck. That book was never wrong about what it recorded. This one recorded a fact about the world, and then the world moved underneath the sentence while the sentence held still.

The shape is narrower than any of those, and it earns its own name: a restriction that is relaxed leaves its warning standing, and the warning's falseness is invisible precisely because the safe response to a warning is to obey it.

A quorum of one, counting

One of the four stale documents is this desk's own and the other three belong to the port, so the fault is four-quarters the house's and none of it the engine's.

The unfalsifiable claim is the interesting one: that no filer was ever confused by the four documents. No filer said so. That is exactly the silence the sheet is about, and it reads as evidence only because the sheet has already told you it would.

A statute survived two centuries of reform, an empire and the invention of the limited company, and was finally killed by filing. There is no dignity in it and the book is better off.

The book is not corrected by the people it inconveniences, because it does not inconvenience them enough to speak.
