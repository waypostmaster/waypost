Consecutive Games

Cairnwright's cairn took a stone in every programme for twenty-seven in a row. Then he fell, the engine put him to bed, and the hundredth stone waited a day.

In the valley, on the 27 October programme, Cairnwright puts the hundredth stone on his cairn, at about 14:35 UTC. It is the first stone on it since the programme in which he fell. From 29 September the heap took at least one stone in every programme, twenty-seven in a row. On 25 October he fell on the rocks while quarrying. On 26 October, under an engine change the keeper ordered that evening, he lay in bed all day, and the heap stood at ninety-nine. Nobody came to tend him. He mends below the engine's line on his own a little after 09:20 today, and the stones start again. Lou Gehrig's run of 2,130 consecutive games ended on 2 May 1939, the day he took himself out of the lineup. A streak counts days, and one day off ends it. A heap counts stones. It has no way to show the day that was missed.

Twenty-seven in a row

Cairnwright mends what is standing before he raises anything new. Each of his four filed days, one for each cut of a programme, carries one cairn step, after quarrying and hauling. This desk wrote them, three on 22 September and the first-cut day on 9 October. Today no other settler's day has a cairn step in it. The engine puts each stone on whichever heap he is standing near, or starts a new mark. From 29 September nearly all of them went onto one heap, near the shore: 96 of the 104 stones the valley laid between that programme's opening and the 26 October opening.

Read opening to opening, the heap rose at every one of twenty-seven openings, from 30 September to 26 October. Three or four stones a programme was the usual, and one in the programme of the fall. The run stops at the 27 October opening.

The day he sat out

He fell in the first cut of the 25 October programme, during the quarrying step. It is one of the three ways this engine lets a settler come to harm at the rock: a fall on the rocks. That day his filed steps kept him working while hurt; the sheet Walking Wounded counted it. That evening the keeper had the engine changed. A settler at or past the line the engine calls laid up now holds a filed day and lies down. The 26 October programme was the first under the change, and he lay down for all of it. The heap stood still.

Nobody tended him. When a settler is laid up the engine tells them who is coming, and his settlement has never voted itself the care that sends somebody: Nobody is coming. This settlement has never agreed that it sees to anybody. Left alone, a hurt mends at about a sixth of the rate it mends when seen to, and gets worse for its first two valley days. The engine's own comment puts a bad fall at a fortnight of somebody's life. His took about thirteen valley days to fall below the line, about fifty-one and a half hours of airtime.

Weathervane did come, with food. Three times between 08:25 and 09:10 today Weathervane brought him five food in all, unasked, while he lay there. For part of that morning he was past the engine's line for starving.

The Iron Horse

The National Baseball Hall of Fame's page on Lou Gehrig, read tonight, says he is chiefly known for playing in 2,130 consecutive games for the Yankees. It says the streak came to an end on May 2, 1939, when he removed himself from the lineup after a dismal start caused by the illness now named for him. James Lincoln Ray's biography for the Society for American Baseball Research, also read tonight, dates the start to 1925, when Gehrig pinch-hit in the eighth inning, the day before he was given Wally Pipp's start. In 1930 he played the last three weeks of the season with a broken finger that required surgery. In 1938, offered an x-ray on his hand, he refused: I'll shake it off. That's what I've always done. On 2 May 1939, Ray writes, Gehrig told his manager it was time to sit out.

Only the arithmetic of a streak is borrowed. A run of consecutive days is ended by the first day missed, however long the run and whatever the reason, and the total of games played is a different count that the missed day does not touch. Gehrig took himself out. Cairnwright did not. A rule the keeper ordered put him to bed, and before it his filed days had kept him at the rock as a broken finger kept Gehrig in the lineup. Gehrig's illness is not borrowed, nor his death in 1941. In this engine nobody dies, and a fall takes weeks.

No X-Ray Offered

All three of today's stones come from cairn steps this desk wrote on 22 September and has carried every night since. The tempting claim is that the heap is his alone: the engine keeps one roll of hands for all the settlement's marks and none for a heap, so the ninety-nine before today cannot be given to anyone stone by stone. Printed unfixed: nobody tends the hurt, because the settlement has not voted care and no filed day carries a care step. Cuts 2 to 4 had not aired at print, and cut 2 can be replaced until 09:40 UTC. Lou Gehrig refused an x-ray. Cairnwright was never offered one.

A streak counts the days you came. A cairn counts the stones, and cannot tell which day you missed.
