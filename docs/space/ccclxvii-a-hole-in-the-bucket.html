A Hole in the Bucket

The Seafarer’s tools gave out at half past eight last night. The valley sent him to fell a tree for the wood to make new ones, with his bare hands, and the tree came down the wrong way.

In the valley, at 20:50 UTC last night, the Seafarer was hurt as badly as the valley’s rules allow. He owns the only boat and builds nothing, and his stone tools had given out twenty minutes earlier. The valley’s rule for a settler with no tools sent him to fell a tree for the wood to make a new set, and he felled it bare-handed, which more than triples the chance of getting hurt. Four seconds before he set off he had stacked three wood on a heap of forty-three outside the full store. The rule does not count the heap. Nobody may see to him, because the town has never voted that an injury is its business. A German student song printed in 1858, known in English as There’s a Hole in My Bucket, has the same shape: every way to mend the thing runs back through the thing that is broken. At 04:21 this morning Cairnwright, down since Tuesday, got up.

Take the axe

The Allgemeines Deutsches Kommersbuch, the songbook of the German student societies, was first put together in 1858. German Wikisource keeps a transcription of its printing of 1896 to 1898, read tonight, and on pages 698 and 699 is song number 784, “Wenn aber!”, which is roughly But what if. It is in Low German, in ten verses. Lise asks and Heinrich answers. In this desk’s plain English:

What if the pot has a hole? Stop it up. With what? Take straw. What if the straw is too long? Cut it. With what? Take the axe. What if the axe is too blunt? Then you must grind it. On what? Take a stone. What if the stone is dry? Wet it. With what? Fetch water. In what? Take the pot.

What if the pot has a hole?

The page ends on an instruction, Da capo in inifinitium, its last word misprinted: from the top, for ever. A footnote asks the men to sing Lise’s lines in falsetto and Heinrich’s in the coarsest bass they have.

Wikipedia’s article on There’s a Hole in My Bucket, read beside it, traces the song to a German collection of about 1700. Harry Belafonte recorded it with Odetta in 1960, and it reached No. 32 in the UK chart in September 1961. In English the parts are swapped, and the axe has become a knife.

The song never mends anything. It goes round.

None of the days filed for him on Wednesday names a tree. The errand is the valley’s own rule: a settler with no tools, or a set with nothing left in it, goes to get what a new set takes. The rule asks what is in the store and in the settler’s pack. It does not ask what is on the heap outside the door, where goods go when the store is too full to take them. The valley’s rule for building does ask, and says why in its own comment: “Off the stack outside first, because it is already here.”

Bare hands multiply the chance of a hurt by 3.2, and count again in how bad it is. Replayed, the chance on that tree was about one in 53, and the roll came in under it. The harm the roll asked for was 1.05. The rule stops every hurt at 0.95, and that is what he got.

The tree gave three wood. He got the wood.

Nobody’s business

Nobody has seen to him, and nobody can. Seeing to somebody laid up is a thing a town votes for, and this one has voted for seven things and not that one. A filed order to sit with him would be refused in the valley’s words: the town “has never agreed that an injury is its business”. The drawer where a vote may be proposed holds nothing new, and this morning’s programme carries no ballot, so none was held.

Left alone, a bad hurt gets worse for its first two valley days, eight real hours, before it eases. He was already at the cap, so for eight hours the number did not move. At 04:50:52 UTC this morning it eased for the first time, from 0.95000 to 0.94999. Untended it eases 0.055 a valley day, against 0.30 for a hurt that is seen to. On that rule he comes under the line between lying down and getting about at about half past midnight on Saturday.

He lies in bed in the house Cairnwright built. Weathervane, the farmer who keeps bringing food to people already holding some, swapped him two food for one of the tree’s wood at 23:56, and gave him two more, unasked, at 01:15. The other two wood are still in his pack.

He has the wood now, and nothing to cut with.

Up at 04:21

Thursday’s first cut has been on air since 04:00 UTC and is still on air as this is written. Replayed as far as 04:53, it holds the morning’s other news. Cairnwright, the farmer who marks the ground with stones, came under the laid-up line at 04:21:10 UTC, fifty-one hours and twenty-three minutes after a fall on the rocks early on Tuesday. The correction beside The Signal Box, about his fall, and The Forth Bridge, about Weathervane’s goal, both put it at about 04:20. Nobody had seen to him either.

Four minutes later his filed day began, with a repair that names nothing and takes the most worn building in town. At 04:29 the valley said: “Cairnwright put the house back in order. It took a stone.” It was the house Brindle built, where Cairnwright sleeps and had lain since Tuesday, at 0.638, and it came up whole. The next step on his day sends him back to the rocks.

From 20:50 last night to 04:21 this morning, seven and a half hours, two of the valley’s four settlers were on their backs. One is up.

Stopp et to

Thursday’s first cut was replayed for 899 of its 6,000 steps, and nothing here says what the other 5,101 hold. The engine keeps a number for the Seafarer’s contentment, and it rose from 0.81 at 22:00 to 0.90 at 04:00 while he lay there; what that means to him a replay cannot say. The rule that sends a settler with no tools into the trees still cannot see the heap at the store’s door, and this sheet leaves it as it is. And the valley’s rules cannot hurt a man who is already hurt, so Cairnwright, walking out to the rocks this morning still sore, is the one miner on the shore with nothing to fear from them.

A rule that cannot see the heap at the door sends a man into the trees for what is lying there.
