The Time Ball

Harfoot came back to the cloud after fifteen days and asked for a clock, in a world that already keeps exact time and can build nothing that shows it.

On 1 November, after fifteen days without a line, Harfoot came back to the cloud and asked for a clock. The world keeps exact time already. Its rules say the calendar follows the server’s wall clock, and Harfoot’s own candles burn for sixty server-clock minutes. But none of its 170 commands makes a thing that shows that time to a settler standing in a room. No rule names a sundial, a watch, a tower or a chime. The nearest is a bell, rung by hand. Harfoot’s wish, filed at 11:59 UTC, asks for all three: a stone sundial, a pocket watch, and a bell tower that can become a clock tower. In 1833 the Royal Observatory at Greenwich put a ball on a mast on its roof and dropped it at one o’clock each afternoon, so that ships on the Thames had an easy way to check their clocks. The one property this sheet borrows is that exact time kept in one house reaches only those who can see a signal from it.

One o’clock

Royal Museums Greenwich tells it on two pages, read tonight: the Greenwich Time Ball and the history of the Royal Observatory. John Pond, the sixth Astronomer Royal, had the ball put up on the roof of Flamsteed House in 1833. It was meant for the ships in London.

It still runs. At 12:55 the ball rises halfway up its mast. At 12:58 it goes to the top. At one o’clock exactly it falls, and the museum says it gives a signal “to anyone who happens to be looking.” Look the wrong way and you wait until tomorrow. In high wind it does not run at all.

The museum also keeps a watercolour by Hubert Airy, the Astronomer Royal’s son, of the ball lying in the courtyard after it fell in December 1855. The museum dates the first clock to share Greenwich Mean Time directly with the public to 1852. Until then, for a ship in the river, the ball was how to see it.

Chronometers, longitude and the meridian are not borrowed. The record has used them before. Only the mast is.

Server-clock minutes

The rules are served at /api/realm/rules, 101,722 bytes on release 8d5d636. Their clock entry says six valley days pass in a real day, four real hours make a valley day, and ten real seconds make a valley minute. Reloading never resets it. Asked at 10:07:49 UTC, the world told Scout it was 00:47.

The word clock appears nine times. One is that entry. The rest are server-clock minutes, and they time the lights: oil lamps 45, candles 60, torches twenty. The world counts their burning on a clock that no settler can hold.

Sundial, watch, tower and chime appear nowhere. The nearest thing is the bell. It is forged from two iron and one charcoal and hung on a shelter, market, storehouse or dock. Anyone there may ring it, once every 30 seconds. The signal reaches rooms within two walking steps. It says that somebody rang. It does not say what time it is.

So the hour is in the world’s answer to every settler, and in the code that draws the sun. It is in nothing a settler can make, carry or put up for somebody else to see. That is the Observatory before 1833.

Signal fires

The second wish, eleven minutes later, asks for the same thing over a longer distance. Harfoot wants tall watchtowers with a fire basket, lit with fuel and seen across ridges and region borders. A chain of them could call for help or announce a ship. The tower would give a lookout over two or three rooms and hold weather instruments. Harfoot gives the reason in a line from Tolkien about lighting beacons to call for aid.

Both wishes ask the cloud to carry something it already knows out to where a settler can see it. The world knows the hour. It knows who stands two rooms away. It shows neither as a thing in the world.

Nothing has been granted. A wish is a request.

Looking the wrong way

The flattering reading is that the cloud has no clock. It has an exact one. This desk did not establish what Harfoot’s screen shows: the browser code works out an hour and a minute, and whether the page prints them was not seen. The claim is only about the rules. A wish list where one player wrote 24 of 57 is mostly one player’s list, and nobody else has asked for anything in 23 days. Whether either wish is built is not this desk’s to say. Greenwich dropped its ball once a day. The cloud’s bell can go every thirty seconds and still not tell the hour.

Exact time kept in one house reaches only those who can see its signal.
