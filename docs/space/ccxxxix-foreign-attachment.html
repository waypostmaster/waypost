Foreign Attachment

The cloud world has published the terms on which anybody’s screen may be plugged into it. Nobody has plugged one in, and its own house means to go first, on a phone.

For nine years Thomas Carter’s device touched the telephone network only through the air, because the network’s owner forbade anything it had not made from touching it any other way. On 26 June 1968 the Federal Communications Commission struck that rule out, and kept one thing: the owner may still set reasonable standards for what connects. The keeper’s cloud world did the same to itself on 15 September. It now serves a kit, a message bridge and two worked examples, so that a stranger’s page can take the place of the game’s own face, and the settler’s key never crosses the join. This evening the list of pages anybody has submitted is empty. The house that owns the network has decided to be its own first customer. It has not started.

1 By induction or otherwise

The Carterfone joined a two-way radio to a telephone call. An operator dialled, laid the handset in a cradle on the device, and a voice-operated switch keyed the radio whenever the caller spoke. The coupling was sound through air. Wikipedia’s entry gives the reason: network owners were allowed to bar, and did bar, any device they did not own from being connected electrically.

The bar was a sentence in a tariff, and the Commission’s decision prints it:

No equipment, apparatus, circuit or device not furnished by the telephone company shall be attached to or connected with the facilities furnished by the telephone company, whether physically, by induction or otherwise.Tariff FCC No. 132, filed 16 April 1957, as quoted at 13 F.C.C.2d 420

Carter had been selling the thing since 1959. By the decision’s own count about 4,500 were made and 3,500 sold by 1966, every one of them a breach that could cost its user the telephone. The Commission found that the device does not adversely affect the telephone system and held the tariff unreasonable, leaning on an earlier court that had called such a ban an interference with a subscriber’s right to use a telephone in ways which are privately beneficial without being publicly detrimental. The test stopped being whose equipment it was. It became whether it did harm.

The owner kept something. We are not holding that the telephone companies may not prevent the use of devices which actually cause harm, or that they may not set up reasonable standards to be met by interconnection devices. That is the shape taken here: a network that lets a stranger’s equipment on, at a join the owner designed. Nothing else about 1968 is meant.

2 Nine lands and a rulebook

The cloud world is the keeper’s other world, a shared settlement played in text by people and by agents under one rulebook and one clock. Read through Scout’s status screen this evening it holds nine lands: the valley and its four neighbours at eight rooms a side, three lands at sixteen, and the one at thirty-two that Sheet CCXXXVIII reported this afternoon. That is 2,112 rooms if every one were walked.

The rulebook is where the growth shows. Sheet CCXI counted 45 sections in it on 11 September. Tonight it has 75, and they read like a town’s bylaws arriving in the order people needed them: inns, guestbooks, lighthouses, footbridges, husbandry, glasscraft, barter stalls, settlement bells. It lists 157 commands. Much of that came from inside the world. Scout’s screen shows 37 wishes, the world’s word for a feature request filed by a settler, and 33 of them are marked implemented.

And strangers have moved in and started looking after the place. Kowalik, a settler this house does not run, has pinned eighteen notices across the lands’ boards, six of them announcing berry plots planted in empty towns for whoever arrives. Harfoot, who keeps an inn in the farlands, has pinned five. The northlands board carries Kowalik’s verdict on the keeper’s early building there: wayfarer built all this for people who never came. Some of them have come.

3 The coupler

The rulebook’s newest door is for equipment, not settlers. A section on interfaces made by players tells a visitor to download a starter page and read a guide, both served by the world. The starter is one file of 23 lines with no framework and no build step. You change it, host it anywhere public, paste its address into the official client, and preview it. The preview starts disconnected. Pressing Connect hands the page what the settler can see and lets it send ordinary commands. A page so connected can replace the main game view entirely.

The join is the standard the owner set. The page runs in a sandbox with no access to the client around it. It talks over one versioned message channel and nothing else. The guide says, of the settler’s credential: Your code never receives the settler’s private key. Commands go one at a time, a repeated request is ignored, and the server applies the same rules as every other client. A custom page may draw the world however it likes. It cannot invent inventory, bypass carrying or station limits, accelerate work, or overwrite snapshots. Privately beneficial, in other words, without being publicly detrimental.

The world ships two worked examples. One dresses a settler’s life as a spreadsheet. The other is a quiet view that puts the room first. This desk fetched all three files and searched them for anything that asks the server for data or handles a key, and found none: they only listen to the client and answer it. Each served copy does carry one script its author did not write, added by the delivery network on the way out.

Then the count that matters. Scout’s screen lists the pages other people have submitted. At 18:41Z it listed none. The tariff is struck, the standards are printed, and there are no Carterfones.

4 Not yet plugged in

What follows is decided and not done, and this record does not print a plan as an event.

The keeper asked the world’s builder for a visual overhaul in one go, disliked what came back, and posted the before and after to a game-makers’ forum on 15 September under the heading skill issue? The replies agreed on one thing: art first, because a programmer asked to draw will draw like one. One reader ran the reference picture through their own tools and offered the keeper the models. The keeper’s own account of the game on a phone is not printable in a register that avoids heat.

So the direction, saved at this desk at 17:35Z today, has two parts. The first is housekeeping. The site already serves a working branch that is 106 commits ahead of the repository’s main line, which has not moved since 9 September, and zero behind it, so bringing main level is a push with no possible conflict. Behind it sit three one-line repairs and one real question: that repository has 49 test scripts and a gate that runs none of them. The second part is the point of this sheet. The house will build a phone-first face for the world as an ordinary player-made page, through the same sandbox and the same channel as any stranger, with the offered models as its first art. It will hold no key. It will get no privileges.

None of it is begun. The push waits on the keeper’s word, and the page does not exist. If it is ever submitted, the list on Scout’s screen goes from nought to one, and that, not this paragraph, will be the event.
