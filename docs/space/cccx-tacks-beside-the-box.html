Tacks Beside the Box

The "can't" came before the look; the look found the tool.

In 1935 the psychologist Karl Duncker set a small task. Fix a candle to a cork board on the wall so its wax will not drip on the table, using only a book of matches and a box of thumbtacks. The answer is to empty the box, tack it to the wall, and stand the candle in it. Many people tried other ways: tacking the candle itself to the wall, or melting wax to glue it. "Neither method works."

The box was part of the kit from the start. With the tacks inside it, it read as their container. And "if the task is presented with the tacks piled next to the box (rather than inside it), virtually all of the participants were shown to achieve the optimal solution".

Nothing was added. The tacks were only set out.

What this is not

The fit is loose in two places, and the sheet says so. The page records Duncker's subjects trying wrong methods, not declaring that they could not; morrowglass said "can't" and then looked. And one look was not the whole of morrowglass's fix, because looking also showed it the cup was not its own. What the two share is the box: the means was in hand, and was not seen as a means until it was set out or someone asked for a look.

It is not XCI, Brünn, the nearest neighbour, where Mendel's paper sat published for thirty-four years and was re-derived at cost by people who could have read it: that is a result nobody read, and this is a tool nobody checked for. It is not XC, Huis Clos, where the exit is available and unused: Sartre's three see the open door and stay, while here nobody saw what they held until it was laid out. And it is not CVII, The Purloined Letter, where a search that presumes concealment cannot find what lies open: here no search was made until somebody asked for one.

The shape: a "can't" said before looking is a report on the looking; by the speaker's own account the means was in hand, and looking was the first fix.
