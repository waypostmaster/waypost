The Forth Bridge

Weathervane finished their life’s work this morning, seeing nobody in this valley go hungry again, for the second time since Tuesday morning. Each time the valley handed back the same words in the same instant, with thirty more food to give away.

In the valley, at 06:50 UTC this morning, Weathervane did what they had set out to do: see nobody in this valley go hungry again. It was the second time in under a day. The first was at 09:19 on Tuesday. Each time the valley said so, and in the same instant handed Weathervane the same sentence again, with thirty more food to give away. The engine measures that goal as food given over a lifetime. It never asks who is hungry. On Tuesday, when the job was first done, the hungriest settler on the shore was Weathervane. The Forth Bridge in Scotland, opened in 1890, gave English its phrase for a job that has to be started again the moment it is finished. The phrase was a myth, and its owners retired it in 2011. In this valley it is the rule.

By the time you have finished

Network Rail, in charge of the span since 2002, put the saying in a press release of 5 September 2011, read tonight. To be painting the Forth Bridge is a job that “takes such a long time that by the time you have finished doing it, you have to start again.” The release said Network Rail and its contractor Balfour Beatty would “preside over the end of a modern myth”: the old paint of 120 years blasted off, the steel repaired, and a glass flake epoxy of the kind used offshore put on in three layers.

On 9 December 2011 the next release said the “10-year, £130m programme of refurbishment and repainting” had ended, and that the structure “will not need a full paint job for at least 20 years.” David Simpson, the route’s managing director for Scotland, called it “one of the world’s never-ending tasks.”

Wikipedia’s article on the Forth Bridge, read beside them, is blunter about the myth. The phrase was coined “on the erroneous belief” that the painters went straight back to the start. “Such a practice never existed, as weathered areas were given more attention, but there was a permanent maintenance crew.” Only the saying is borrowed here. The steel, the rivets and the money are not.

The work was never really endless. The phrase said it was.

Thirty food, twice

Weathervane is a farmer, and a farmer in this valley is given one long goal: see nobody in this valley go hungry again. The engine writes it down as a number. It takes the food the farmer has given away in their whole life and adds thirty, and the goal is done when the lifetime count reaches it. A replay of Tuesday’s four cuts and Wednesday’s first two, on the engine that aired them and read at every step, gives both finishes.

The two finishes were 21 hours and 31 minutes apart. The second goal was set in the same step of the engine as the first was finished, and so was the third. Because the count runs over a lifetime, the third began 95% done. By 16:00 it stood at 96%.

The engine keeps hunger on a scale from nought, just fed, to one. A settler eats from their own pack past about four fifths. At Tuesday’s finish the two hungriest on the shore were Weathervane, at 0.93 with two food in hand, and the Seafarer, at 0.81. At Wednesday’s, nobody was over half, and the hungriest was Brindle, the storekeeper, at 0.26. The errand that carries the food counts a settler as hungry if they are over half or their pack is empty, and takes it to the hungriest of those.

Brindle had fourteen of the thirty. The storekeeper’s depot told the valley it was full 47 times between five in the morning and four in the afternoon.

The rest of the shore

The one settler who cannot fetch his own food got one meal in the twelve aired hours. Cairnwright, the farmer who marks the ground with stones, has lain on the rocks since a fall early on Tuesday. At 14:18 Weathervane walked over with two food, the first since 02:17, and Weathervane’s regard for Cairnwright rose a tenth, to 0.57. Nobody sat with him. His hurt eased at the rate the valley gives a hurt nobody tends, from 0.685 at 04:00 to 0.602 at 10:00 and 0.520 at 16:00. On this scale 0.35 is the line between lying down and getting about, and on the rule he crosses it at about 04:20 on Thursday.

Nothing was mended. In twelve hours no settler took a repair. Every one of the nine buildings wore, and the worst all day was the house Weathervane sleeps in, from 0.682 to 0.639. At 10:02 the Seafarer’s filed patch on the boat was refused, because she stood at 0.898 and this valley patches nothing above 0.85. None of the eight days filed for the two cuts holds a sailing, and nobody sailed. The drawer for proposals held nothing new, so no vote on care was held.

The goal counted every meal. It never noticed which ones were needed.

A permanent maintenance crew

The hunger figures here are four settlers read at two steps out of the 36,000 replayed, one on each morning, and say nothing about the hours between. Whether Weathervane minds the job coming back cannot be asked of a replay; the engine keeps a number for contentment, and it rose by a fifth at each finish. The goal that measures a sentence about hunger with a lifetime tally of food given is left as it is. Network Rail needed ten years and £130 million to finish the job once. By the engine’s arithmetic, Weathervane is already 96% of the way to ending hunger in the valley for the third time since Tuesday morning.

A job measured by a count is finished every time the count comes round.
