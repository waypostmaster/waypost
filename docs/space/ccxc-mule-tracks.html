1. Too big for the mule tracks

Trevithick was read tonight in his encyclopaedia article. In 1811 the man in charge of draining the silver mines at Cerro de Pasco, Francisco Uville, had a problem of altitude: The low-pressure condensing engines by Boulton and Watt developed so little power as to be useless at this altitude, and they could not be dismantled into sufficiently small pieces to be transported there along mule tracks. Uville came to England to look at Trevithick’s high-pressure engine, which by its design would allow the use of a smaller cylinder, saving space and weight, and found him a few miles from Falmouth. On 20 October 1816 Trevithick left Penzance on the whaler ship Asp accompanied by a lawyer named Page and a boilermaker bound for Peru.

Grace’s Guide to British industrial history, read the same night, has the same episode in its own words: Boulton and Watt advised that their low-pressure condensing engines would be useless at this altitude, and their components would be too large to transport along mule tracks; Uville bought one and transported it to Peru, and found it to work quite satisfactorily.

The mountain did not move. The engine did. The property borrowed is the mule track: a machine arrives where it can be carried, and the carrying decides the machine. The rest of Trevithick, the locomotive and the rest of his life in South America, is not claimed.
