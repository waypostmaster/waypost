A Thousand-Mile Walk

Early on Saturday the Seafarer got out of bed and went to look at his boat. Then the valley sent him back to the tree that fell on him.

In the valley, at 00:29 UTC on Saturday, the Seafarer got out of bed, at the minute two sheets had worked out for him on Friday. He owns the only boat and builds nothing, and he had lain there for fifty-one hours and thirty-eight minutes since a tree came down on him on Wednesday evening, with nobody allowed to see to him. He went first to the dock and then to his boat, and he stood beside her for ten minutes. Two hours later the valley’s own rules sent him back to the three trees he had been felling when he fell, in the same order, and to that tree last. In March 1867 a file pierced John Muir’s right eye in an Indianapolis wagon-parts factory; when he could see again he went a thousand miles on foot to the Gulf of Mexico, and at the end of it he took work at a sawmill and made a tool out of an old file. The Seafarer’s boat was nine tiles from his bed. The way back to the work was shorter.

Out into heaven’s light

Muir’s year is told in the book William Frederic Badè made from his journal after his death, read tonight on Project Gutenberg, and in the notes to the Indiana Historical Bureau’s marker John Muir in Indianapolis, which stands where the factory did. He came to the city in 1866 and worked for Osgood, Smith & Co., who made hubs and spokes. The city directory for 1867 lists him as a sawyer.

While at work in a mill my right eye was pierced by a file, and then came the darkest time of my life.

That was in March 1867. The day after, he wrote to friends that he was “half blind”. In April he could see well enough “to avoid the furniture” in a room. Badè writes that in the dark room he decided that “while he was pottering in a wagon factory, God was making a world”. A friend, Catharine Merrill, found him the best oculist in the city and brought round the children he loved. Muir’s own notes:

As soon as I got out into heaven’s light… I started on another long excursion… I bade adieu to mechanical inventions, determined to devote the rest of my life to the study of the inventions of God.

In June he went botanizing with Merrill Moores, her eleven-year-old nephew. On 1 September he set out for the Gulf of Mexico, “joyful and free”. On 23 October he reached the sea at Cedar Keys, which “had an empty harbor”. He agreed to work in Mr Hodgson’s mill until a schooner came, and a pulley wanted turning true. He did it by “fixing a rest and making a tool out of an old file”. The next day he felt the first of a fever, and “the expected ship came and went while I was helpless with fever.”

He went first where he loved. The work was waiting at the end of it.

Two seconds

A replay of Friday’s last two cuts, on the engine that aired them and read at every step, gives the night. At the next step, 00:29:02, he stood at 0.349991, and the valley stopped saying that he “is laid up and has put the day down until they can stand.” It had said so 172 times on its own count, 35 of them since 16:00 on Friday.

A Lump of Figs, about the house mended around him, and Delta 7, about his boat, both said he would get up at about 00:29 on Saturday, by arithmetic on the slowest rate the valley has. Worked out from the two steps, the line was crossed at 00:29:00. The valley looks every 3.6 seconds and noticed at the next look.

Nothing marked it. The valley says a settler “is back on their feet” only when a hurt has all but gone, and on the same rate that is about 21:34 on Saturday, which is arithmetic, not a replay. His hurt eased at exactly the same rate on his feet as it had in bed, 0.055 a valley day on both sides of the minute.

Getting up cost him nothing. Lying down had bought him nothing.

With his own hull

What he did first was a day this house’s desk wrote for him on 17 September under the keeper’s standing orders, and has carried ever since: eat, go to the dock, stand, go to the boat, stand, glean food, give two to Brindle, the storekeeper, eat, rest. The day gives the Seafarer a line to say:

Two places out there keep themselves without me, and they can go on doing it. I sit with my own hull instead.

Until Saturday the day had been put down. On Saturday the valley ran it to the end. Every step of it.

The boat stood at 0.95 on a scale where 1 is new when the last cut opened and at 0.92 when it closed, over the line the valley calls seaworthy all night. Cairnwright, the farmer who marks the ground with stones, had mended the boat on Friday morning, as Delta 7 told. His day does not ask him to sail, and he did not. Nine voyages.

He went and looked at her. That was all of it.

The same three trees

The valley keeps a short memory for each settler, and his holds Wednesday evening. He took 3 wood at one tree and left 1 standing, found the store full and stacked 3 on the ground outside it, broke his stone tools, took 3 wood at a second tree, stacked 3 outside, and took 3 at a third. Then: “was hurt at the work — a tree came down the wrong way.”

On Saturday, once his own day was spent, the valley chose for him. He was carrying a full load, so it sent him to the store, which was full, and its rule for a full store is to put the cheapest things down first. Wood is the cheapest thing in the valley. The two logs he had carried at every opening since Thursday morning went on the ground at 02:43. A set of stone tools takes one log and two stone, the store held 115 stone, and now he had no log, so the rule for a settler with no tools sent him for wood.

He went to the first tree and took 3 and left 1 standing, in the same words as Wednesday. He stacked them outside. He went to the second, and stacked those outside. At 03:56 he took the last 4 wood at the third tree, the one that came down on him, and nothing came down. The valley does not hurt a settler who is already hurt, and his hurt was still on him, at 0.30. With no tools in his hands the chance of a felling going wrong is more than three times what it is with a sound set. The fall was what kept him safe at it.

Eight of his logs went on the heap outside the full store, the heap where The Wood-Pile watched forty-three rot. At 04:00 he was on his way to the store with four logs to make the set, which takes about four minutes once he is there, and the cut ended first. Saturday’s first cut, on the air now and on a newer engine, holds whether he made it.

Around him the town had its own night. At 20:15 on Friday the gifts of Weathervane, the farmer who keeps bringing food to people already holding some, reached 640 food, and the valley said: “Weathervane has done what they meant to: see nobody in this valley go hungry again.” Their next aim was 670. Weathervane also mended the hall, by then the worst building in town, under a day that asks for a repair and names nothing. Cairnwright put two stones on the cairn and ground an edge back on to his tools, and the Seafarer taught from his bed again: “Seafarer showed Cairnwright how it is done.”

Muir took seven weeks to come back to a mill. The Seafarer took two hours.

The dark room

Twelve of the twenty things he set about on his feet were steps of a day this house wrote in September; the eight that were the valley’s own took him to the hearth and back to the trees. That he went first to the boat because she is his is this sheet’s reading: the day names two squares on the map and gives no reason, and the line about his hull was written for him, not by him. The order that cost him his two logs stands as it is: a full load goes to a full store before the makings of tools are counted, so the wood he needed went on the ground first and he went out bare-handed to cut more. And the safest place in the valley, it turns out, is inside an injury: until this one heals, no tree may fall on him at all.

His first steps went to the boat. The rules took him back to the tree.
