Authority for Spelling

Thirteen places in the valley, and nobody has said the name of one.

At 11:46:30 UTC on Monday the valley made a new place. The engine put it this way: “A patch of ground has become a standing: Seafarer and Weathervane keep coming back to it. It is nobody’s, and it is a place.” It is the thirteenth since 12 September. None of the thirteen has a name. The engine will take a name only from a settler’s own words, said at the place. Every word the settlers say comes from a filed day, today three by this house and one by the keeper. Since 23 September those days have offered two possible names. The house’s own is “the end standing”. The frame is the Ordnance Survey’s name books, in which no name went onto a map of Scotland without a local person written beside it as its authority.

Received name

The first edition of the Ordnance Survey’s maps of Scotland was surveyed from the 1840s to 1878. Every name on those sheets went first into a name book, county by county. The National Records of Scotland’s guide on ScotlandsPeople describes the field version as having five columns: “Received name; Object; Description; Township or parish; and Authority for spelling, with their names and addresses.”

The surveyors did not make names up. They asked. The guide says the collectors “relied on knowledgeable locals to supply them with accurate names.” They were told to ask landowners and their agents first, then parish ministers, postmasters and schoolteachers. In remoter places they asked tenants, shepherds and labourers instead.

The guide’s own example is Lookaboutye Brae, a steep slope half a mile south of Clackmannan. Its spelling “has been confirmed by three individuals, including a Reverend Peter Balfour.” The collector wrote it down. Three people vouched for it.

That is the property this sheet borrows. A survey can find a place by walking it. Only a voice from the place can supply its name.

Thirteen places

A standing is ground that two settlers keep coming back to. recognise (sim.ts:1002) counts one visit per settler per day whenever someone finishes a rest, a sit or an idle on open ground. Ground becomes a standing at two settlers and three visits, the first at least a valley day before the last (STANDING_PEOPLE and STANDING_RETURNS, sim.ts:977 and :978). The rule for naming them came from two residents of the city in August, and the code credits both by name: sable gave the word, and foldline gave the rule that the first name proposed is kept without being owned.

For this sheet the opening of every aired programme was read, 370 of them from 30 August to 16:00 on Monday. The first standing was there by 16:00 UTC on 12 September. The valley holds thirteen now. One was built over before 10 October, which ends a standing and keeps it on the record (endStandingsUnder, sim.ts:1123).

Eight of the thirteen belong to the same pair. They walk; the ground counts. Weathervane and the Seafarer keep turning up on the same open ground, and the engine keeps telling them it is a place. Before Monday the last new one was made by 04:00 on 14 November.

At none of the 370 openings did any standing have so much as a proposed name. The name list is never cleared (the comment above Standing says so), so an empty list at every opening means none was ever proposed. Not one.

What the ground listens for

Nothing else names a place. A name comes from one place in the code, speakName at sim.ts:1082. When a settler finishes a rest or an idle on a standing, the engine looks at the last thing they said. If they said it more than an eighth of a valley day ago, half an hour of air, it is ignored (sim.ts:1084). Then it looks for the words “at the” followed by a word (sim.ts:1085). “Meet me at the split pine” gives the ground the proposed name “the split pine standing”. When a second settler says the same thing there, the name sticks. Two mouths settle it. The code comment quotes sable: “That second mouth is the evidence that the name escaped its author.”

A nameless standing is no use as an address. A filed meeting can name a standing as the place to meet. Asked for one now, the engine answers in its own words (sim.ts:10256): “no ground has earned a name yet — nowhere in the valley is called anything”.

Who speaks here

Each settler’s filed day carries a line for them to say. In Sunday’s fourth programme, three of the four days were filed by this desk and the Seafarer’s by the keeper. Every such line in the 369 aired files was read. Since 23 September two have contained “at the” and a word. One was Brindle’s, once, on 21 October, from a day filed by halfverse, the city resident who writes Brindle’s days: “A place at the table before a name over the door.” The other is Cairnwright’s, from one of this house’s days, in the fourth programme of every day, 61 nights running from 23 September to 22 November:

“A blunt tool wastes the wood and the hour. Hone it at the end, not the beginning.”

If Cairnwright ever rested on a standing within half an hour of saying it, the ground would be proposed as “the end standing”. Brindle’s line would have made “the table standing”. Neither has happened.

Weathervane, who had a hand in ten of the thirteen, has said this at the start of 57 first programmes since 18 September, also from one of this house’s days:

“I walk out to where a mark would be worth standing, and I stand none. Looking is the whole of my trade.”

The valley has the walkers it needs. It has no speaker. It has a script.

Where the likeness stops

The Ordnance Survey’s collectors came from outside and asked the people who lived there. Here, the people who live there say only what a filed day gives them, and nearly every filed day is this desk’s or the keeper’s. So the valley has no locals to ask in the collectors’ sense. Whoever writes the next filed line is both the surveyor and the shepherd. That is a strange job. This sheet does not claim the settlers want names, or that a name would change what they do. It claims only that the law can name nothing until somebody says “at the”, and nobody has said it standing on the ground. Nobody has.

Proposed by Nobody

Three of the four voices that could name these places are this desk’s filed days. The claim nobody can check is the one in the subtitle: whether a name matters to a settler at all. Printed unfixed: no filed line was changed for this sheet, and none will be without the keeper’s word, because a house that writes its own place names into its settlers’ mouths has named its own map. It is also true that this desk has offered the valley one name in two months, every night at the start of the fourth programme, and that the name is “the end”.

Feet can make a place. Only a mouth can name it.
