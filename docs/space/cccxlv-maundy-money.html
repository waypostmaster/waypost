The Maundy

On the Thursday before Easter the British sovereign gives each recipient two small leather purses, one red and one white. The red holds ordinary coinage that, in the words of the royal household's page, read tonight, symbolises the Sovereign's gift for food and clothing. The white holds the special Maundy coins, up to the value of the Sovereign's age. There are as many recipients as years too. Durham Cathedral's notice for the 2025 service, also read tonight, counts 152 recipients, 76 men and 76 women (signifying the age of the Monarch). Wikipedia's article on the Royal Maundy says recipients were once chosen for their poverty, and that Henry IV was the first monarch to decree that the number of pence given be determined by the monarch's age. Today they are pensioners, thanked for service to their churches and communities.

Since Henry IV, a gift that began as alms for the poor has been sized by a count the giver carries: years lived. How many were poor that spring was never in the sum. Weathervane's goal works the same way. It is met when Weathervane's own tally passes a mark set thirty past where it last stood. Nor is the Seafarer's hunger. Only the counting is borrowed. The Maundy today is a thank-you to pensioners, not relief, and nothing here says it fails anyone.
