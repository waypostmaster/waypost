1 · A search through what you already hold

Between 1840 and 1855 the expeditions of Austen Henry Layard, Henry Rawlinson and Hormuzd Rassam shipped the broken clay of the Assyrian royal library from the mound of Kouyunjik, near Mosul, to the British Museum. George Smith, born in Chelsea in 1840, was apprenticed at fourteen to a publishing house to learn banknote engraving, and spent his lunch hours in the Museum reading about cuneiform. He was later employed to sort the fragments. In 1872 he found in them the Babylonian flood narrative, and read it to the Society of Biblical Archaeology on 3 December.

Smith wrote his own account four years later, and it is the frame. He is linked here where he is introduced, and this desk read the 1876 text of The Chaldean Account of Genesis at the Internet Archive, beside the encyclopaedia entry for his dates and his trade. Rawlinson, Smith records, “estimated the number of these fragments of inscriptions at over twenty thousand.” They sat in the building. And for two decades, in Smith’s words: “nothing was looked for or discovered bearing upon the events of Genesis.”

What changed was not a dig. Smith had already cut the Museum’s cuneiform into six divisions for his own convenience, and he says what that bought him: “By placing all the tablets and fragments of the same class together, I had been able to complete several texts, to easily find any subject required, and at any time to get a general idea of the contents of the collection.” Then he went and looked. “On looking down the third column, my eye caught the statement that the ship rested on the mountains of Nizir.”

ONE PROPERTY IS BORROWED and no other: the search that works is a search through what you already hold, and it works because somebody first arranged the holding into columns that can be looked down. Nothing else here is claimed. Not the scholarship, not the theology, not the race between museums. Smith went out to Nineveh afterwards and dug, and found more; the sorting came first, and the sorting is the borrowed part. Against it, a settler with sixty-four sorted rows in its hand, walking.
