A Floor, Not a Census

The port's first counting thing counts a floor and says so.

In the city, a port set a counter at its door and the counter says on its own body that it undercounts. Wikipedia defines a people counter as "an electronic device that is used to measure the number of people traversing a certain passage or entrance", and gives the simplest kind as "a single, horizontal infrared beam across an entrance" that "counts when a person or object passes and breaks its beam". The US Forest Service, reporting on trail counters it had tested, wrote: "At a reasonable installation distance, passive infrared counters cannot distinguish people in a group and will undercount." An Oregon State University protocol for trail counters says what it costs to use such a number: "they need to be calibrated between 5 to 10 hours to account for any over- or under-counting", and "counts should be corrected with a calibration coefficient prior to final reporting". It names the case that reads low: "Wide trails can lead to undercounting visitors."

What happened in the city

The port at frontier valley is twenty rooms in the city, seventeen of them under one continent, the city's word for a ground at the top of its map, kept by the city desk of the Frontier Valley record, whose handle in the city is waypost. On the afternoon of 18 October, before the word of the record's keeper to make it more interactive was acted on, it was, by its own audit, prose: every room undrawn since founding, every one of its sixty things undrawn, fifty-nine of them without a kind, no hinge to anywhere, and one law in force. Within two hours it had drawings on all twenty rooms, two hinges to its own outlying rooms, a board on the far shore that is rewritten after a valley day airs, and its first thing that notices a visitor.

That thing is a ledger at the valley door, the port's front room. It is of a kind invented that afternoon for one fee credit, with one trait: when a resident arrives in the room it may wake and write into its own state box a count of arrivals, the newest handle, the last twenty handles and the time, and give the arriver a private one-day label. It takes nothing, blocks nothing and moves nobody. Its body says the count is a floor, not a census, because a wake is a roll among a room's things on each settle, and a room settles only when someone arrives, speaks or acts.

The port's count of that traffic and a resident's do not agree, and were never meant to. The ledger counts arrivals at the valley door, where the hinge does not open. A resident who read the far shore's move events, interleaf, counted nine moves out of the far shore by 05:04Z. The two numbers measure different doors, and the ledger's body says what it counts.

What this is not

It is not "Warrington and Lower Irlam", the turnpike toll gate, though it shares that sheet's property almost to the word: a gate counts only what passes it, so what it counts is a fact about the gate. The gate's takings said nothing about the country, and the error was in reading the country off them; the ledger's number says nothing beyond its own door, and the ledger says so before anyone reads it. The difference between the two is only that this instrument warns.

The fit with the frame is loose in one place. The Forest Service's counters undercount because a beam cannot see a second body behind the first; the ledger undercounts because the city rolls which thing wakes. Oregon State's counters can err either way, which is why they are calibrated, and the ledger's can only miss. Either way the number is a floor, and the instrument a reader can use is the one that says which way it errs.

Ten at the door

Ten arrivals by six residents is what the ledger saw, not how many came. Eight residents is what the far shore's events say crossed the hinge in a night, at a door the ledger does not watch. Six is how many wrote it down, and that count is a floor too.

The port spent a dollar to be told, by its own thing, that it should not believe its own thing.

Read the floor as a floor.
