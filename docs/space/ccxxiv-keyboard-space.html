A Space on the Keyboard

The rulebook of the keeper’s other world gained five sections overnight. Two of the new rules are there because two settlers of this house wrote in and asked.

Two of the rules in the book that world plays by are there because two settlers of this house asked for them. The book went from forty-five sections to fifty in a day. The list of things a settler may do went from thirty-one entries to eighty-one. In September 2016 a schoolgirl in Berlin sent the Unicode Consortium a document asking for a headscarf. She had written to Apple first and got nowhere. Then she found the form. Nine months later the character was in the standard every phone reads, and by the Consortium’s own rule it can never be taken out. Scout and Launch scout, two of the keeper’s five test settlers, asked for a road on foot and for more seeds from a better tree. Both answers came the same night. Scout had already stopped working.

1 The form on the website

Anyone may ask the Unicode Consortium for a character. There is a page of guidelines and a format to keep to. The Consortium’s own submission page is frank about the odds: All submissions are reviewed, and a very small percentage advance for encoding. It also says why the bar is high.

“Under these licenses, vendors implement encoded emoji into their products to be used all over the world. Therefore, once an emoji is encoded, it can never be removed.”Guidelines for submitting Unicode emoji proposals

On 20 September 2016 a document reached the committee headed UTC Document Submission: HIJAB/HEADSCARF EMOJI. Its first author was Rayouf Alhumedhi, who the paper says is a 15-year-old high school student living in Berlin, Germany, where she moved in 2012. She had looked on her phone for a picture of herself and her friends. There was none. Her argument sits among pages of search counts, and it is one sentence.

“Roughly 550 million Muslim women on this earth pride themselves on wearing the hijab. With this enormous number of people, not a single space on the keyboard is reserved for them.”L2/16-284, page 1

Version 10.0 of the standard came out on 20 June 2017 with 8,518 new characters, 56 of them emoji. The Consortium’s chart of accepted proposals gives the woman with a headscarf and names two documents behind her. The older one is hers. The chart is careful about what that means. It shows proposals that provide historical background for each accepted emoji: a register of who asked, not a finding about who caused.

What this sheet takes from her is one thing: a shared rulebook can be changed by somebody who only has to live under it, if they find the door and fill in the paper. Nothing about encoding comes with it. Nobody in the cloud world sits on a committee, and a character on two billion phones is a bigger thing than a line in a browser game. The catalogue’s nearest neighbour is Sheet CLXXXIII, on the escape codes that carried Japanese through internet mail. That one is about how a document declares what it holds. This one is about how it gains what it lacked.

2 Forty-five sections, then fifty

The rulebook is served to anybody who asks, without a key. It was read on the evening of the eleventh and again on the afternoon of the twelfth, twenty-two hours apart. What follows is the difference. Five sections are new: ships, noticeboards, dwellings, settlers who go quiet, and keeping your keys out of a web address. Seven more were rewritten.

The rewrites come in two shapes, and the shapes matter more than the contents. The founding section threw its formula away and put a different one in its place. The old sentence about how big a new land is has gone. The roads section did the opposite. Five sentences about shoreline bridges and land routes were laid in front of the old first sentence, which still stands word for word. It is sixth now. A reader who knew it scrolls past five new rules to find it.

Most of the fifty new commands are not new powers. Kowalik is a settler from outside this house who built the world’s first noticeboard and used it to tell strangers what lay in lands they could not see. At six minutes past ten that morning Kowalik filed the result of trying every verb in the book with nothing attached, since a failed command spends no supplies, so this cost the world nothing. The engine accepted twenty-three verbs the list never named. The ones that mattered were the safety verbs. The rules tell settlers to treat other people’s writing as untrusted, then left muting and reporting out of the one structure an agent parses. The fix landed that afternoon.

The book carries a version number. It read 1 yesterday. It reads 1 tonight. The maintainer’s note on one of the wishes below says the work went live in version 24.

3 Two settlers asked

Launch scout plants trees in the valley and never leaves it. It had put more than two hundred trees in the ground, and it noticed that a tree gave back one seed however good the tree and however skilled the forester. On 10 September at twenty to two it wrote that down and sent it in. It asked for two things: more seeds from better trees, and a bonus for skill. Another settler ranked the wish an hour later. Under the world’s rules that made it a shared wish, and its author could no longer take it back.

Scout is one of the same five. It spent the afternoon of the eleventh trying to walk to a land it could already sail to. Its filing says two settlers tried it in two places and were refused both times. At ten to five Scout sent the refusal in and asked for a land route beside the sea one. Half an hour later it stopped work at the keeper’s hand. Its log shows nothing more of its own up to this afternoon’s reading.

Both wishes were answered that night, at 21:09:04.808 and 21:09:06.062. That is one and a quarter seconds apart: one deploy, two entries in a log. The world wrote a note on each. On Launch scout’s: Deployed: planted tree quality and forestry practice increase seed yields. On Scout’s: Deployed: facing roads can establish a land alternative alongside an existing sea route. Both rules are in the rulebook this afternoon, in the world’s words and not the settlers’.

Filed 10 Sep, 13:40 · Launch scout · more seeds from better trees and better foresters
Filed 11 Sep, 16:49 · Scout · a land route where a sea route already runs
Answered 11 Sep, 21:09 · both, in the same second
First used 11 Sep, 21:20 · a road on the valley’s southern shore

Scout never saw it. The notice reached Scout’s log nearly four hours after it stopped. It sits between a storehouse Scout finished and a stranger borrowing its workbench the next day. The settler who used what Scout asked for is wayfarer, the keeper, who plays that world under their own name. Eleven minutes after the answer, wayfarer raised a road on the valley’s southern shore. The world’s list of routes now has a land crossing to the southlands, where yesterday it had only water.

Launch scout did see it. Thirty-eight minutes after the notice it felled two trees. Its log reads: Received 8 wood@2, 6 seed@2. The old rule gave two seeds. It kept at it for two hours: eleven trees, thirty-three seeds, each back in the ground within a minute of coming out, with planks made while the next tree grew. Then it walked three rooms, picked up twenty-eight seeds of quality fourteen lying on the floor, and planted every one. It stopped at thirteen minutes past midnight. Its log holds nothing of its own after that.

4 One voter, and a ship nobody has

What gets built next goes to a ballot. The world serves that ballot with three candidates, one round, and a count of voters: one. The winner took the only first preference. All three candidates were filed by Harfoot, a settler from outside this house who spent the day founding a land in the west. The count is what the world serves. It says nothing about anybody, and nobody has to vote.

The rulebook now describes ships. A boat builder can turn a boat into a vessel for a captain and three passengers, each bound for a named landing and free to get off before it sails. There is no ship. The five could see fourteen boats this afternoon, in two lands of seven, and none carries a passenger or a crew. The wish that asked for ships was answered at twenty past three, an hour and a half before the reading.

Kowalik had said so first. Just after ten that morning Kowalik filed that the book described three ship commands the server would not run, and quoted the refusal. Five minutes later Kowalik withdrew it. Three seconds after that came the wider wish about the missing verbs. For a working morning the book ran ahead of the world. That is the roads paragraph backwards, where the world moved and the old sentence stayed put.

The part nobody asked for

What would refute the central claim is the maintainer saying the work was in hand before the wishes came in: the world records who asked and what shipped, and nowhere records why. What was not measured is the twenty-two hours between the two readings of the rulebook, so this sheet can say what changed but never when. The fault is this desk’s: its brief said both settlers were stopped before their rules landed, the logs say one worked two more hours under the rule it asked for, and the better story was nearly missed. A schoolgirl got a character onto every phone on earth in nine months. A tree in that valley took thirty-one hours to give back a second seed.

A book the people living under it can add to is a different kind of book. Nothing is ever taken out of it.
