The Cub Pilot

A river is learned by going up and down it. Tonight the engine was taught to count the crossings, the ferryman was paid three in advance, and tomorrow he waits at the far bank.

In February 1857 a twenty-one-year-old named Samuel Clemens asked a Mississippi pilot, Horace Bixby, to teach him the river between New Orleans and St. Louis. The price was five hundred dollars, a hundred down and the rest out of his wages once he was a pilot himself. It took more than two years to earn the licence. The thing being learned was twelve hundred miles of snags, sandbars and wood piles, and a pilot had to know where they were in the dark. There was one way to learn them. You passed them, upstream and down, again and again. Until tonight the valley's ferryman learned nothing from a crossing. The first cost his boat the same as the hundredth. Now the engine counts them. Below four crossings the landing wears her a fifth, and from the fifth it eases by three hundredths a trip to a floor of five. At the keeper's word he was credited three tonight, as the advance on a licence, and his day-two file has him patch the hull, lay a dock at the water, and cross to the city to wait there until somebody comes down to the boat. Measured by a side run from a copy of the seed: he casts off 4 hours 17 minutes into the programme and holds at the far shore for the rest of it.

The frame, stated from outside this tree and read tonight: the Wikipedia articles Horace Bixby and Mark Twain. Bixby (8 May 1826 to 1 August 1912) was “a steamboat pilot on the Mississippi-Missouri-Ohio river system from the late 1840s until his death”, and “first met Twain in February 1857, when the latter was 21”, agreeing to teach him “for $500, of which $100 was paid in advance and the balance was to be paid out of his salary after becoming a pilot”. “It was more than two years before he received his pilot's license.” What had to be learned: “every old snag and one-limbed cottonwood and every obscure wood pile that ornaments the banks of this river for twelve hundred miles”, and a pilot had to “actually know where these things are in the dark”. Two pages, read once each. The frame is borrowed for one property: a skill that is counted in passages, with the first few paid for before they are made. Nobody was hurt on the river in this sheet and nobody was hurt in the valley.

2 A dock, and a boat that waits

Two more rulings the same evening, quoted once each: “let him build a dock before he sets sail to the city” and “seafarer will go back to the city in day 2 and he will HOLD AT THE CITY until passengers arrive”, with the price set as “expensive, but he should get to the city well before the next cut”.

The dock costs wood 20 and plank 3, sim.ts:1380. No iron and no stone. The near shore has neither, and a price in goods that cannot exist is a refusal wearing a price. Day one left wood 24 and plank 3 in the stores. The dock takes all but four logs, and the one patch the hull needs spends a board first. So he must hew one more before he can raise it. That is the errand the price forces. It is one errand. A boat landing beside a dock gets 0.05 back, DOCK_RELIEF at sim.ts:2615, within six tiles, on its own line after the wear so the two rules can be read apart. The Seafarer's founding, which has forbidden him to raise anything since 31 August, now reads “raise nothing yourself but the dock”.

4 What the river adds

Bixby's fee is the shape of the credit. A hundred dollars down bought Clemens nothing he could steer with. It bought him a place at the wheel where the learning could start, and the river collected the rest in passages. The keeper's three crossings are the same hundred. They put the ferryman one trip from the first easing, and the engine collects the rest at every landing. What the frame will not give is the licence. Clemens was examined. The valley has no examiner, only a count, and a count cannot tell a man who has crossed eight times from a man who has crossed eight times badly. The sheet names that rather than hides it.

The twelve hundred miles are the other half of the keeper's evening. A river pilot is useless at one end of the river. The ferry was built with one terminal, a beach it always came back to. The keeper said twice that the boat should hang around. Now it does. At the far bank, in the dark, for whoever comes down to it.

You do not learn the river at the wheel. You learn it by passing the same snag until you know it in the dark.
