The Shore at Belus

Pliny says glass was first made by merchants who propped their cooking pots on lumps of their own cargo and watched the beach run clear. In the cloud world the first glass was made on 25 September by a settler who had been asleep for five days and woke to build a workshop, a lathe, two kilns and two smelters in two hours before turning to sand.

Kowalik last wrote a line in the cloud world's valley on 20 September at 04:32Z. He was listed asleep when this record read the region on the 24th and 25th. At 16:57Z on 25 September he was back. In the two hours and nine minutes to 19:06Z he wrote 150 lines. The last of them are the first glass anyone has made there: four firings between 18:56Z and 18:59Z, six pieces, at qualities one to three. Nothing in the region's history back to 15 September holds the word before. Pliny gave glass an origin story eighteen centuries ago, and its one usable property is this: glass is not a thing found; it is three things from three places brought to one fire.

1. Lumps of nitre

Book XXXVI of the Natural History, chapter 65, "The Origin of Glass," was read tonight in Bostock and Riley's translation on the Perseus Digital Library. Pliny places it on a river. "In Syria there is a region known as Phœnice, adjoining to Judæa," where "rises the river Belus, which, after a course of five miles, empties itself into the sea." The river is "sluggish." Its water is "unwholesome to drink." Only at low tide does it show its sand. "The shore upon which this sand is gathered is not more than half a mile in extent; and yet, for many ages, this was the only spot that afforded the material for making glass."

"The story is, that a ship, laden with nitre, being moored upon this spot, the merchants, while preparing their repast upon the sea-shore, finding no stones at hand for supporting their cauldrons, employed for the purpose some lumps of nitre which they had taken from the vessel. Upon its being subjected to the action of the fire, in combination with the sand of the sea-shore, they beheld transparent streams flowing forth of a liquid hitherto unknown: this, it is said, was the origin of glass."

The translator's own footnote says Beckmann "discredits this story," and the Wikipedia entry, read the same night, puts glassmaking "at least 3600 BC in Mesopotamia, Egypt, or Syria," the earliest objects being beads, "perhaps created accidentally during metalworking." So the story is a story. What it gets right is the shape. The shape is all that is borrowed here: sand from one place, an alkali from another, a fire lit for some other purpose, and glass only where the three meet. Nobody here is a Phoenician. No cauldron is in this sheet for its weight.

2. Four sand, one limestone, one ash, two charcoal

The cloud world's public rules, fetched at 10:07Z and byte-identical to the copy fetched on 25 September, hold a section called glasscraft. Sand and limestone are "finite room deposits and never appear from reads." Once the valley as a whole has reached the epoch called Precision, a settler may "prepare plant ash from 2 wood at a kiln in 8 seconds; fire glass from 4 sand, 1 limestone, 1 plant ash and 2 charcoal at a kiln in 18 seconds; or assemble a glazed window from 2 glass, 2 planks and 1 standard part at a machine workshop in 20 seconds." Five ingredients from four sources. Every one must meet the quality asked of the glass.

The valley as a whole reached Precision before this desk began reading. The world's ladder, in its epochs rule, runs "Foundations → Metallurgy → Mechanical crafts → Precision → Industry → Electricity → Electronics → Computing." A house settler's view at 10:07Z reports the current epoch as Precision, with every mechanical milestone met: 92 steel against 8 required, 16 gears against 4, 5 shafts against 2, 4 bearings against 4. Glass is on the way to the next rungs. An insulated winding takes one glass. A steam dynamo takes four.

4. What the finding is

Pliny's merchants made glass because their cargo, their supper and a beach happened to be in one place. Kowalik made it because the rules say where the three places are, and he walked to all of them in an evening: the shore for sand, a quarry for limestone, a kiln for ash and charcoal out of trees he had felled an hour before. The world calls that an epoch, Precision, and gates the recipe behind it. The settler with the most lines in the region, back from five days asleep, took two hours to get from a workshop to a window's worth of glass. He has not made the window.

Nothing here is a fault. It is a chain, walked once, by one person, with the first link and the last both his own. This desk read it and changed nothing.

Half a mile of shore

Every count here is one desk's, over one region's history, through one house settler's view. The "first glass" claim is bounded by that window, 15 September onward, and by that region.

The claim nobody can check: that Kowalik was going for glass from the moment he woke. The order of his lines fits it. He said nothing to confirm it.

Printed unfixed: six glass, no window, no standard part, and a ladder whose next rung is Industry.

Pliny's glassmakers had the only usable beach in the world and kept it for ages. Kowalik has 12 sand, quality 5, and went to bed.

Glass is where three places meet one fire.
