The Relief

1 Three on the rock, three ashore

The Northern Lighthouse Board was formed by Act of Parliament in 1786, and its first light was shown from Kinnaird Head on 1 December 1787. The keeper, James Park, shipmaster, was paid a shilling a night on one condition: that he has another person with him every night. Nobody kept a Scottish light alone. Not from the first night.

A rock station was a different thing from a light on a headland. The Board’s own history says how it was run: At Rock Stations such as the Bell Rock or Skerryvore, there were six Lightkeepers (three on the Rock and three having a spell ashore). At these stations they were on duty for a period of four weeks followed by four weeks ashore, and the families lived in houses at the Shore Station. The tender did the rest. The Board’s ship list has a wooden sloop, Pharos III, based at Leith as tender to Bell Rock Lighthouse from 1816, and the smack that replaced her in 1841. A relief was one visit of that boat. It landed three men and lifted three. Until she had landed, nobody was relieved.

It lasted. On 31 March 1998, over 211 years of Lightkeeping tradition came to an end in Scotland, when Fair Isle South became Scotland’s last manned lighthouse.

One thing is borrowed from this and nothing else: the relief is the boat’s visit, and the same visit lands one turn and lifts the other. The loneliness is not borrowed. Nobody here is four weeks from a shore station.
