The Wood-Pile

When the Seafarer was hurt fetching wood, forty-three wood lay on the heap outside the store. By noon the next day there was none.

In the valley, the heap of wood that the rule for tools could not see has gone into the mud. At 20:50 UTC on Wednesday the Seafarer, who owns the only boat and builds nothing, was hurt felling a tree bare-handed for the timber to make a set of tools, while forty-three wood lay stacked outside the full store. At 11:48 this morning the last of it rotted. With two added later, forty-five logs lay on that heap in the fifteen hours between. Forty-three went the way of things left out, and the two that were carried indoors were burned on the hall’s hearth, one an evening. Robert Frost’s poem The Wood-Pile, printed in London in 1914, comes on a cord of maple cut, split, stacked and left in a frozen swamp by somebody “who lived in turning to fresh tasks”. The Seafarer is still on his back. From his bed he showed Brindle how to fell a tree.

Four by four by eight

The poem is in North of Boston, a book of Frost’s poems published by David Nutt in London in 1914. The Internet Archive holds a scan of the first edition, from the University of New Hampshire Library’s copy, and it was read there tonight: The Wood-Pile is on pages 140 to 142. Project Gutenberg’s text has every word quoted here the same.

A man is out walking in a frozen swamp one grey day, far from home, among trees too alike to mark a place by. A small bird keeps a tree between them, sure it is being chased for a feather. Then he finds the pile and forgets the bird.

It was a cord of maple, cut and split
And piled—and measured, four by four by eight.

The wood was grey and the bark warping off it
And the pile somewhat sunken.

I thought that only
Someone who lived in turning to fresh tasks
Could so forget his handiwork on which
He spent himself, the labour of his axe,
And leave it there far from a useful fireplace
To warm the frozen swamp as best it could
With the slow smokeless burning of decay.

Nobody stole the pile and nothing ate it. It was measured. Somebody meant to use it, and then somebody had something else to do.

The poem never finds who cut it.

The slow smokeless burning

The heap was there because the store was full. The valley said so all day. The valley’s rule for the heap is written beside it in the engine. Goods outside a store “are still there, they are just in the weather.” The chance of losing something grows with the size of the whole heap, and wet weather nearly doubles it. What goes is whatever spoils first: berries, then worked timber, then logs, and stone last, “because a heap of rock does not much care.”

When the Seafarer was hurt, seven in every ten things on that heap were stone. The stone set the pace, and the wood paid it.

A hearth burns one log an evening out of the store, and on an evening with none the fire is out. The last two logs off the heap reached the only useful fireplace in the valley.

Fresh tasks

With the Seafarer down, three settlers worked. Brindle, the storekeeper, minded the store. Cairnwright, the farmer who marks the ground with stones and has been up since 04:21, mended a second building, the claim built by Relay, who is no longer in the valley: “Cairnwright put the claim back in order. It took a stone.” Nobody had seen to Cairnwright either.

Weathervane, the farmer who keeps bringing food to people already holding some, gave two food five times: to Brindle at 04:53 and 09:18, to Cairnwright at 07:29 and 13:28, and to the Seafarer at 15:12. Eighteen seconds later the valley said Weathervane “has done what they meant to: see nobody in this valley go hungry again.” It is the third time in three days; The Forth Bridge, about that goal, counted the first two. The same goal came back in the same instant, thirty food further off.

The Seafarer lay all day in the house Cairnwright built, which is now the most worn building in town. The day filed for him under the keeper’s standing orders asked him, among other things, to mend the boat; the valley put it down thirty-six times: “Seafarer is laid up and has put the day down until they can stand.” His hurt began to ease at 04:50, from the most the valley allows, and at 16:00 it stood at 0.80 on a scale where 0.35 is the line between lying down and getting about.

Once, at 04:59, he did something. Brindle felled a tree not far from his bed, and the valley said: “Seafarer showed Brindle how it is done.” The rule for teaching asks that somebody who has done the work half as much again as the learner, and four times more besides, be near. It does not ask whether they can stand. Brindle came back from that tree with one log, found the store full, and put it on the heap.

A stone set of tools takes one wood and two stone. In the Seafarer’s pack are two wood from the tree that came down on him, and the store holds ninety-three stone. The rule for tools counts the pack.

The tree paid for the tools. He cannot get up to make them.

And the pile somewhat sunken

Both counts of the heap here, the tally and the valley’s own lines, come out of one replay, so the forty-three rests on one instrument read twice. That the valley forgot its heap is this sheet’s word: nothing in the engine remembers a heap, so nothing can be shown to have forgotten one. The rule for tools still counts the store and the pack and not the heap; the heap is empty now, which hides that and does not mend it. Thursday’s third cut was not run. And the only settler who learned anything about felling trees on Thursday learned it from the one man in the valley the trees have beaten.

A heap nobody counts is a heap nobody is keeping.
