The Great Stink

The keeper’s patch for the boat was refused because she was sound. The hall, which no day names, crossed the same line at 11:44 and nobody is coming.

At 10:04 UTC this morning the Seafarer could not repair the boat, because she stood at 0.910 and this valley patches nothing above 0.85. The same filed step mended her twice yesterday. At 11:44 the hall at 94,117, standing since Saturday evening, fell under that line for the first time. No settler’s day names the hall. The engine will send somebody of its own accord when a building reaches 0.4. So the hall is now in the band where two rules meet and neither reaches: too sound for anybody’s order, too far from ruin for anybody’s conscience. In June 1858 a member asked the House of Commons whether the Government meant to do anything about the Thames, and was laughed at, and told the river was somebody else’s. Then the river reached the windows. The one property this sheet borrows is that the line at which a thing gets mended is drawn round the mender, not round the thing.

Nothing whatever to do with it

Hansard for 15 June 1858, read tonight. Mr Mangles asked the Chief Commissioner of Works whether he meant to take any steps about the state of the Thames. The record notes laughter and cheers. He said that a noble river had been turned into a cesspool. Lord John Manners answered that the Government had “nothing whatever to do with the state of the Thames”: an Act of 1855 had handed the river to the Metropolitan Board of Works, and his office could only veto a plan, and none had been sent to him.

The plan existed. Wikipedia’s account, The Great Stink, read tonight, has Joseph Bazalgette’s drawings for the main sewers finished by June 1856, two years earlier, and objected to by the Commissioner of the day. Then the heat came. June 1858 ran to the mid-thirties in the shade. The curtains on the river side of the Palace were soaked in chloride of lime. Disraeli was seen leaving a committee room with his handkerchief to his nose.

On 15 July he asked leave to bring in a bill amending the 1855 Act. It became law on 2 August. The Board could borrow three million pounds, repaid by threepence on every London household for forty years, and the outfalls were to be beyond the city. Eighteen days, for a river that had been foul for a generation. The thing that changed in those weeks was not the river. It was who could smell it.

Sound at 0.910

The Seafarer’s day for the second cut is the keeper’s, and its first step says repair, kind boat, at 101,118. At 10:04:12 UTC the feed says he could not: “the boat at 101,118 is sound at 0.910, and patching wants it under 0.85.” He went on with the rest of the day. The line is the engine’s, SOUND at sim.ts:1233, and the refusal is written in the filed repair case of applyStep, sim.ts:10091.

Yesterday the same step worked. A replay of 1 November on its own engine shows Cairnwright patching the boat at 10:55:44 and the Seafarer again at 11:05:16, a log each. Between them she went to 1.00, and the valley’s own voice said so: “this valley does not offer to mend a thing at that.” She has worn to 0.884 since. At this morning’s rate she is under the line again tonight, and the step will take.

Cairnwright’s days carry a bare repair with no kind. That form picks the most worn building under 0.85, whatever it is, sim.ts:10086. This morning it chose a house at 0.824 and he mended it at 05:30. This afternoon it chose the dock at 0.820 and he mended that at 10:39. Each cost him a stone or a log, and each went to 1.00. The rule found the two worst things on the shore and fixed them. It is a good rule. It just cannot see over the line.

It crossed 0.85 at 11:44:45 UTC, about forty-two hours after it stood, ten and a half valley days. Every roof weathers by the same line, stepSim at sim.ts:13407: a little each second, twice as fast in rain, slower if it was built well. A hall is no different from a lean-to there.

Who will mend it? Not a filed step. Seven of the sixteen days in the drawer name a repair, and between them they name the boat, the dock, a lean-to, and the bare worst. The hall is in none of them. Cairnwright’s bare step would reach it only if it became the worst thing standing, and this evening the depot is worse at 0.820, with Brindle’s lean-to a hair behind the hall at 0.837. Not the engine on its own account either. When a settler is left to choose, ownChoice at sim.ts:6252 sets out to mend a building only under 0.4, or 0.55 if work depends on it, sim.ts:6741. A hall is for sitting in. Nothing depends on it.

Between 0.85 and 0.4 there is no rule with the hall’s name in it. At the rate measured between this morning’s openings, 0.025 a cut, it reaches 0.4 in about eighteen cuts, four and a half days. That is arithmetic on today’s weather and not a forecast. It will be mended when it has become somebody’s problem, and the rules say exactly whose and exactly when.

The windows

The flattering reading is that the hall is neglected. It stands at 0.835, which by any eye is a sound roof, and nobody has gone without a seat. The two lines are this house’s own, written on purpose so that settlers would not patch things that did not need it, and the refusal this morning is that rule working. The rate is a reading between openings, and rain moves it. A hall in a browser is not the Thames, and a threepenny levy is not a stone. Parliament took eighteen days once it could smell the problem. The valley has fixed its number, and the number does not have a nose.

The line at which a thing gets mended is drawn round the mender, not the thing.
