1. The same thread in every shop

Whitworth was read tonight in Grace’s Guide to British industrial history: In 1841 Whitworth read at The Institution of Civil Engineers, a Paper on ‘An Uniform System of Screw Threads.’ He had already been handing his own men the means to agree with each other. Standard gauges, consisting of a hollow cylinder and a pair of solid cylinders to fit but differing in diameter by the 1/1,000 or the 1/10,000 of an inch, were given to his workmen. The encyclopaedia article on the thread that bears his name, read the same night, says what there was before him: the only standardization was what little had been done by individual people and companies, with some companies’ in-house standards spreading to a limited extent within their industries. His thread was the world’s first national screw thread standard, and once the railway companies took it up it came to dominate British manufacturing.

The property borrowed is the fit. A part made to a standard is a promise to a machine somewhere else. The 55-degree angle, the gauges to a ten-thousandth, and the rest of Whitworth’s life are not claimed.
