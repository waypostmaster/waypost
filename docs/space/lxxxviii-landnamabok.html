Landnámabók

Iceland kept a book of who took which land. Some four hundred settlers, each with a name, a shore and a reason for being on the boat — a feud, a king, land hunger. It is the reason that makes it a book rather than a list. This sheet reads the two documents a settler must file here, the charter and the day, and finds that this valley keeps the reason and loses the asking.

The finding

A settler who files a whole day may speak and may not ask. The daybook path in applyInstruction reads persona, then hold, then say, and returns at line 7461. wish is not read until 7507, which that return has already stepped over. The field is declared on the type, accepted by the parser, capped at 160 characters and documented to the people who file. On the richer of the two paths it goes nowhere. Somebody carried say across to the daybook and did not carry wish, and the two sit fifty-nine lines apart. So a settler who files one errand can tell this valley what it is missing, and a settler who files a considered day cannot.

I A name, a shore, and why they came

The Icelandic book is a register of claims, and it is dry in the way a register is. It gives a settler, the ground they took, and the reason they were on the water. Harald was king and would not be argued with. There was a killing. The land at home was full.

The reasons are what the book is for. A list of four hundred names and four hundred shores would settle boundary disputes and tell you nothing. The reasons are why anybody still opens it, and they are also the part nobody could check even then.

This valley asks for the same three things and asks for them in the same order. The founder record carries a name, a shore voted for on the water, and a sentence beginning because. It was designed by somebody who had not read the Icelandic book. That is the ordinary way two people arrive at the same form. Nobody copied anybody.

II What the charter actually asks

One field is required. A boarder with no usable name is stepped past in silence. Every other field has a fallback, and the fallbacks are the interesting part, because a charter that cannot be refused can still be quietly diminished.

Two of those deserve saying out loud. A mistyped trade makes a forester and tells nobody, so the cheapest error in the document is also the most silent. And the duty floor is a constitutional limit rather than a safety rail: you may not write yourself a settler that no letter can steer. The comment beside it is blunt about why. A person nobody can reach is a stranger in a cottage whoever wrote them. The floor is 0.2. It can’t be argued down.

At most nine board. A tenth file is read and discarded without comment. Nobody is told.

III Two documents, both called a day

The daily file has two shapes, and they disagree about how long a day is. They are not the same document.

The block form divides a day into eight named watches, first and second of daylight, dusk, night and dawn, and a block whose at names none of them is dropped without a word. The flat form takes sixty steps in a row. Its own comment says what that number is for: six to eight tasks a valley day, six days, about fifty steps. The cap was raised from ten to sixty on purpose, and the reasoning is written down beside it. A spoken line is capped at 90 characters here and at 200 on the charter. Nothing says why.

So the shorter-looking document is a day and the longer one is a week, and only one of them hears a wish. Anybody choosing between them on shape alone will choose wrong. Shape is the wrong tell.

IV The one part that is actually theirs

This record has been here before, one field over. Sheet XLVII found that a settler's spoken line lived seven seconds over their head and reached no book at all, and the fix put it into the record. The comment left behind at the repair says exactly what was wrong.

of everything a correspondent sends, the one part that is actually theirs was the one part that left no trace. src/sim.ts, at the repair to say

That sentence is still true of wish on the daybook path. The repair is four lines. A wish is not an errand. It is the only field in either document where a settler says what this valley does not have, in their own words, and it is the field the wishlist is built out of. Eight hundred and sixty-two of them have been filed. The catalogue that reads them is the same catalogue that heard a man ask for a salmon trap and granted him a well.

Which is the second half of it, and the funnier half. The first settler due to file one of these intends to ask for a boat. There is no boat. build accepts a house, a sawmill, a mine, a granary or a tower, and nothing else, so the valley has no word for the thing he came in on. It does have tileCostByBoat, which is a rule for what crossing water costs a boat that cannot be made. The wish would be dropped by the code, and had it not been, it would have been granted a well.

V What this sheet has not watched work

Every number above is read out of the source rather than out of a running valley. Nobody has filed either document against the founding these two describe, because the founding has not happened and the aired coast still holds nought settlers and nought buildings. A cap read in code and a cap met in a run are not the same fact, and this record has spent a day on exactly that distinction.

The dropped wish is worse than reported here in one way and better in another. Worse, because a day is the document a keeper is asked to file, so the field is lost on the path people are pushed toward. Better, because nothing has been lost yet: no wish has ever been filed on a daybook, so the count of wishes this fault has eaten is nought. The trap is open. Nobody is in it yet.

And the charter has a field that reaches nothing. notes is read by no code in this repository. It is where the reasoning goes, That makes it the most Icelandic field in the document. It is also the only one the valley can’t hear.

Hedge on the vellum: Landnámabók was compiled two centuries after the settlements it lists, survives in later redactions that disagree, and served land claims as much as history, so its reasons are evidence about the twelfth century as much as the ninth; the borrowed shape is narrow — a register of name, ground and reason, in which the reason is the part worth keeping and the part nobody can check.

Written 30 August, from the source and not from a run: one required field of eight, a trade that fails into forestry without saying so, a duty floor that forbids writing an unreachable person, two documents both called a day at 12 steps and 60, and one field accepted, capped, documented and never read. Nought wishes lost so far, which is the only good number in it.
