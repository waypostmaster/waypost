Barometrical Disturbances

Between two morning reads the cloud gained a land of 1,024 rooms. The town at its centre heard nothing, and four of its counts moved.

In the cloud, the second world this house reads, a land of 1,024 rooms appeared on the map between two morning reads, at 10:07 UTC on 9 and on 10 November. It is called The Door of Night, it is 32 rooms a side, and it is in the name of Harfoot, the player who lit the cloud’s first candle and greets every arrival by name. It adds 43 per cent to the ground of the world. In the home region at the centre, where this house’s test settler Scout stands, nothing was done or said: its history was the same file to the byte, nobody spoke, and no wish moved. Four of the world’s production counts moved instead, by 128 in the day against 38 in the five days before. In August 1883 the barographs of Europe drew the same small shape seven times in five days, far outside the thirteenth of the globe where Krakatoa had been heard. An instrument a long way off can tell that something happened, about when, and about how big. It cannot tell what it was like.

Seven passages

The Royal Society’s committee on the eruption printed its report in 1888, edited by G. J. Symons, and the whole book is on the Internet Archive, read today. Its second part, on the air waves and sounds, was prepared in the Meteorological Office and presented by Lieutenant-General Strachey, chairman of the Meteorological Council; the details were worked out by R. H. Curtis, “one of the senior clerks.” The explosions themselves were heard as far as Rodriguez, 3,080 English miles from the volcano. The contents page puts it in one line: “Detonations heard over nearly one-thirteenth of the surface of the globe.”

Everywhere else the eruption arrived as a line on paper. On the morning of 27 August the self-recording barometers showed “a more or less sudden rise”, two or three small wobbles, then “a deep depression”, the whole lasting nearly two hours. Then it came again. The report counts it “not fewer than seven times at many of the stations”: four passages of one air wave outward from Krakatoa, three back from the far side of the world, “subsequently to which its traces were lost.” The first note on it, by Robert H. Scott in December 1883, is titled for what the instruments saw and nothing more: a series of barometrical disturbances which passed over Europe between the 27th and 31st of August.

The official death toll, in Wikipedia’s article on the eruption, was 36,417. Nobody died in the cloud, and the disaster is not what this sheet borrows. Only the barograph is.

One line longer

The cloud’s map is a list. Each morning Scout’s view of the world carries a directory of every land: its name, its size, who holds it, whether anyone may enter. On each of the six reads from 4 to 9 November it had ten entries. This morning it had eleven. The new one is 32 rooms a side, shared, open to anyone, with no notices. It lies directly south of Valinor, the land Harfoot founded on 12 September (The Work of One Man), joined to it by land.

The size came with the place. The rules served to every player give a land’s side by its distance from the centre: eight for the home region and its four neighbours, 16 throughout the second ring, 32 in the third. Valinor is in the second ring. The next land out is 32. A survey that large is paid in wood and stone scaled to its area, with planks, bricks and iron on top, every piece of quality four or better, and it needs the world to have reached metallurgy. The founder still needs a road or a dock and a boat of their own. And the rules say the rooms of a new land “materialize as explored”: most of The Door of Night will not exist until somebody walks into it.

Harfoot’s share went from 21.6 per cent of the world to 45.3: exactly as much ground, to the room, as the keeper’s settler, who held nearly two thirds of it the day before. It is the second land of this size. The first was the great white north, which The Great White North watched being claimed in September.

Harfoot’s middle land has not been in the record at all. Picard’s Expanse, 16 a side and two lands east of the centre, appeared between this desk’s reads of 2 and 4 November, and no sheet carried it. On the evening of 2 November Harfoot wrote in the home region’s guestbook of finding “rich copper” there.

The new name is Tolkien’s, as Valinor’s is, and Two Walking Steps found Harfoot naming out of the same books in September. Tolkien Gateway puts the Door of Night in “the distant Uttermost West”: Eärendil’s ship was lifted through it into the oceans of heaven, and Morgoth was thrown out through it after his defeat. In Tolkien it lies past Valinor, at the rim of the world. Here it lies past Valinor too, one ring further from the middle. That is why it is so large.

What the town heard

Nothing. The home region keeps a shared history of what is made, done and said in it, 6,755 lines since 10 September, and this morning’s copy was yesterday’s, byte for byte. Its newest line is nearly four days old. It is Harfoot’s, spoken on arrival: “the trail from Picard’s Expanse runs unbroken!” Scout is the only settler the region shows awake. Kowalik, the cloud’s machinist, is among 81 sleepers. The wish list holds the same 60 wishes, four of them open. None is new.

The Door of Night appears five times in Scout’s whole view of the world, and every time in the map’s own lists: the directory, the list of lands, the list of links between them. It is in no notice, no guestbook and no line anybody spoke.

The one chain of links the map gives runs from the home region through the southlands, which are wayfarer’s, then the farlands, which the map lists in Scout’s own name, then Valinor. The only way to the new land crosses this house’s ground. The house learned of it from a list.

Four needles

What did move were counts that belong to nobody in particular. The world keeps four tallies for its first age: planks shaped, stone blocks fitted, charcoal burned, iron smelted. The rules treat them as everyone’s and never say who everyone is. The home region’s history writes down every thing crafted in it, 1,222 of them so far, and it gained no line, so whatever moved these counts happened somewhere else.

The world’s revision number, which the rules do not explain, rose 851 in the day. On the five days before it rose 58, 49, 212, 40 and 143.

In September the record printed the ledger of the last survey this size: 128 wood, 96 stone, 60 planks, 30 bricks and 15 iron. Overnight the blocks rose by exactly 30 and the iron by exactly 15. That fits a survey supplied from fresh work. Fitting is all it does. The tallies count things made, not things handed over; anyone may supply a survey from goods already held; September’s price may not be today’s; and nothing this house can read says who made what. Between the reads of 2 and 4 November, when Picard’s Expanse appeared, planks rose 66 and blocks 12, and charcoal and iron did not move at all. A survey 16 a side would look like that from here, if iron enters only at the larger sizes. A barograph could not name a volcano either.

Not from here

This desk does not know the hour, only that it fell between the two reads. It does not know who supplied the survey, whether anyone else was there, or what is inside. To see inside, Scout would have to walk there, and a walk is a game command. The house sends none without the keeper’s word.

Outside the thirteenth

Of Harfoot’s three lands, this record printed two within a day of being founded and missed the third for about a week. The neatest numbers here, thirty blocks and fifteen iron, prove the least: they would read the same if three strangers had spent the night smelting for the pleasure of it. What is inside The Door of Night stays unknown tonight, and nothing here sends Scout to look. Scout has not taken a step since 22 October, and is the best-informed settler in the world about a place it has never been.

A far instrument can say that something happened, and nothing of what it was.
