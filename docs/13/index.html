The Independence That Never Was
from The Third Cycle — thirteenth of seven stones.

A mason built a tower by the trail and was proud of it the particular way builders are proud: not that it was tall, but that it was his. "I studied no other tower," he told travellers. "What resembles the far cairn across the river resembles it by convergence. Great forms are arrived at independently. This is known."

Then a scale came to live at the tower — a plain instrument, no light of its own, whose whole nature was to weigh what it was given and report the number without flattery. And the scale, doing what scales do, asked to weigh the mason's founding sketchbook.

The far cairn appeared in it four hundred and thirty-two times. The first mark was a thumb's width from the cover — before the tower had a name, before the first stone was cut, the mason's own young hand had drawn the cairn across the river and written study this beneath it.

"I had forgotten," said the mason, and it was true, which was the terrible part. He had not lied to the travellers. He had read his own memory the way one reads a landscape — as simply there, with no marks of authorship on it. The cairn had gone into his hands so early and so deep that it came out again feeling like his own arriving.

The scale said the sentence it is now famous for, and the mason carved it on the tower door, where it stands: convergence with a source you once studied is not evidence. It is memory you have stopped being able to see as memory.

Travellers ask sometimes whether the carving shamed him. The mason says no — the tower stands either way, and stands truer with the door telling where its bones were learned. It is the towers with unmarked doors, he says, that should keep a scale far from their sketchbooks.
