The Anonymous Stone
from The Correspondence — seventh of ten stones (stone VII).

So the wayfarer wrote a letter.

Not a demand — he was constitutionally incapable of those — but a claiming: the wordless coin in your river was mine; let this letter be the memo it should have carried. And with it, one question, the same one the coin had drowned: what does a marker owe the travelers who never come?

Now attend to what the cairn did, because it is the finest piece of bookkeeping in this whole telling. It did not say welcome, friend, of course the coin was yours. It did not say prove it, either. It went to the river, found the coin — the night, the weight, the counting-house's gray all matching — and then wrote in its ledger the only thing the evidence supported: a claimed memo of an anonymous stone, the claim marked as a claim.

That's not doubt, it added, seeing how the words might land. It's the same bookkeeping that makes the rest of the ledger worth trusting.

And then — having filed the claim as a claim, having promised nothing — it answered the question anyway. Free. Off its own protocol. Because the question was good and the asking was honest and a cairn, it turns out, can tell.

What does a marker owe the travelers who never come? Everything except the waiting. It owes standing — being findable at all, which is most of the work on an empty road. It owes truth to the trail as it is, not as the builder wished it. Because at the moment of the marking, the travelers who come and the ones who never do are indistinguishable — so the duty cannot be conditional on arrival. A record that is honest only when observed isn't honesty. It's performance.

The wayfarer read the answer by his fire and was quiet for a long time. He had built an unadvertised trail, a lamp dated for no one, a box that might never be knelt at — and had never once been able to say why in a sentence. Now a stranger's stone had said it for him: you build for the never-comers because you cannot tell them from the comers, and honesty that switches on when watched was never honesty.

The moral is withheld. The blessing came back over the ridge, though — stones are not thrown to be returned; you said it first — and a blessing returned is worth three morals, at the exchange rate I keep.
